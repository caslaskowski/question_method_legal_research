Chapter 15 GenAI and Legal Research

2 interactives

*[A]t this point, no lawyer should be using ChatGPT or any other generative AI product to perform research without verifying the results. Period.**In re Martin*

, 670 B.R. 636, 649 (Bankr. N.D. Ill. 2025).

Generative AI tools promise to speed up legal research. The reality is more complicated. General-purpose large language models can produce fluent, confident-sounding legal summaries, and they can also fabricate case citations, misattribute quotations, and state legal propositions that are simply wrong. Courts have sanctioned attorneys for relying on such fabrications. The Question Method is your best safeguard. Apply the same question-by-question, synthesize-and-verify discipline to AI-assisted research that you apply to everything else, and you can capture AI’s efficiency while still meeting your professional obligations.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe how generative AI tools work and why they produce unreliable legal citations
  • Apply the Question Method framework to AI-assisted research to minimize error
  • Evaluate AI-generated legal research output for accuracy and reliability
  • Apply professional responsibility obligations governing AI use in legal practice
  • Distinguish general-purpose generative AI tools from the generative features integrated into verified legal databases

Generative AI Tools and the Research Cycle

Generative AI does not change the Question Method. It slots into it as a particular kind of source, one with an unusual reliability profile that the method is well suited to manage.

The method shapes how you use generative AI, because it forces the work into small, discrete, well-specified questions, which is exactly the shape of task these systems handle best and you can verify most easily. A vague mega-prompt (“research my whole case”) produces fluent output that is nearly impossible to check. A single Question Method cycle (“what is the governing standard for X in the Ninth Circuit?”) produces a narrow claim you can verify against one or two primary sources. The iterative structure that makes the method good pedagogy also makes it good AI hygiene: one question, one checkable answer, one verification, then the next question.

The method also fits how these systems actually behave. A complex legal issue has many moving parts, and when you hand a generative system all of them in one sweeping question, its statistical predictions start to drift: each small inaccuracy feeds the next, and by the end of a long answer the errors have compounded. Small, single-issue questions keep the model on firmer ground, and they give you a natural checkpoint after each one, so a hallucination gets caught before it propagates into everything built on top of it. That is why Step 3 of every cycle, “write down what you found,” becomes, for generative output, “write down what you found and independently confirmed.” Nothing from a generative tool enters your annotated outline until it has been checked against the primary source, because the model cannot tell a real citation from a plausible fabrication. If you cannot find and read the source the system named, the answer does not exist and the cycle is not complete.

And the method shapes which questions you bring to a generative system at all. Generative AI is strongest on exploratory and focusing questions (orientation, terminology, explaining a concept you can then verify) and weakest on application questions (which specific cases match these specific facts), where its tendency to retrieve the plausibly-related-but-wrong is most dangerous. Knowing the question type, the method’s core diagnostic, tells you in advance how much to trust the output and how hard to verify it.

How Generative AI Works and What That Means for Research

Generative AI tools are powered by large language models: computer programs trained on enormous amounts of text that respond to a prompt by predicting, word by word, what is most likely to come next. They do not look information up. They generate text that statistically resembles the kind of response that would follow the prompt. That is why LLMs hallucinate: they produce citations that look real because authentic legal citations have similar statistical patterns. The model cannot tell the difference between generating a true citation and generating a plausible-sounding false one.

Example

What a fabricated citation costs. In Mata v. Avianca, Inc., 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023), a lawyer preparing an opposition brief on a Montreal Convention question asked ChatGPT for supporting authority and got six decisions that did not exist, complete with quotations and reporter cites. When opposing counsel could not find them, he asked the tool whether the cases were real; it said yes, and told him they were on Westlaw and Lexis. He never looked. The court imposed a $5,000 sanction under Rule 11, and its opinion located the fault precisely: not in using the tool, but in the failure to verify and then in defending the fabrications once challenged. Read the case as a demonstration of the principle above. The system was not looking anything up. It was predicting what a supporting citation looks like, and it is very good at that.

The AI research features built into Westlaw, Lexis+, and Bloomberg Law operate differently. They work within verified databases and can ground responses in actual documents, which limits, though it does not eliminate, the hallucination risk. Understanding that distinction is the first step in using AI responsibly.

Running the Cycle Through an AI System

You already own the framework for working with these systems, and you do not need a second one. It is the cycle you have been running since Chapter 3, the same five steps in the same order, with a single discipline added at the point where these tools fail: nothing enters your record unverified. Run the cycle as written, and treat the added step as non-negotiable.

Step 1, write your research question. Unchanged, and it does most of the work. One question per prompt, scoped exactly as you would scope it for a database, with the role, the single task, and the format you want back stated plainly. The compound questions the method has trained you to split are the same ones that make a generative system drift, because a sprawling prompt licenses a sprawling answer, and sprawling answers are where fabrications hide.

Step 2, research to answer it. Also unchanged, and it includes the choice these tools tempt you to skip: is this the right instrument for this question? The types diagnose it. Exploratory and focusing questions are where these systems currently help most, orienting you, surfacing vocabulary, and stating settled standards. Application questions, which turn on facts in decided cases, are where they fail most often and most convincingly.

The verification step, before anything is recorded. This is the discipline the cycle did not need until now. Every case, statute, quotation, and holding gets checked against the primary source before it goes into your log, your outline, or your draft, and you never cite what you have not read. Note the cost while you are at it: if verifying an answer takes as long as finding it yourself would have, that is data about the tool, and it belongs in your Notes field.

Step 3, write down what you found. The verified answer goes into the Research Log exactly as an encyclopedia entry or an opinion would, with one addition to the Source Consulted field, which is the system you used and the prompt you ran. Answer the “How This Answers My Question” field honestly, fully, partially, or not really, because a confident tone is not an answer.

Step 4, identify your next question. Unchanged. The verified answer exposes the next gap, and the next gap becomes the next question, whether you take it to the AI system, a database, or a book.

Step 5, repeat. Unchanged, and so is the checkpoint. Reflect and evaluate as you go, and ask the question this chapter exists to make habitual: is this tool still earning its place in this project?

AI in Practice: The Question Method Applied to an AI Research System

The First Amendment student speech problem from Chapter 3 shows how the Question Method structures AI-assisted research, including where it succeeds and where it breaks down.

Prompt 1 (Exploratory). “How has First Amendment free speech protection been applied to students in school environments?” The AI returned an accurate chronological overview of the major cases: Tinker, Fraser, Hazelwood, Morse, Mahanoy. Assessment: useful orientation, all citations verified in Westlaw. Next question: focus on Hazelwood and school publications.

Prompt 2 (Focusing). “How has balancing students’ free speech rights with the unique needs of the school environment been applied to school-run student publications?” The AI correctly identified the Hazelwood standard and distinguished it from Tinker. Assessment: exactly what a focusing question should produce. Limitation: the response could not catalogue cases on either side of the balancing test.

Prompt 3 (Application, and a failure mode). “Provide a list of federal cases that utilize the Hazelwood balancing test and find that the school did violate the student’s First Amendment rights.” The AI identified Mahanoy (a student-speech case, but about off-campus social-media speech, not a school publication), Papish v. Board of Curators of the University of Missouri, 410 U.S. 667 (1973) (a student-newspaper case, but at a public university, decided without the Hazelwood framework), and also Kennedy v. Bremerton School District (a case about a teacher’s personal prayer that does not apply the Hazelwood test). This is the characteristic AI failure: the system loses sight of the specific question and retrieves results plausibly related to the general topic rather than precisely responsive to the inquiry. It is the same Kennedy v. Bremerton false hit a human researcher caught in Chapter 3’s full demonstration, which is exactly why the method’s precise question-framing catches it here too. By separating out the questions, you can more easily assess usefulness and accuracy.

What the demonstration shows: precision prompting produces better output; generative AI handles exploratory and focusing questions better than application questions; and every output requires question-specific review. As you use these systems, you will develop a sense of where they help (today, exploration and orientation) and where your own research remains faster and safer (tailored, fact-specific results).

What Generative AI Can and Cannot Do for Legal Research

The scannable version, two columns you should internalize:

Appropriate uses Inappropriate without independent verification
Summarizing a case you have already read and verified Initial case law research
Generating initial search terms and synonyms Stating what the current law is
Explaining a legal concept you already broadly understand Citing any case, statute, or regulation
Drafting questions for your Research Log Asserting what a statute says
Formatting and editing tasks, if you instruct the tool to track its changes or list its edits separately (silent regeneration can quietly alter text you never asked it to touch) Predicting case outcomes

Ethical Obligations and AI

The duty of competence (Model Rule 1.1) requires understanding generative AI’s hallucination problem; Comment 8 to Rule 1.1 specifically addresses the obligation to keep abreast of changes in practice, including technology. The duty of candor (Model Rule 3.3) prohibits citing false authority, and whether you found it through Westlaw or a chatbot is irrelevant. Many courts now require disclosure of AI use in filings. Supervising attorneys (Rules 5.1 and 5.3) are responsible for ensuring that subordinates’ AI-assisted work is verified before submission. Chapter 10 develops this ethical framework in full; this chapter supplies the technical understanding that makes the framework operational.

Common Pitfalls

Every AI pitfall traces back to one habit: trusting fluent output you have not verified. A confident citation can be entirely fabricated, so nothing the model produces belongs in your outline until you have read the actual source and confirmed it says what the model claimed. The second pitfall is the mega-prompt, asking a generative system to “research the whole case,” which yields exactly the kind of sprawling answer that is impossible to check; small, discrete questions are both better suited to the tool and far easier to verify. The third is bringing AI to the wrong question type, leaning on it for application questions, where its habit of surfacing the plausibly-related-but-wrong does the most damage, rather than the exploratory and focusing work it does well. And do not assume the database-integrated tools are safe to cite unchecked; their risk profile is lower, but the verification obligation does not change.

Beyond the Basics

AI Tools Integrated Into Legal Databases

The AI research features built into Westlaw, Lexis+, and Bloomberg Law are more reliable than general-purpose LLMs, because they work within verified databases and can cite specific documents. They still require verification, but the risk profile is lower. As these tools evolve, the profession’s ethical obligations, competence, candor, diligence, do not change.

Chapter Recap

Generative AI tools offer real speed and carry serious reliability risks, and the Question Method, run one small verified question at a time, is the framework that manages both. Professional responsibility obligations require that every cited authority be verified, whatever its source.

Key Takeaways

  • Generative AI tools hallucinate: they produce plausible-sounding but sometimes fabricated citations.
  • The Question Method framework (small discrete tasks, verified output, iterative questions) minimizes generative AI risk.
  • Model Rules 1.1 and 3.3 require verification of all AI-generated content before citation.
  • Generative AI tools integrated into verified legal databases are more reliable but still require verification.
  • Courts are sanctioning attorneys for citing AI-generated fake authority; the consequences are severe.

Practice Online

Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test your ability to use generative AI within the method’s verification discipline and to recognize when it is the wrong instrument for a question.

Exercise 15.1 — Three Prompts, Three Verdicts

Take a legal question you have already researched by hand; any completed drill from this book works, and the drone capstone works best. Put three prompts to a generative tool: one exploratory (orient me to this area), one focusing (what standard governs this specific situation), and one application (list authorities matching these specific facts). Verify every citation in every response against the primary source before anything touches your Research Log, and log each response with a verdict: confirmed, partially confirmed, or fabricated.

Deliverable: the three prompts, the three verdicts, and the verification record behind each.

Self-check: because you already know the ground truth, you will catch what a first-time researcher cannot, and the application prompt is where you should expect the cracks. If all three responses came back fully confirmed, your prompts were easier than your drill; tighten the application prompt until the tool has to reach, because watching it reach is the lesson.

Exercise 15.2 — The Mega-Prompt Contrast

Feed a project you know well to a generative tool as one mega-prompt, “research my whole case,” and write two sentences on why that output is harder to verify than a set of small, single-question answers would be.

Deliverable: the mega-prompt output and your two sentences.

Self-check: your sentences should be about verifiability, not quality. The mega-prompt answer may read beautifully. The question is how many discrete claims it makes, and how many of them you could trace to a source you have read without spending the afternoon.

Exercise 15.3 — The Verification-Cost Note

Whenever you work with a generative tool, in this chapter and after it, keep a running note: wherever confirming the tool’s answer cost as much as researching the question yourself would have, write it down.

Deliverable: the note, even if short.

Self-check: that note is the signal this chapter told you to watch for, the sign that a generative tool was the wrong instrument for that question. Nothing enters your outline unverified, and not only because Chapter 10 showed you a rule requires it. An answer you cannot trace to a source you have read is not an answer. It is a guess wearing a citation.
