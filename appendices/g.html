Appendix G

The Worked Examples in Full

Two of the chapters compress their worked examples into boxes so the text can keep moving. This appendix holds each full chain, question by question, in the form a Research Log would tell it. Appendix E’s bracket convention applies here too: brackets mean verify against current law before relying, and illustrative slots are marked as such.

The Foster Mother as Bystander (Chapter 5) #

The scenario. Dana has been the licensed foster parent of nine-year-old M. for the past three years. M. has lived in Dana’s home that entire time, and Dana is the only mother M. has known across those years. One afternoon, as the two walk to the corner store, a driver runs a red light and strikes and kills M. in front of Dana. Dana is not physically hurt, but the experience leaves her with severe, lasting emotional trauma. She wants to know whether she can recover from the driver. The jurisdiction is California.

Where the researcher’s knowledge actually is. Our researcher has heard that a person who witnesses a terrible accident can sometimes sue for the emotional harm, and has run across the phrase negligent infliction of emotional distress. That is the whole of it. She does not yet know the elements of the claim, she does not know there is a special rule about the witness’s relationship to the victim, and, this matters, she does not yet know that Dana’s status as a foster parent is the fact the whole case will depend on. She knows it as background, the way she knows M.’s age. Whether it is legally relevant is something the research has to tell her. So we do not start by cataloguing facts. We start by writing the first question we can actually write.

Question 1 (Exploratory, orienting). If you witness a negligent accident that injures someone else, can you sue for your own emotional distress?

Answer, scoped to the question. Yes. A secondary source on tort claims for emotional distress, an encyclopedia entry or a California practice guide, supplies the orientation: in California a person who suffers no physical injury can sometimes recover for the emotional distress of witnessing injury to another, under what the courts call a bystander theory of negligent infliction of emotional distress. (The discipline matters here: the question asked only whether such a claim is possible. The answer says yes, sometimes, and hands us the name. It does not yet list what the claim requires; that is the next question’s job, not this one’s.)

The claim exists and now has a name. A claim that succeeds only “sometimes” is a claim with requirements. The next question asks what they are.

Question 2 (Focusing). What must a bystander prove to recover for NIED in California?

Answer, scoped to the question. The secondary source’s citations point to the leading case, and you cross from secondary to primary authority to read it. Under Thing v. La Chusa, 48 Cal. 3d 644 (1989), the plaintiff must show three things: (1) that she is closely related to the injury victim; (2) that she was present at the scene of the injury-producing event when it occurred and was then aware that it was injuring the victim; and (3) that she suffered serious emotional distress beyond what a disinterested witness would feel.

Three requirements, each a potential question of its own. Two are not in real doubt on these facts: Dana watched the collision happen (requirement 2), and a foster mother who sees her child killed plainly suffers distress beyond a stranger’s (requirement 3). The genuinely contested requirement is the first, whether a foster parent is “closely related” to her foster child. And here is the moment a fact becomes relevant: Dana’s foster status, a piece of background a moment ago, is now the pivot of the case, because the first requirement is the one that asks about it. This is the method’s central claim in miniature: we did not list “Dana is a foster parent, not a biological parent” as a relevant fact at the outset. It became relevant the instant the research turned up a requirement that depends on it.

Question 3 (Focusing / Definitional). What relationship counts as “closely related to the injury victim”?

Answer, scoped to the question. Thing itself draws the line. Absent exceptional circumstances, it limits recovery to relatives residing in the same household, or to the victim’s parents, siblings, children, and grandparents, and it describes the qualifying relationships as those of blood or marriage. Two more decisions in the line, one Thing itself discusses and one found by running Thing through a citator, apply the same boundary to relationships that fall outside it. In Elden v. Sheldon, 46 Cal. 3d 267 (1988), the court held that an unmarried cohabitant, a romantic partner living as a spouse, is not “closely related.” In Moon v. Guardian Postacute Services, 95 Cal. App. 4th 1005 (2002), the court held that a son-in-law was not “closely related” to his mother-in-law even though he had lived with her and was close to her.

The decided cases sort relationships along a single line: blood or marriage. A foster parent has neither, no blood tie, no marital tie, to the foster child. But no case in this line decides a foster relationship. Note what that gap is and is not. The rule that governs Dana is articulated, Thing states it, and Thing itself leaves the door ajar with “absent exceptional circumstances.” What is missing is a decision applying the rule to this relationship. Recognizing that the courts have never addressed this relationship is itself a research finding. And the express exception is what makes the next question arguable at all. A hedged line invites you to ask what the line is for.

Question 4 (Application, fact-finding by analogy). How have California courts treated relationships outside blood or marriage that claimed to be “closely related,” and what reasons did they give for drawing the line where they did?

Answer, from the decided line. No California case reaches a foster relationship, so the research answer is the line itself and the reasons the courts gave for it. The synthesis goes like this. The decided cases protect relationships defined by blood or marriage, and the reason the court gives is administrability: a bright line that can be applied without inquiring case by case into how close any particular relationship really was, and that keeps a negligent defendant’s exposure predictable (Thing; Elden). And the categories that make the line administrable are not arbitrary: blood and marriage are statuses that already come bundled with legal rights each party holds in the other, which is what lets a court apply them at a glance. Read strictly, that line excludes a foster parent. Foster status is neither blood nor marriage, and Elden and Moon establish that deep affection and a shared household are not, by themselves, enough to cross it.

Research pause. Whether Dana falls inside that line cannot be looked up; it is argued by analogy, and it remains genuinely unsettled. The argument for Dana, written into the outline in brackets under the “closely related” element, goes like this. The foster relationship differs from the relationships rejected in Elden and Moon in a way that may matter. It is not an informal arrangement. It is a formal, state-recognized, legally created caregiving relationship in which the foster parent functionally occupies the role of parent, the very relationship at the center of Thing’s protected list. And Thing did not slam the door: it limited recovery “absent exceptional circumstances.” A lawyer for Dana would argue from what the line is for: the categories the court chose are exactly the statuses that come bundled with legal rights each party holds in the other, and foster parenthood is such a status, a formal, state-created relationship carrying legally imposed caregiving duties and recognized standing in the child’s life, so a multi-year foster parent-child relationship either falls within the principle of the protected parent/child category or is exactly the exceptional circumstance Thing left room for. The rejected relationships fail that principle, not just the list. An in-law relation fails it even though a marriage sits somewhere in the chain, because the marriage is the son-in-law’s to the daughter, not a marriage between the two of them, and the relation it creates affords neither any legal rights in the other: a mother-in-law could not sue for a son-in-law’s wrongful death and holds no rights or duties respecting him. And Elden’s cohabitants had, by definition, declined the legal status that would have vested them. A lawyer for the driver would answer that the entire point of Thing was to refuse this kind of functional, relationship-by-relationship inquiry, and that a line drawn at blood or marriage means what it says. That the question is genuinely open is not a guess: California practitioners describe whether a foster parent qualifies as “closely related” as unsettled and likely to be resolved by future case law.

Closure. The analysis is now as far along as the authority allows. The governing test is identified; the contested element is isolated; the decided relationships are synthesized into a blood-or-marriage line with a stated rationale and an “exceptional circumstances” hedge; and the foster-parent question is placed as genuinely open, with the argument available to each side laid out. More research would sharpen the wording of those arguments; it would not change the picture. This is analytical closure on a question that is open, and it is a legitimate stopping point. (If the assignment demanded a predicted outcome, the researcher would now make the best-supported prediction and flag the risk. That is an argument step, not another research question.)

What this example is meant to teach. Relevance was discovered, not pre-loaded: Dana’s foster status became the decisive fact only when Question 2 turned up a requirement that depended on it, which a planning-first approach that front-loads every fact cannot teach. Every term of art arrived as an output: “bystander,” “closely related,” “blood or marriage,” each entered the chain only after an answer delivered it. The gap was a finding, not a dead end: discovering that no case addresses a foster parent is the result that pushes an ordinary fact-finding question to its analogical edge. And synthesis can yield an open answer: the skill in Question 4 is synthesizing the decided line and stating, fairly, the strongest argument on each side, and an express exception is an invitation to argue what the line is for, a rationale the parties will characterize differently, because manufacturing false certainty here would be the real error. One more thing students should not lose: the blood-or-marriage line buys administrative certainty, a real virtue, and it also means a person who has been a child’s only parent for years may have no remedy at all for watching that child die. Both halves are true at once, and a good researcher holds them together rather than letting the tidiness of the rule hide its cost.

Borrowed Statutes and the Neighbor’s Answer (Chapter 5) #

Sometimes your jurisdiction has no law on the precise question, but a neighboring state does, and the two states are not strangers. Your state borrowed the governing statute from that neighbor, the frameworks run in parallel, and your courts have a habit of reading the borrowed provisions the way the neighbor’s courts read them. When that is the situation, the neighbor’s resolution carries far more weight than an ordinary out-of-state opinion. It is unusually strong predictive authority, for a reason with a name: under the prior-construction (or “adopted-statute”) canon, a legislature that adopts a statute from another jurisdiction is presumed to take it with the construction the source jurisdiction’s courts had already placed on it. The researcher’s job is to recognize the silence, find the neighbor, and then weigh, carefully, how much that neighbor’s answer is really worth.

The scenario. A client in Nevada has been sued for defamation over things they posted in a closed, invitation-only online forum, a private professional listserv with a few dozen members. The client wants to file an anti-SLAPP special motion to dismiss, arguing the posts were protected speech on a matter of public concern. The threshold question is whether posts in a small, closed forum like this one count as protected activity under Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute.

Where the researcher’s knowledge is. The researcher knows the client wants to use Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute and has a rough sense that such statutes give defendants an early way out of speech-based suits. That is the starting point. The researcher does not yet know the statute’s structure, does not yet know that Nevada modeled the statute on California’s, and, importantly, has no reason yet to care about California at all. Whether California matters is something the research will reveal, and only once Nevada turns up empty.

Question 1 (Focusing). What must a defendant show to prevail on a Nevada anti-SLAPP special motion to dismiss?

Answer, scoped to the question. Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute (NRS 41.635–41.670) sets up a two-step special motion. First, the defendant must show the claim is based on a protected “good faith communication in furtherance of the right to petition or the right to free speech in direct connection with an issue of public concern.” If the defendant carries that burden, the second step shifts to the plaintiff to show a probability of prevailing on the claim.

The motion depends first on whether the client’s posts are protected. The relevant protected category here is speech on a matter of public concern made in a public forum. So the next question is whether posts in this particular forum qualify, and here the facts about where and to whom the client spoke start to matter.

Question 2 (Focusing / Application, deeper). Does Nevada treat statements made in a small, closed, invitation-only online forum as protected speech “in direct connection with an issue of public concern” in a “public forum”?

Answer, scoped to the question. Neither the statute nor any Nevada appellate decision addresses this specific kind of forum. Nevada’s courts have resolved some forum questions at the other end of the spectrum, a message broadcast to a roughly 50,000-person listserv, posts on a public neighborhood app, but not a small, closed group. On this precise application, Nevada law is silent: it is a question of first impression here.

When the home jurisdiction has not spoken, the next question is whose reading of this kind of provision a Nevada court would be most likely to follow. Note what just happened: until Nevada turned up empty, there was no reason to look anywhere else. The silence is what makes the next question worth asking.

Question 3 (Focusing, choosing the persuasive authority). Whose construction of this kind of provision would a Nevada court find most persuasive?

Answer, scoped to the question. Two findings. First, California has a large, developed body of law on what counts as a “public forum” and an “issue of public interest” for anti-SLAPP purposes, reaching limited- and closed-audience settings as well as public ones. Second, Nevada did not write its anti-SLAPP statute on a blank slate: it modeled the statute on California’s (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16); it amended the statute in 2015 to require plaintiffs to meet “the same burden of proof” required under California’s anti-SLAPP law as of June 8, 2015 (NRS 41.665(2)); and the Nevada Supreme Court has repeatedly adopted California’s framework, including a set of public-interest considerations drawn directly from California’s appellate decisions.

Because Nevada borrowed the statute from California and its courts read the parallel provisions the way California does, California’s resolution of this kind of forum question is unusually strong persuasive authority, far stronger than an out-of-state case would ordinarily be. Three terms just arrived: persuasive authority, the borrowed-statute relationship, the prior-construction canon. None was assumed at the start. Each came up only after the prior answer made it relevant.

Question 4 (Focusing, the neighbor’s rule, with the parallelism check). What is California’s rule for a forum like this one?

Answer, scoped to the question. [Illustrative; research would supply the precise holding.] California’s courts analyze “public forum” and “public interest” through a developed set of factors, and research would show how they have treated a closed, limited-membership setting like this one. On parallelism, a caution: Nevada and California share the public-forum/public-interest framework, but their statutes are not identical. Nevada additionally requires the defendant to show the communication was truthful or made without knowledge of its falsehood, a requirement California’s statute does not impose, and the Nevada Supreme Court has expressly noted points of divergence (see Kosor v. Olympia Cos.). Before importing California’s answer, the researcher must confirm that the divergence does not touch the public-forum question.

Question 5 (Constructive application, predicting the home-state rule). Given the borrowing relationship and the parallel framework, what rule would a Nevada court likely adopt for a forum like this one?

Answer, constructed from the relationship, and marked as a prediction. The strongest prediction is that a Nevada court would follow California’s construction of the public-forum/public-interest requirement, for two reinforcing reasons: the prior-construction canon and the Nevada Supreme Court’s demonstrated practice of reading these provisions in step with California. But the prediction comes with limits the researcher should state plainly. Persuasive is not binding: a Nevada court remains free to part ways, and Nevada’s own decisions and statutory differences come first. Timing affects weight: the adopted-statute presumption is strongest for California constructions that predated Nevada’s adoption (and its 2015 amendment pegged to California law as of a fixed date); a later California decision is persuasive but not presumptively adopted, so its date matters to how much it is worth. And divergence narrows the borrowing: where Nevada’s statute departs from California’s, the truth/good-faith element is the clearest example, California authority carries little weight.

Closure. The Nevada framework is identified; the gap is established as a question of first impression; the most persuasive authority is located and weighted through the borrowing relationship; and the predicted rule, with its confidence qualified, is stated. That is analytical closure on a first-impression question. This is not a hypothetical phenomenon, either: in May 2026, the Nevada Supreme Court resolved two previously unsettled anti-SLAPP questions in Rosenbrook v. Lloyd, 142 Nev., Adv. Op. 36, and on one of them California had worked out its approach years earlier, which is part of why the answer was foreseeable. A researcher facing that question before Rosenbrook would have run essentially this chain, and would have predicted the result Nevada in fact reached.

The Timeclock and the Fingerprint (Chapter 6) #

The scenario. Our client owns a gym in Peoria, Illinois. In March 2022 the client installed a finger-scan timeclock. The vendor said the device stores a mathematical template of each finger rather than an image of the fingerprint, so the client never gave employees written notice or asked them to sign anything. A lawyer for a former front-desk employee has sent a demand letter claiming the gym violated the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act every time she clocked in. The client asks two things: what did the statute require of us, and how much trouble are we in? The attorney asks for an answer from the Act and the cases construing it.

Where the researcher’s knowledge is. The researcher has the name of the Act and nothing else. He does not know what it requires, whether a finger template counts, whether a gym is covered, or that the Act was amended partway through the period the letter covers. The first research question asks what the duty is.

Question 1 (Focusing). What does the Act require of a private business before it collects an employee’s biometric data?

Answer, scoped to the question. The annotated code’s Popular Name Table gives the citation, 740 ILCS 14, and the Act’s table of contents shows its shape: definitions, an operative section, a right of action, and exemptions. Read in full, the operative section, 740 ILCS 14/15, says in subsection (b) that no private entity may collect a person’s biometric identifier or biometric information unless it first informs the person in writing that it is being collected, states in writing the purpose and the length of storage and use, and receives a “written release.” [Illustrative; verify the exact wording against current law.]

Each duty attaches to a “biometric identifier” or “biometric information,” the words the vendor’s template claim was aimed at. Whether a template is one of those things is a definitional question, so it goes to the definitions section before it goes anywhere else.

Question 2 (Definitional). Is a mathematical template derived from a finger scan a “biometric identifier” or “biometric information” under the Act?

Answer, scoped to the question. Section 10 defines both terms. “Biometric identifier” is a closed list: a retina or iris scan, fingerprint, voiceprint, or scan of hand or face geometry. “Biometric information” means any information, “regardless of how it is captured, converted, stored, or shared,” based on a biometric identifier and used to identify the individual. [Illustrative; verify the exact wording against current law.] The second definition answers the vendor. A template is information based on a fingerprint, converted and stored, and used to identify the employee. The text is not silent, so the researcher does not go to the cases for the definition. Had it been silent, the Notes of Decisions under section 10 would have been the next stop, and only then.

Quick Check. The researcher asks whether he is still reading or has started skimming. He has not read the rest of section 15 or opened the exemptions section. Chapter 6 asks you to read the whole section before relying on any part of it. He writes the next question from that gap.

Question 3 (Focusing). Does the Act exempt a business like this one, or does any other part of section 15 change what the gym owed?

Answer, scoped to the question. Section 25 exempts, among others, financial institutions subject to the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act and contractors working for state and local government. A gym is neither. [Illustrative; verify the exemptions against current law.] Section 15(a) adds a duty the client never mentioned: a private entity in possession of biometric identifiers must develop a written, publicly available retention schedule and destruction guidelines. The gym has none. And section 10 defines “written release,” which in the employment context means a release executed by an employee as a condition of employment.

The letter covers scans from 2022 to this year, so the next question is whether the current text governed those years.

Question 4 (Currency). Which version of the Act governed the scans the letter covers, and has it changed since 2022?

Answer, scoped to the question. The historical notes beneath section 15 show the Act’s enactment, Public Act 95-994, effective October 3, 2008, and nothing since for that section. The notes beneath section 20 show an amendment, Public Act 103-0769, effective August 2, 2024. The session law provides that a private entity that collects the same biometric identifier from the same person by the same method commits a single violation of section 15(b), rather than a violation on every scan. [Illustrative; verify the amendment’s text and effective date against current law.] The duty did not change; the count of violations did, and the client’s scans run on both sides of August 2, 2024. Whether it reaches earlier scans is a question the session law does not answer, and courts have begun to. It belongs to exposure, the client’s second question, so it goes on the map in those words and the chain stays on the duty.

Checkpoint. The duty is stated from the statute, the definitional argument is resolved from the statute, the exemptions are checked, and the version in force is confirmed, all from Illinois primary law. One thing is missing before the rule can be synthesized in writing: how courts have applied section 15(b) to employers who ran biometric timeclocks without a release. That is the application question, and it asks about decided cases.

Question 5 (Application, fact-finding). How have Illinois courts applied section 15(b) to employers who collected employees’ finger scans for timekeeping without the written notice and release the section requires?

Answer, from the decided cases. The Notes of Decisions under sections 15 and 20 point to a line of Illinois Supreme Court decisions, and the researcher reads each opinion rather than the editor’s summary of it. [Illustrative; confirm each holding against the opinion.] In Rosenbach v. Six Flags Entertainment Corp., 2019 IL 123186, the court held that a person whose data is collected in violation of section 15 is “aggrieved” and may sue without alleging injury beyond the violation itself. In McDonald v. Symphony Bronzeville Park, LLC, 2022 IL 126511, the court held that an employee’s claim over a fingerprint timeclock was not barred by the exclusivity provision of the Workers’ Compensation Act. In Tims v. Black Horse Carriers, Inc., 2023 IL 127801, the court applied the five-year catch-all limitations period to section 15 claims. And in Cothron v. White Castle System, Inc., 2023 IL 128004, the court held that a separate claim accrued on each scan; Cothron is the decision the 2024 amendment answered. Read together, the cases treat the missing notice and release as the violation, with no further harm needed.

Research pause. The researcher opens the annotated outline and writes the application under the section 15(b) element, in brackets. [Our facts: finger-scan clock installed March 2022; no written notice, no statement of purpose or duration, no release, no retention schedule. Under section 10, the template argument fails on the text. No section 25 exemption applies. Under Rosenbach, the former employee need not show harm beyond the violation. Under McDonald, workers’ compensation exclusivity is no defense. Under Tims, the 2022 scans are within the limitations period. Exposure: the count of violations before August 2, 2024 depends on whether the amendment reaches earlier conduct; open, on the map.]

Closure. On the duty question, this is analytical closure: the rule, the version in force, and the decided applications are all in hand. On exposure the closure is practical, and the note says so: the reach of the single-violation amendment is open in the courts, and the attorney needs the duty answer this week. The closing-out protocol runs before anything goes out: a final sweep for appellate decisions on the amendment, a citator run on every authority, a check that each decision is cited for what it held, a review of the map with the accrual question parked by name, a look for outline gaps, and a closure note naming both kinds of closure and the date each authority was verified.

What this example is meant to teach. The chain ran down one element, the section 15(b) duty, through its sub-issues before it moved anywhere else: what the duty is, what its defined terms cover, whether an exemption removes the client from it, which version governed, and how courts have applied it to facts like these. Only when that element could be synthesized in writing and applied to the facts did the researcher let a sideways question, exposure, onto the map, and he parked it there rather than chasing it. The currency check on one section turned up an amendment in the section next door. And the application question asked how courts had applied the section to other employers’ timeclocks, not how it applies to this gym. That was answered in brackets, during the pause, by the researcher himself.

The Salaried Store Manager (Chapter 7) #

The scenario. Our client runs four bakery-cafés in Ohio. Each store’s general manager is paid $52,000 a year, about $1,000 a week, with no overtime, for fifty to fifty-five hours most weeks. One manager has filed a complaint with the Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division claiming unpaid overtime back to early 2024. She says she spent most of every shift on the counter and the register, the hourly staff’s work. The client asks: were we allowed to treat her as exempt? The attorney asks you to work from the Department’s regulations, starting with the statute that authorizes them.

Where the researcher’s knowledge is. The researcher knows the client has been using “salaried” and “exempt” as the same word and suspects they are not. She does not know there is a duties test, and does not yet know that how the manager spent her hours is a fact the rule will ask about.

Question 1 (Focusing, the delegation). What does the Fair Labor Standards Act say about the executive exemption, and what did Congress leave to the agency?

Answer, scoped to the question. Section 13(a)(1) of the Act, 29 U.S.C. § 213(a)(1), exempts “any employee employed in a bona fide executive, administrative, or professional capacity … as such terms are defined and delimited from time to time by regulations of the Secretary.” [Illustrative; verify against current law.] That clause is the authority line: the regulation is law because the statute says the Secretary of Labor may define these terms, and it is bounded by that grant.

Question 2 (Focusing). Which regulation defines “employed in a bona fide executive capacity,” and what does it require?

Answer, scoped to the question. The annotated code’s Implementing Regulations note under § 213 points to 29 C.F.R. Part 541, and the part’s table of contents in the eCFR confirms it. The authority note at the head of Part 541 cites 29 U.S.C. § 213, the statute from Question 1. Section 541.100(a) sets out the test: a salary basis at not less than the stated weekly amount; a primary duty of management; customarily and regularly directing the work of two or more other employees; and authority to hire or fire, or recommendations given particular weight. [Illustrative; verify the current text.]

The complaint is about what she did all day, so the disputed element is primary duty, and the chain goes down it first.

Question 3 (Definitional). What does the regulation mean by “primary duty”?

Answer, scoped to the question. The definitions are in Subpart H. Section 541.700(a) defines primary duty as the principal or most important duty the employee performs, and names four factors: the relative importance of the exempt duties, the time spent on them, the employee’s freedom from direct supervision, and the relationship between her salary and the wages paid for the nonexempt work she performs. Section 541.700(b) says time is a useful guide and not the sole test. Section 541.106, on concurrent duties, is about this manager: doing exempt and nonexempt work at the same time does not by itself defeat the exemption, and the section’s own example is an assistant manager in a retail establishment. [Illustrative; verify the current text.]

The time split the client described is one of four factors, and not the deciding one. The other three ask for facts nobody has gathered: who scheduled and hired, who the manager answered to, and what the hourly staff were paid.

Question 4 (Currency). Is the salary threshold I am reading the one that governed the complaint period, and has Part 541 changed since early 2024?

Answer, scoped to the question. The eCFR carries its updated-through date, and the source note for § 541.600 lists the Federal Register documents that set the threshold. The 2019 rule set $684 a week from January 1, 2020; a 2024 final rule raised it to $844 on July 1, 2024 and $1,128 on January 1, 2025. The Federal Register and a citator run on § 541.600 then show what the codified text does not: in November 2024 a federal district court vacated the 2024 rule nationwide, and the Department announced it would enforce the 2019 threshold. [Illustrative; verify the rule’s status and the current eCFR text against current law.] At $1,000 a week the manager was above $684 and $844 and below $1,128. Had the 2024 rule stood, she would have fallen below the threshold in January 2025; with it vacated, the salary element is met for the whole period.

Checkpoint. The research is working, with one caution: the codified text and the enforceable rule came apart in Question 4, and the researcher found that only because she went past the eCFR to the Federal Register and the citator. She notes in the log that the currency check is not finished until both have been run.

Question 5 (Focusing, agency guidance). What has the Department said about managers who spend most of their time on nonexempt work, and what weight does that carry?

Answer, scoped to the question. The Wage and Hour Division’s website collects its fact sheet on the executive exemption and its opinion letters. [Illustrative; verify the current guidance.] None of these went through notice and comment. They state how the agency reads its own regulation and what its investigator will look for. What weight a court would give them is a different question, answered in Chapter 11 with the decisions that govern it, Loper Bright among them. She logs the guidance as the agency’s position, non-binding, and puts its weight on the map as a Chapter 11 question.

Quick Check. The researcher notices she has started reading the opinion letters for an answer about the client’s manager. Measuring the rule against the file is writing, and it belongs in the pause. She writes a note for tomorrow’s self: decided cases next, read the opinions and not the summaries, stop when the line is clear.

Question 6 (Application, fact-finding). How have courts applied the primary-duty test to store managers who spent most of their hours on the hourly staff’s work?

Answer, from the decided cases. A citator run on §§ 541.700 and 541.106 points to a long line of retail and restaurant cases, and the researcher reads the leading opinions, Sixth Circuit first. [Illustrative; confirm each holding against the opinion.] The decisions divide, and the pattern in them is the finding. Courts have found managers exempt despite majority nonexempt time where the manager was the only supervisor on site and controlled scheduling and hiring (Donovan v. Burger King Corp., 672 F.2d 221 (1st Cir. 1982)). Managers were held not exempt where a district manager made the real decisions, manuals confined the manager’s discretion, and her pay was near the hourly staff’s (Morgan v. Family Dollar Stores, Inc., 551 F.3d 1233 (11th Cir. 2008)). The cases that go against the employer tend to do so on freedom from supervision and relative pay rather than on the time split alone.

Research pause. Into the outline, under the primary-duty element, in brackets: [Our facts: manager at $1,000 a week, 50 to 55 hours, says most of her time was counter and register work. Salary basis met for the whole period given the vacated 2024 rule. Time factor favors her. Unknown, requested from the client: who set schedules and made hiring recommendations; whether an area manager directed daily operations; the hourly staff’s rate against hers. Only manager on site, controlling scheduling and hiring: Burger King supports the exemption. Area manager running the store by manual, effective rate near the hourly staff’s: Family Dollar cuts against it.]

Closure. Analytical on the rule. The rule, its definitions, its threshold for every month at issue, and its decided applications are in hand, and the guidance is logged with its weight parked. What remains is factual, and that goes to the client. The closing-out protocol runs before the memo: a final sweep for Sixth Circuit decisions and new Federal Register activity in Part 541, a citator run on every authority, a check that each case is cited for what it held, a review of the map with the Chapter 11 question parked, a look for outline gaps, and a closure note with the eCFR updated-through date and the verification dates.

What this example is meant to teach. The chain started at the statute’s delegation because a regulation is law only to the extent a statute says so. It then ran down one element, primary duty, through its sub-issues before moving sideways: the regulation’s own definition, the concurrent-duties provision, the threshold in force for the dates at issue, the agency’s reading, and the decided cases, until the element could be synthesized in writing and applied to the facts in brackets. The currency check showed why the chapter stresses it: the eCFR text and the enforceable rule had parted. And the application question asked how courts applied the test to other store managers. Whether this manager was exempt went into brackets, and it waits on facts.

ADA Website Accessibility (Chapter 8) #

Macro question: Must a business make its website accessible to persons with disabilities under the ADA?

Step 1, Secondary Source (Orientation). Begin with Am. Jur. 2d or a disability law treatise. The secondary source identifies 42 U.S.C. § 12182 (Title III, public accommodations) as the key statutory provision and notes that courts are divided on whether websites count as “public accommodations.” Next question: does Title III apply to websites?

Step 2, Statute (Primary Framework). Read 42 U.S.C. § 12182. The text does not mention websites, because it was enacted in 1990. The annotations show many cases divided between circuits. Next question: what do the DOJ’s regulations say?

Step 3, Regulation (Implementing Rules). Review 28 C.F.R. Part 36 (DOJ’s ADA Title III regulations). No specific web-accessibility regulation exists, though the DOJ has issued guidance documents and proposed regulations. That raises two questions, logged and taken one at a time: what does the DOJ’s guidance say? And, after it, what weight do courts give it?

Step 4, Case Law (Judicial Interpretation). Find a leading circuit decision on ADA website accessibility. Use headnotes and key numbers for “Public Accommodations, Disability, Website” to locate additional cases. Check the citator for pending Supreme Court consideration. Note the circuit split.

Step 5, Synthesize. Your annotated outline now holds the statutory framework, the current regulatory gap, the agency guidance and its weight, and the state of the case law including the circuit split. You can now write an accurate, complete analysis of a genuinely contested question. Each step’s “next question” pointed to a different source type. That is multi-source research working as designed.
