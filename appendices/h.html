Appendix H

Sample Answers for the Reviewing Through Practice Exercises

How to use this appendix. Attempt the exercise first, all the way to a finished deliverable, and only then come here. What follows are sample answers, not the only answers. Where an exercise depends on your jurisdiction, your project, or your search date, the sample models one honest instantiation and marks the variable parts in brackets, exactly as Appendix E does: brackets mean verify against current law before relying on this. Where sibling questions could run in any order, your order and the sample’s are equally correct. The test to apply is never “does my answer match the sample word for word.” It is the test each exercise’s self-check already gave you, and the sample exists to show you what a deliverable that passes it looks like on the page.

Chapter 1

Exercise 1.1 — The Layered-Authority Fact Pattern

A passing map reaches both levels of government and at least three source types, and every row is traceable to something given: the scenario supplied the federal prohibition and the state authorization, and the chapter’s employment-dispute example supplied the template for the rest. Notice what the predictions do and do not claim: each says what the layer might contribute, because at this stage prediction is all the map entitles you to. Whether any layer actually controls is what the research will decide.

Level Source type Predicted role
Federal Statute The federal drug statute classifies marijuana as illegal regardless of state authorization; it may limit what protection any other layer can give.
Federal or state Statute An anti-discrimination statute may exist at either level, as in the chapter’s employment-dispute example; whether one reaches a medical condition or its authorized treatment is a research question.
Federal or state Regulation Where a statute at either level applies, an agency’s regulations may fill in its detail, as the chapter’s example paired agency rules with the statute they implement.
State Statute The state medical marijuana statute authorized her use; whether it also protects employees from termination is a research question, since states differ.
State Statute The state disability or anti-discrimination statute may treat the underlying condition, or the authorized treatment, as protected.
State Case law The at-will employment doctrine is the common-law baseline; any protection has to displace it, and judge-made exceptions (such as a public-policy exception) may apply.

A map that also flagged the state constitution is stronger still. A map with every row at one level or in one type fails the self-check, and so does a row invoking a doctrine neither the chapter nor the scenario gave you: at this stage, a prediction you cannot trace to the page is a guess wearing a prediction’s clothes.

Exercise 1.2 — Binding or Persuasive?

The seven classifications, each tied to a stated rule. (a) Binding: the Arizona Supreme Court sits above the trial court in the same system, and the chapter’s own example says an Arizona trial court must follow it on state law. (b) Binding: the Court of Appeals is also a court above the trial court in the same system. (c) Persuasive: California’s decisions have no binding effect in Arizona; authority binds only when it comes from home. (d) Persuasive: a decision from a court at the same level does not bind, however sound its reasoning. (e) Persuasive: a judicial opinion binds only if it comes from a higher court in the same court system, and the federal district court sits in a different system; the chapter even notes the influence runs the other way on state-law questions. (f) Persuasive: a treatise is secondary authority, which explains the law but never binds. (g) Binding: the chapter states that all courts must comply with U.S. Supreme Court decisions interpreting the U.S. Constitution. The final sentence, from the chapter’s own list: a court is most likely to follow persuasive authority when the issue is new or unsettled at home, when the reasoning is particularly strong, or when the law is trending that way across the country. Every justification above invokes a rule the chapter stated and nothing else, which is the pass condition; no knowledge of Arizona negligence law was needed, and none was used.

Exercise 1.3 — Who Gets the Last Word?

Rowan Companies. The Court read the statute; Congress disagreed with the reading and amended the statute; the amendment stands. Congress held the last word, because when the conflict is over what a statute means, the legislature can always answer a court by changing the text.

Texas v. Johnson. The Court held the statute violated the First Amendment, and no ordinary statute could respond, because the conflict was between a statute and the Constitution. The Court held the last word; only a constitutional amendment could have answered it.

The answer the two stories set up. The kind of conflict decides it, not the actors. When the conflict is over what a statute means, the legislature can always have the last word by amending the text; when the conflict is between a statute and the Constitution, the court’s word stands, because no ordinary statute can override the supreme law of its jurisdiction. The self-check’s trap is worth naming: the same two institutions, Court and Congress, appear in both stories, so any answer built on who was involved cannot explain why the outcomes differ. Only the kind of conflict can.

Chapter 2

Exercise 2.1 — Translate the Question

Three concepts hide in the dog-bite question: the animal, the injuring act, and the owner’s responsibility. A passing translation: concept one, dog OR canine OR “domestic animal” OR pet; concept two, bite OR bites OR bitten OR attack! OR maul!; concept three, owner OR keeper, paired with plain responsibility words: liable OR responsible OR fault OR damages. Joined: (dog OR canine OR pet) AND (bite OR bitten OR attack! OR maul!) AND (owner OR keeper) AND (liable OR responsible OR fault OR damages). And the root-expander trap, caught: bit! looks like a tidy way to gather bite, bites, and bitten, but it also drags in bitter, bitumen, and every other word sharing the root, so the passing answer lists the bite forms out by hand and reserves the expander for roots that survive the drag-in test, like attack! and maul!. All of these are everyday words, and that is the point: this chapter’s skill is translation from plain language, and the terms of art courts actually use will arrive with Chapter 4’s vocabulary work. A set built honestly from what you know today passes.

Exercise 2.2 — Two Modes, One Question

A passing set of search notes, with the result observations bracketed because they vary by platform and date: Filters, set before either search: jurisdiction, [your state]; source type, cases; because the question asks what this state’s courts do, and unfiltered results would bury the answer under fifty states of noise. Natural language query: is a dog owner liable when the dog bites a mail carrier. Terms-and-connectors query: the synonym-set string built above, concepts joined with AND. Three sentences, illustrative: [The natural language run surfaced conceptually related material, including secondary sources and near-miss cases, ranked by the platform’s relevance judgment. The terms-and-connectors run returned a tighter set that matched my words exactly, including everything with my synonyms and nothing without them, which means its misses are my vocabulary’s misses. To start the project I would trust the natural language run for orientation and the terms-and-connectors run once I knew the terms of art.] The generic version of those sentences, “the good one was better,” fails the self-check; the sentences must name what differed.

Exercise 2.3 — Retrieve, Don’t Search

The three anatomies. Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988): case name; 484 is the volume; U.S. is the reporter (United States Reports, which itself tells you the court); 260 is the first page; 1988 is the decision year. 42 U.S.C. § 12101: 42 is the title; U.S.C. is the code (United States Code); 12101 is the section. 29 C.F.R. § 1605.1: 29 is the title; C.F.R. is the code (Code of Federal Regulations); 1605 is the part and .1 the section within it. The retrieval paths: the case through the platform’s citation-lookup box, entered as the citation; the statute through the code’s title-and-section lookup or by browsing Title 42 to the section; the regulation through the eCFR, browsing Title 29 to Part 1605. No subject-matter words entered anywhere, because none were needed: each citation is an address, and the whole exercise is learning to drive to the address instead of asking the neighborhood.

Chapter 3

Exercise 3.1 — Name the Type

  1. Definitional: it asks what a term of art means. (b) Exploratory: it asks for the shape of an area, not a specific rule. (c) Focusing: it asks for the governing standard on a specific point. (d) Focusing, one level deeper: it narrows a broader rule (the necessity requirement) to a sub-rule. (e) Application: it asks how a rule operates on specific facts by comparison to decided cases. (f) Exploratory, survey flavor: it asks how jurisdictions divide, which points at survey sources. (g) The intruder: not a research question at all. It is a factual, investigative question; no source of law will ever answer it, only evidence will, thermostat records or witness testimony. (h) Exploratory: it asks about a statute’s purpose, and if the text proves ambiguous it may eventually justify legislative history research, but as drafted it is a landscape question. If all eight received a question type, the distinction between what you research and what you investigate needs another pass, because keeping those separate is what stops a research log from filling up with questions no database can close.

Exercise 3.2 — Reverse-Engineer the Paragraph

Answers vary with the assigned paragraph, so here is the standard modeled on a paragraph summarizing the core reasoning of Hazelwood (sentences paraphrased): Sentence: the newspaper was produced in a journalism class as part of the curriculum, under a faculty advisor. Question it answers: is this newspaper school-sponsored? (Application). Sentence: the school had never, by policy or practice, opened the paper to indiscriminate student use. Question: was the paper a public forum? (Focusing, then Application). Sentence: educators may exercise control over school-sponsored expression when their actions are reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. Question: what standard governs a school’s control over school-sponsored student expression? (Focusing). Sentence: the principal’s concerns involved protecting the privacy of students discussed in the articles. Question: do the stated reasons fit a legitimate pedagogical concern? (Application). The pass test: read the four questions alone, in order. They chain, each one askable because of the answer before it. If your questions read as a disconnected list, some of them answered the paragraph’s topic rather than its sentences, and the fix is to go one sentence at a time. One more thing worth noticing: the application questions in this chain are the ones the court answered in writing, applying the rule to the parties before it. In your own project those questions live in the outline’s bracketed notes, as Chapter 3’s demonstration shows, which is why you meet them here in a finished opinion rather than in a Research Log.

Exercise 3.3 — One Full Cycle

A passing entry, run on the standard question from the chain above:

Field Entry
Research Question What standard governs a school’s control over a school-sponsored student newspaper?
Question Type Focusing
Source Consulted Hazelwood Sch. Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, [database], [date accessed]
What I Found A school may regulate school-sponsored expression so long as its action is reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns.
How This Answers My Question Fully answers the standard question; the standard’s two working parts (school-sponsored, legitimate pedagogical concerns) are not yet applied.
Citation Hazelwood Sch. Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988)
Next Question What makes a publication “school-sponsored” under that standard?
Notes Opinion also addresses forum analysis and distinguishes the personal-expression line of cases; both logged as later questions, not folded into this answer.

Watch what the What I Found field refuses to do: the opinion offered the whole landscape, and the entry took only what the question asked. The forum analysis and the distinction from the personal-expression cases went to Notes as later questions. That refusal is the scoping discipline, and the Next Question following visibly from What I Found is the chain discipline. An entry that fails either one is the entry to rerun.

Exercise 3.4 — Draft the First Question

Client one. Diagnosis: no vocabulary; you do not know what this document is called. Opening move: acquisition, by plain-language search. First question: what is a document called that gives up the right to sue an employer? Everything in that question is either plain English or file-supplied, and its only job is to surface the terms of art, which research would show to be words like release, waiver, and severance agreement, none of which you may use until an answer has delivered them. Client two. Diagnosis: you have the words (permit, zoning, from the file) but no grip on how the area is put together. Opening move: exploratory. First question: how do zoning and permit requirements regulate what a homeowner may build on their own property? Client three. Diagnosis: firm enough grip that a concrete question is answerable. Opening move: focusing. First question: does [State] require landlords to provide heat? And the tomato check on all three: none of the questions presupposes what any legal term means. Client three’s question does not assume what “provide heat” requires, how quickly, or to what temperature; those are exactly the sub-questions the first answer will hand you.

Chapter 4

Exercise 4.1 — Match the Source to the Question

Researcher one gets a legal encyclopedia: plain-language orientation and vocabulary for a no-clue start is exactly what encyclopedias are built for. Researcher two gets a treatise: full doctrinal structure with the leading authorities organized is the treatise’s job description. Researcher three gets an ALR annotation or a fifty-state survey: a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction count on a narrow rule is survey work. Researcher four gets law review articles and working papers: the newest scholarly thinking on unsettled questions lives there, months or years before any treatise catches up. Four researchers, four different sources; if two of yours matched, the mismatched one was almost certainly researcher one or two, and the difference to internalize is orientation versus structure.

Exercise 4.2 — The Vocabulary Harvest

A passing harvest from a single encyclopedia entry on the grandparents’ question, terms in the order they surfaced: grandparent visitation (the area’s name, and the term that unlocks every database); third-party or nonparental visitation (the broader category the doctrine sits inside); best interests of the child (the recurring standard); fit-parent presumption (the constitutional weight given a fit parent’s decision, which the entry flags as the doctrine’s central tension, anchored to a leading Supreme Court case the entry names [confirm the case and its current treatment]); and the fact, noted rather than assumed, that the rules are statutory and state-by-state. The focusing question the harvest permits: under [State]’s grandparent-visitation statute, when may a court order visitation over a fit parent’s objection? Thirty minutes ago that question was unwritable; every term it uses was acquired on the page, and that before-and-after is the pass standard.

Chapter 5

Exercise 5.1 — The Five Moves, Run Once

The annotated outline entry, scoped: under Hazelwood, a school may exercise editorial control over the style and content of student speech in school-sponsored expressive activities so long as its actions are reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. 484 U.S. 260 (1988). That is the question’s answer and nothing else. The collateral list, with pathways: (1) When is student expression “school-sponsored” rather than personal? — Hazelwood itself, and the personal-expression line it distinguishes, Tinker v. Des Moines, 393 U.S. 503 (1969). (2) When has a school opened a paper as a public forum, and what changes if it has? — the forum cases Hazelwood collects [record the citations from your reading]. (3) What counts as a “legitimate pedagogical concern” at the edges? — the applications discussed in the opinion, and the school-speech cases decided alongside it, including Bethel Sch. Dist. v. Fraser, 478 U.S. 675 (1986) [confirm]. Three or more collateral questions logged, none synthesized: the pathways are recorded for later cycles, which is the fourth move doing its job. An outline entry that instead summarized the whole opinion read the case; the exercise was reading for the answer.

Exercise 5.2 — Dispositive Facts

Three facts survive the test, each with its sentence. The curricular production: if instead the paper had been an independent student publication rather than a journalism-class product, then the personal-expression standard rather than the school-sponsored standard would govern, because the Court’s reasoning turns on the school lending its name and resources to the expression. The forum history: if instead the school had by policy or practice opened the paper to indiscriminate student use, then the school’s editorial control would face a stricter test, because the Court’s deference rests on the paper never having become a public forum. The stated concerns: if instead the principal’s reasons had no connection to pedagogy, bare disagreement with a viewpoint, then the action would fail even the deferential standard, because the standard’s own terms require the pedagogical link. Facts like the paper’s name or the articles’ exact subjects fail the test: change them and the reasoning holds, which is what background means. Every passing sentence points at a specific passage of reasoning; a sentence that works without one was a guess, and the exercise exists to replace guessing with locating.

Chapter 6

Exercise 6.1 — Anatomy of a Section

Maps vary with the assigned statute, so here is the standard modeled on the ADA’s employment title, chosen because its anatomy sprawls across neighboring sections the way real statutes do. Operative provision: the general discrimination prohibition, 42 U.S.C. § 12112 [confirm], stating who may not do what to whom. Defined terms: the operative section leans on terms it does not define; “disability” is defined in § 12102 and terms like “qualified individual” and “reasonable accommodation” in § 12111 [confirm both], neither of them the section you started in. Exceptions and defenses: a separate defenses section [locate it] qualifies the prohibition. Consequences: the enforcement section [locate it] supplies remedies by incorporation from another statute entirely, a move worth noting because it means the penalty question cannot be answered inside this title alone. Currency: the code’s currency note, with its through-date recorded. The self-check predicted this: a map showing no defined terms and no exceptions almost always means the neighboring sections went unread, and this statute makes the point unmissable, since nearly every load-bearing term lives next door to the section that uses it.

Exercise 6.2 — Annotations Are Pathways

Two passing entries, case names bracketed because they depend on your statute and your annotated code’s editors: Entry one. Research Question: how have courts interpreted [the key term] in this section? Source: annotated code, Notes of Decisions under the section. What I Found: the annotation describes [Case A, full citation as given] as addressing [the point the editor’s squib claims]. How This Answers My Question: lead only; the annotation is an editor’s summary and the case is unread. Next Question: does [Case A] actually hold what the annotation claims? Entry two runs the same form on [Case B]. The pass condition sits in one field: How This Answers My Question says lead, and What I Found attributes the claim to the annotation, not to the case. An entry stating what the case held exceeded the exercise, because all you possess is what an editor said about it, and the gap between those two things is the reason the verification question exists.

Exercise 7.1 — Locate and Verify

The regulation: the National Organic Program, 7 C.F.R. Part 205, with the definition of “organic” in the definitions section, § 205.2 [confirm]. The currency note, one line per step, the date-dependent findings bracketed because yours will differ: (1) eCFR: text located; Title 7 shown current through [the exact date displayed on the day you checked, which the deliverable requires you to record]. (2) LSA: not needed; the eCFR was the working source, and the note says so, which is itself a finding. (3) Federal Register: [what your sweep of the part surfaced; organic-program rulemaking is active enough that a competent sweep almost always finds proposed or recent rules somewhere in the part]. (4) Citator: [decisions interpreting, applying, or challenging the sections you care about, if any]. (5) Agency website: [USDA’s organic-program guidance and handbook materials, which shape how the rules operate in practice]. Exercise 7.2 — Trace the Delegation

The authority note for Part 205 points to the Organic Foods Production Act, codified at 7 U.S.C. §§ 6501 et seq. [confirm the range against the authority note as printed]. The two sentences: The statute decided that a national organic certification program would exist, that products sold as organic must be certified to national standards, and that USDA would run the program. The regulation supplies what the statute left open: the operative definitions, the production and handling standards, the certification machinery, and the labeling thresholds that turn the statute’s framework into rules a farmer can follow. The pass test is that the sentences describe different things, the legislature’s judgment and the agency’s detail, and here the line is bright: Congress said there shall be a program with standards; the agency said here is what the standards are.

Chapter 8

Exercise 8.1 — Build the Triangle

The corners: statute corner, the Organic Foods Production Act, located through the regulation’s authority note, which created the program and delegated the standards; regulation corner, 7 C.F.R. Part 205, located through the eCFR, which supplies the definitions, standards, and labeling rules; case-law corner, [a decision interpreting either, located through an annotated code or a citator]. The edges, which are the exercise: statute to regulation, “what detail did the agency supply under this delegation, and where?”, the question that sent you from the authority note into the CFR; regulation to case law, “how have courts read and applied this standard?”, the question an annotation answers, and “has it survived challenge?”, the one a citator answers; case law back to statute, “does the regulation stay within what the statute delegated?”, the question a challenge forces, and the one courts now answer without deferring to the agency’s own view of its statute. Three full corners with empty edges fails the self-check; the corners are a catalogue, and the edges are the research.

Exercise 8.2 — Unsettled or Unfound?

The form of a passing pair, content bracketed because it comes from your own drills. The unsettled finding: “The law is genuinely unsettled on [the point]: [the leading authorities] expressly reserve it, and [the survey source] confirms the jurisdictions divide. I searched [the databases], with [the term sets, including alternatives], through [date].” Its defense earns the professional conclusion by showing the search was thorough enough that a supervisor would accept it, and by pointing at authority that says the question is open rather than at silence. The unfound confession: “I have not yet found the answer to [the point]. I searched [one database] with [one term set]; I have not yet tried [the alternative terms or source types a full sweep would include].” Its defense is honest about the gap between what was searched and what a closure-grade sweep requires. The two labels cannot trade places, because the defenses are doing different work: one demonstrates exhaustion, the other admits its absence. If yours could swap, neither defense was specific enough yet.

Chapter 9

Exercise 9.1 — Precision Translation

One of the three modeled in full; the other two follow the same form. Question: is a dog’s owner liable when the dog bites a delivery worker on the owner’s property? Precision translation: (dog OR canine) /p (bite OR bitten OR attack! OR maul!) /p (owner OR keeper) AND (liab! OR negligen! OR “strict liability”), jurisdiction filtered to [your state], source type cases. The Search Log line: query as entered; filters and why; result count [yours]; one line on quality [yours]. What makes it a Chapter 9 translation rather than a Chapter 2 translation is the proximity work: /p binds the animal, the act, and the owner into the same paragraph, so the results discuss them together instead of merely containing them somewhere in the same document. Three strings whose only connective tissue is AND fail the self-check; the upgrade was the point.

Exercise 9.2 — The Bad Translation

The deliberately bad version: landlord AND staircase AND injury AND liable. The proper version: (landlord OR lessor OR “property owner”) /p (stair! OR step OR railing) /p (injur! OR fell OR fall!) AND (guest OR visitor OR invitee), jurisdiction and source type filtered. Both counts recorded [yours will vary by platform and date]. Two passing sentences, illustrative: [The bad string returned a far larger set whose first page scattered the four words across unrelated discussions, premises pages apart from injuries. The precision string returned a fraction of the volume, and its first page discussed the landlord, the staircase, and the injury in the same breath, which is what the proximity connectors purchased.] The generic “the good one was better” fails; the sentences must name the specific difference, and the fastest place to find it, as the self-check said, is result ten in each list.

Exercise 9.3 — The Closing-Out Run

A passing closure record, run on any finished exercise: the final sweep documented, with the alternative terms actually listed and their results noted, even when the note is “nothing new surfaced,” because that nothing is evidence; the citator step, every authority updated, statuses recorded; the check back against the original question, confirming every sub-question is either answered or named as genuinely open, with Chapter 8’s unsettled-versus-unfound distinction applied to any that remain; and the explicit finding, in a sentence: “Analytical closure reached: [the questions] are answered, [any named point] is genuinely open per [the authority that says so], and further research would only refine wording.” Or, where an external limit forced the stop, the practical-closure version, naming what remains. The single pass condition is the chapter’s own line. If you cannot document why you stopped, you have not closed. You have just stopped.

Chapter 10

Exercise 10.1 — The Sanctions Autopsy

The rebuilt table, from the opinion’s own account [supply pincites from your reading]: Failure one, counsel relied on an older decision without conducting research beyond it; rule, the duty of competence, Rule 1.1, which demands the current, controlling answer; preventing step, the citator update in the closing-out protocol, which exists to catch law that has moved. Failure two, counsel did not reread the cases cited in the most recent controlling appellate decision on the issue; rule, again 1.1, competence in reading and synthesizing, not just locating; preventing step, the read-before-you-cite check, because an unread case is an unverified claim. Where your table and the chapter’s differ, apply the exercise’s own diagnostic: a failure you caught that the chapter’s table generalized over is good reading; a failure you attributed that the opinion does not support is the autopsy catching you, which is the more valuable lesson of the two.

Exercise 10.2 — The Adverse-Authority Question

A passing entry, modeled on the store-duty drill: Research Question: what controlling authority in our state cuts against the conclusion that the store owed no duty [or owed a duty, whichever your chain reached]? Question Type: Focusing, adversarial posture. Source Consulted: [database], with the searches listed as run, including the terms aimed at the opposite conclusion, not just your own conclusion restated. What I Found: [the adverse authorities located, or “none located”]. How This Answers My Question: [addressed how, or empty result stands]. Both outcomes pass, with one condition: the searches appear in the entry. An empty result logged with its searches is Rule 3.3 compliance in miniature; an empty log is the thing the rule refuses to accept.

Exercise 10.3 — The Verification Pass

Three sample lines showing the form; a full log has one line per source: Hazelwood Sch. Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988) | verified in [database] | citator: [status and any flags, with the flagged points checked against the propositions you cite it for] | [date]. [Your state’s statute] | current text confirmed against [the official code], through-date [recorded] | pending amendments: [checked] | [date]. [The regulation] | eCFR, current through [date shown] | citator: [status] | [date]. Every line carries a status and a date, because the log’s professional function, the one Rules 5.1 and 5.3 give it, depends on a reviewer being able to see not just that you checked but when.

Chapter 11

Exercise 11.1 — Read the Docket

Entry numbers vary by case, and the case must be recent enough to live in the electronic filing era, which is why the exercise says so; a passing answer’s standard is the recognition method: Complaint, [entry number], recognized as the earliest substantive filing, plaintiff as filer, labeled as a complaint or petition. Dispositive motion and brief, [entry number], recognized by the motion’s label (to dismiss, for summary judgment), the filer, and the supporting memorandum docketed with or beside it. Resolving order, [entry number], recognized as a court-entered document, after briefing concluded, whose text line disposes of the motion. One line each on the recognition, and, where the docket text alone failed you and a document had to be opened, that failure noted, because on a metered system each open is money, and the log-reading skill is the skill of not paying for what the log already says.

Exercise 11.2 — The Brief Autopsy

For whichever brief you located, the three harvested authorities: [as cited in the brief, full citations]. The verification entry on the one you chose: The brief’s claim, quoted or pincited as the brief made it: [the brief’s characterization]. The source’s own words, quoted or pincited from your reading of the primary authority: [what it actually says]. Verdict: [supported; supported but overstated; or unsupported], with one sentence on the gap if there is one. The pass condition is structural: both sides on the page. A verdict without the brief’s language and the source’s language is an impression, and an impression about a characterization is how the error you are checking for reproduces itself.

Exercise 11.3 — The Rules Ladder

A passing ladder for whichever district you chose, numbers bracketed because they are the exercise: Rung one, the governing Federal Rule, and note that for many filing mechanics the federal rule is silent on the number you went looking for; silence recorded is a finding, because it tells you which rung actually supplies the rule. Rung two, the district’s local rule, [cited], supplying [the page or word limit], found on the court’s website. Rung three, the assigned judge’s standing order, [cited or “none”], and where one exists it typically tightens or adds: [the delta]. The self-check’s test is the delta: if two rungs say the same thing, reread, because local rules and standing orders exist to add or tighten, and the delta is what a filer gets sanctioned for missing. Where the judge has no standing order on the point, “none” recorded is a passing third rung; an empty rung known is knowledge.

Chapter 12

Exercise 12.1 — The Entry Ticket

Both passing shapes, modeled. The ticket earned: “The statute is ambiguous as to [the term]: the text does not define it, the definitions section [cited] omits it, the interpreting cases [cited or ‘none located after the searches listed’] have not resolved it, and no regulation [checked where] supplies content. Documented: text read; definitions checked; case search [terms, database, date]; regulatory check [where].” The record stays closed: “The term is resolved by [the definitions section § __ / the controlling decision cited]; the legislative record stays closed, and the research proceeds in [that source].” The failing shape is the one the self-check named: a completed ambiguity sentence sitting on an empty documentation list. An undocumented ambiguity claim is just an unread statute, and courts committed to plain meaning will treat it as exactly that.

Exercise 12.2 — The Credit-Line Drill

For the ADA, the identifier set the sources yield: Public Law number, Pub. L. No. 101-336; Statutes at Large, 104 Stat. 327; enactment year, 1990; bill number, S. 933; Congress, the 101st. The compiled-history check: compiled legislative histories for the ADA exist in [the compiled-history sources this chapter listed; record which one you located and what it collects]. The chapter printed these answers, and the exercise was producing them from the credit line and Congress.gov rather than from the page; the step that cannot be skipped, even with everything else right, is the compiled-history check, because on a public law this significant, someone has already assembled the record, and building your own first is the most expensive way to learn that.

Exercise 12.3 — Rank the Record

A passing ranking, with the ADA’s committee report as the anchor: (1) the committee report passage, H.R. Rep. No. 101-485, pt. [__], at [__] (1990), highest weight, because it is the drafting committee’s collective explanation and the document courts cite most; (2) the bill-version change, [described, with the versions compared], moderate weight, because deliberate language change is evidence of intent even though it speaks without explaining itself; (3) the floor statement, [speaker and citation], variable and here lowest, unless the speaker was a sponsor or floor manager, in which case it may climb past the bill version, and with the check the self-check required: whether the statement was actually spoken on the floor or merely inserted into the Record, because an inserted statement carries reduced weight. One sentence each; and if your order differed, the sentences are where the disagreement becomes visible and fixable.

Chapter 13

Exercise 13.1 — Binding or Guidance?

Three passing entries, compressed to their load-bearing fields: (1) 29 C.F.R. Part 1605, the framework itself; Authority Level: binding regulation, promulgated with the force of law. (2) The EEOC guidance document you located [the compliance-manual section or enforcement guidance on religious discrimination, cited as the agency publishes it]; Authority Level: persuasive guidance, influential in enforcement, weighed under Skidmore in proportion to its thoroughness, not binding on anyone. (3) The adjudication decision [a decision applying the framework to facts, located through the agency’s decisions database, or the substitute agency’s decision your instructor designated, logged identically]; Authority Level: binding within the agency’s own process, subject to judicial review, and the most detailed application of the framework available. The pass condition is the field itself, completed and unhedged on all three, because the entry with a blank authority level is the entry that later walks into an outline dressed as the rule.

Exercise 13.2 — The Vague-Term Drill

A passing run, on the term the regulation genuinely leaves open: the term, [for instance, what accommodation is “reasonable,” or when hardship becomes “undue”]; the practical definition, [what the agency’s materials say the term requires in operation, with the source cited, and note that research here would also surface whether courts have recently reshaped the standard, which the agency’s materials may lag]; the product that answered, [the compliance manual, an opinion letter, or an adjudication], with the mapping sentence: a what-does-this-mean-in-operation question pointed at interpretive guidance, exactly as this chapter’s question-to-product mapping said it would. The self-check’s trap door: if the CFR text itself answered you, the term was not vague enough, and the drill only works where the regulation runs out, because that running-out is the entry point to this whole chapter.

Exercise 13.3 — The Post-Loper Bright Check

A passing note, form modeled, findings bracketed: “Courts addressing [the guidance document] have [cited it under Skidmore for persuasive weight / declined to follow it on the ground that it exceeds the regulation / not addressed it, per the searches listed]. Searches: [citator run on the document where supported; terms-and-connectors search pairing the document’s name with the regulation, database, date].” The standard the self-check set: a reader should be able to place the document on the correct side of the line between legally required and the agency’s preference without reading anything else, and after Loper Bright the placement carries more consequence, because the agency’s own view of its statute no longer settles the question for a court.

Chapter 14

Exercise 14.1 — Three Postures, One File

A passing page, draftable from the printed scenario and this chapter’s parameters alone. Litigation (the neighbor has sued): Q1, what causes of action govern a complaint about drone flights over neighboring property in [State]? (exploratory shading into focusing); Q2, what are the elements of [the claim the complaint pleads]? (focusing); Q3, how do our client’s flight facts, altitude, frequency, camera use, compare to the decided cases? (application). First source: case law. Closure: controlling authority per issue, adverse authority identified, procedural requirements confirmed. Transactional (the business is being sold): Q1, what licenses or authorizations does this operation depend on? (focusing); Q2, what consents or approvals does transferring the operation require? (focusing); Q3, what compliance representations are market for a deal of this kind? (focusing, market flavor). First source: statutes, regulations, and form and practice materials. Closure: all requirements and risks identified, diligence complete. Compliance (the audit): Q1, what body of law governs operating a camera drone commercially? (exploratory shading into focusing); Q2, what state and local rules add obligations on top? (focusing, survey flavor); Q3, what do recent enforcement actions show the regulator actually prioritizes? (application, enforcement flavor). First source: the CFR and agency guidance. Closure: all obligations mapped, pending rulemaking checked. Nine questions, no two postures alike, and the page shows what the chapter argued: same facts, different parameters, because the posture sets them.

Exercise 14.2 — The Meta-Question, Cold

The form of a passing pair, on an unfamiliar context [illustrative]: Cold: “Default question type: focusing, since the area feels statute-driven. Where I start: the governing federal statute. What tells me I am done: every requirement for the client’s situation identified.” Checked, after fifteen minutes in a secondary source: “Revised: the area runs on a statute plus a dense agency layer of regulations and manuals, so the starting source is the secondary source’s map of that layer, not the bare statute; the closure criterion gains a piece, because [the area’s procedural posture] adds application questions the cold guess missed. What the check changed: [named specifically].” The honesty test is the last line. A check that changed nothing means either a remarkably good guess or a remarkably thin check, and the exercise asked you to say which.

Chapter 15

Exercise 15.1 — Three Prompts, Three Verdicts

Results vary by tool and by month, which is itself the finding to internalize, so the sample models the record, with illustrative verdicts shaped like the chapter’s own demonstration: Exploratory prompt [as put], verdict: [confirmed; the orientation and the authorities it named checked out against the primary sources, listed]. Focusing prompt [as put], verdict: [confirmed with a gap; the standard was stated accurately, but the response could not reliably catalogue the cases applying it]. Application prompt [as put], verdict: [partially confirmed or fabricated; the list mixed responsive authorities with plausibly related but off-point ones, and the verification record names which]. Behind each verdict, the record: every citation traced to a primary source read, or marked unfindable, which for a citation means fabricated until proven otherwise. And the self-check’s push holds: three clean confirmations means the prompts were easier than your drill. Tighten the application prompt until the tool has to reach, because watching where it reaches, and what it grabs when it does, is the lesson this exercise exists to deliver.

Exercise 15.2 — The Mega-Prompt Contrast

Two passing sentences, illustrative: [The mega-prompt answer makes dozens of discrete legal claims woven into a single narrative, and verifying it means separating every claim back out and tracing each to a source, which is the decomposition work the small questions had already done for free. A set of small, single-question answers makes one checkable claim apiece, each verifiable against one or two primary sources in minutes.] The self-check’s boundary matters: the sentences are about verifiability, not quality. The sprawling answer may read beautifully; fluency was never the problem, and the exercise is designed to make you feel the difference between an answer that reads well and an answer you can check.

Exercise 15.3 — The Verification-Cost Note

A passing note can be one line: “[The application prompt]: verifying the case list required running my own search anyway, so the tool added a verification burden without saving research time; wrong instrument for this question type.” That line is the signal the chapter told you to watch for, recorded where your future self will find it. And the closing discipline stands regardless of what the note says: nothing enters the outline unverified, because an answer you cannot trace to a source you have read is not an answer. It is a guess wearing a citation.

Chapter 16

Exercise 16.1 — The Clocked Cycle and the Five-Skill Audit

Step one, the skeleton of a passing clocked answer, modeled on the store-duty drill’s closed universe: Macro question, stated: is the store liable to the customer for the parking-lot assault? Broken into its research questions: did the store owe a duty; if so, what standard; was it breached; [the remaining elements named]. The provided authorities ranked: [your state’s highest-court decision] controls; [intermediate appellate decisions] bind subject to it; [out-of-state decisions in the set] persuade only. The ambiguity named: the duty question turns on [the point the provided authorities contest, for instance what makes third-party crime foreseeable enough to ground a duty]. Applied: [the controlling rule run on the scenario’s facts, with the analogous and distinguishable cases used as comparators]. The sufficiency call, committed: “the provided materials answer the duty question; they do not address [the named element], which is the gap, and [what would fill it] is the missing piece.” The call is present and unhedged; “probably enough” is not a call.

Step two, the marks, placed on that skeleton: ambiguity spotting, at the named foreseeability point; source ranking, in the ordered list; dispositive fact identification, inside the application, where the scenario’s facts met the comparator cases’ facts; gap identification, in the named missing element; closure, in the committed sufficiency call. Every mark points at a sentence that exists in the answer, and the honest alternative also passes: “I cannot place the dispositive-facts mark” is a finding and next week’s assignment, because the audit’s function is converting a skipped skill from an invisible habit into a visible target.

Exercise 16.2 — Break It Down, No Searching

A passing ten-minute breakdown, entirely from the provided list. Macro question: does the client’s termination for refusing Sunday work violate the state’s employment-discrimination statute? The research questions, each matched to its material: what does the statute prohibit, and does it reach religious practice? — the statute itself. What standard have the state’s courts applied under it, and what have employers been required to do? — the two intermediate appellate decisions. How do her facts compare to the decided applications? — the same two decisions, read for their facts. What have other jurisdictions done where the home authorities run out? — the neighboring state’s decision. What is the scholarly account of the area, useful for orientation and pathways but never for the rule? — the article. The ranking for the state’s trial court: the statute, binding law to be applied; the two intermediate appellate decisions, binding as decisions of a higher court in the same system; the neighboring state’s supreme court decision, persuasive; the law review article, secondary and persuasive, at the bottom. If either of the bottom two surfaced as the answer to a controlling-rule question, the hierarchy of authority needs the reread the self-check prescribed, because on the exam that misranking is the whole wrong answer.

Exercise 17.3 — The Question-Form Outline

The five passing lines, on the surviving candidate [illustrative]: I. Framework: what role did agency guidance play in statutory interpretation before the change, and under what doctrine? II. Problem: what has the overruling decision unsettled about the weight courts owe that guidance now? III. Critique: why do the emerging lower-court answers fail to give regulated parties, agencies, or courts a workable rule? IV. Proposal: what framework should govern the persuasive weight of agency guidance, and why would it serve all three audiences better? Plus the interdisciplinary line: the public administration and political science literature on agency expertise and its institutional value, which lives in JSTOR’s political science journals and Public Administration Review. Each heading is answerable, which means each section can be finished, and finished sections are the whole trick. A subject area is a shelf. This is a paper, started.
