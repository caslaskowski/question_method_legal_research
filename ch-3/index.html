Chapter 3

The Question Method of Legal Research

Legal research is a moving series of questions that continues until you find the answer you need or must stop for practical reasons. Collecting sources is easy; a pile of printouts is not research. The skill is knowing what you need at every stage.

Good researchers pause and ask themselves things like: What type of question am I asking now? Is this the right level of inquiry for where I am? What source can best answer it? To a beginner, those pauses feel like interruptions. That is because it is not yet instinctive as it is for experts, and so it feels like an extra step.

The Question Method builds the those pauses into the process: craft a question, research the answer, synthesize the answer into your writing notes, and craft the next question. With a well-crafted question in hand, you will find where to look faster, understand what you find more clearly, spot gaps and ambiguities sooner, and know with some confidence when your research is actually done.

Learning Objectives

  • Articulate the Question Method and distinguish it from passive, collection-based research
  • Draft precise, well-scoped research questions at each stage of inquiry
  • Identify the type of question being asked and select the source best suited to answer it
  • Distinguish a research map, a research log, and an annotated research outline, and explain where each belongs in the research process
  • Use a research log to document each cycle of research, recording the research question, method, and findings
  • Synthesize research findings into an annotated outline
  • Recognize analytical closure and practical closure, and use the research
  • Explain how the Question Method integrates research and writing

The Role of Questions in Legal Research, Analysis, and Writing #

Three terms carry this chapter and everything after it, and if you arrived here without the Introduction, here they are in brief. The legal issue is the problem the law must resolve, the reason the file landed on your desk. The macro question is that issue framed as the single big question your finished document exists to answer: the question presented in a memo, the issue on appeal in a brief. A research question is the micro question you ask and answer along the way, one of the many subquestions whose answers, assembled, resolve the macro question, and it is what this book always means by the term. You never research the macro question directly; held alone, it makes every passage look relevant and buries you. You write it down where you can see it, hold exactly one research question at a time, and let each answer hand you the next. The macro question is the destination; the research questions are the steps. (The Introduction develops this vocabulary in full.)

Research and writing are one iterative process, but early-career researchers tend to split them in two. Research becomes a prewriting phase where you gather everything that might conceivably be useful. Writing becomes a later phase where you sift through the pile. That split reliably produces three problems: you feel overwhelmed about where to begin analyzing the material; you struggle to organize the research and structure the argument; and you discover major gaps too close to the deadline to fix them comfortably.

The Question Method closes that split. Each answer you synthesize becomes a heading in your annotated outline, and findings become the bullets under it. You have started writing and organized your research in the same motion, and you can move to the next question knowing that nothing you found has slipped through the cracks.

Questions are working tools in legal analysis. As you move through a project, asking questions lets you take stock of what you have learned, see where the gaps are, and fold new information into the argument you are building.

The Five Steps and Intermittent Checkpoints #

Step 1: Write your research question. State the one thing you need to know right now as a question, in terms that reflect what you currently understand. This is a research question in the Introduction’s vocabulary: a micro question, not the macro question your document will answer. Be specific. A vague question gives you vague results. Your research map, or the answer you just found, will usually supply the question.

Step 2: Research to answer it. Choose the source most likely to answer the type of question you are asking. Search. Read. Track.

Step 3: Write down what you found. In your research log, record your research question, the method you used to attempt to answer it, the source you consulted, what it told you, and your next research question. Add the synthesized answer to your annotated outline.

Step 4: Identify your next question. Based on what you found, what do you still need to know? Write it down.

Step 5: Repeat. Cycle through Steps 1 through 4 until no substantive questions remain, and add any new questions the source raised to your research map.

Checkpoint, Reflect and Evaluate: Before you close out your research, review your work. Are your sources current? Authoritative? From the right jurisdiction? Did your sequence of questions capture the full scope of the issue? Build it as a habit into every cycle, then perform it formally at the end, rather than saving it for a separate phase.

Done Usually Means Done for Now #

Step 5 tells you to keep cycling until no substantive question remains, and that is right. It is worth being honest, though, about what “no more questions” usually means in practice. It means no more questions for now. When you take your notes and start turning them into a full draft, you will very often find gaps you could not have seen until you tried to write the argument out. A connection that felt solid turns out to need a case you do not have. An element you were sure you understood raises a sub-question the moment you have to explain it on the page. None of that means your research failed. Research and writing are cyclical, and they feed each other.

So the goal is not to track down every authority you might conceivably need before you write a single word. Chasing that kind of completeness is slower rather than faster, and it runs up a bill your client should not have to pay. The efficient move is to stop once you believe you have most of what you need, start writing, and let the act of drafting show you which gaps are actually worth closing. Stopping for now is a real research decision, not a loss of nerve, even when it leaves you with the nagging sense that there might be a little more out there.

Why This Method Works: Making Your Thinking Visible #

The blank page is paralyzing because it asks you to produce something out of nothing. The Question Method replaces it with a prompt. At every stage, you are answering a specific question, and each cycle leaves behind a small, discrete piece of writing. Those pieces pile up in your research log and annotated outline, so by the time you sit down to “write,” your outline is a first draft hiding under bullet points. You will then just be organizing and refining what you have already sketched out.

There is a name for the habit the method trains: metacognition, which means thinking about your own thinking. It is the ability to monitor what you know, recognize what you do not know, evaluate whether your current approach is working, and adjust when it is not. Expert legal researchers are metacognitive all the way down. They keep asking themselves: Do I understand this well enough to move on? Is this source actually answering my question? Have I missed something? Each step of the Question Method cycle corresponds to a metacognitive act.

The cycle, and the question each step asks of you

Four steps turn. The checkpoint sits in the middle because you can stop and take it at any point. Select each one.

  • 1. Write the question | self-monitoring. What do I currently need to know? You cannot write the question without noticing the edge of your own knowledge. That noticing is the skill.
  • 2. Research to answer it | strategic selection. What is the best way to find this out? Naming the type of question turns a guess about where to look into a choice.
  • 3. Write down what you found | self-assessment. Did I find what I needed? If you cannot state the answer in your own words, you have not found it yet.
  • 4. Identify the next question | self-regulation. Where should I go next? The answer you just wrote down tells you. Then the cycle turns again.
  • Checkpoint | at any point. Is my overall approach working? This is self-evaluation, and it belongs to no single step. Pause whenever the research feels off, and always before you close out. Are your sources current and authoritative? Are they pointed at the question the project is really asking?

Each step asks you to make a judgment, not just perform a task. You are training yourself to think like a researcher.

Why Writing It Down Matters #

Writing forces you to be honest about whether you actually understand something. It is easy to read a case and feel like you grasp the rule. It is much harder to state that rule in your own words, in a single sentence, and connect it to the question you asked. If you cannot, you have found a gap, and finding it now beats finding it in the final hours before a major filing deadline. Writing as you go also protects you from collecting a stack of sources and then realizing you no longer remember why each one mattered.

Three Tools, One Process #

The method uses three tools that work together. The Research Map is your plan. It lists the questions your writing must answer, each with leads to the sources that might answer it, organized by the logic of your analysis. The research log is your record of the research process. It captures every question you asked, each method you used, all the sources you consulted, and every answer you found, in the order you found them. The Annotated Outline is your record of the research product. It organizes your verified findings by legal issue, so each section lines up with an element, a factor, or a sub-question in your analysis. As you finish research log entries, you move the synthesized findings into the right section of the outline and mark the question answered on your map. One way to keep them straight: the research map tells you where you are going, the research log tells you where you have been, and the annotated outline tells you what you know.

Follow a single question through all three. It is born on the map, often handed to you by a secondary source or by the answer to an earlier question. You work it in the log, sometimes over several entries if the first source only partially answers it. Once you have a verified answer from primary authority, that answer, not the question, goes into the outline, and the question is marked answered on the map. The map holds questions; the outline holds answers.

The Research Map #

A research map is a list of the questions you need to answer in your writing to reach a conclusion about how the law applies to your client. You build it over time from the sources you encounter. Questions land on the map from the assignment and the file, from the answers to earlier questions, from sources that answer more than you asked (an encyclopedia entry or a case will often point to several later questions and cite the authority for each), and from your own curiosity about the facts. Next to each question, note any leads: citations you think might answer it, recorded before you have read them.

Add questions as you discover them, but file each one where it belongs in the analysis, so a sub-question sits under the question it narrows. Mark each question open, answered (with a pointer to its place in the outline), or parked, meaning done for now on purpose. Two kinds of questions stay off the map. Questions about your client’s facts go on a separate fact-gathering list, because no database can answer them. And the orientation question you ask just to learn an area’s vocabulary stays in the log, because the map holds only the questions your writing must answer. When you finish one line of reasoning, the map tells you which question to take up next.

The Research Log #

A research log captures your process and your results in one place, so you can put the work down and reenter it later without starting over. In law school, research arrives in stable chunks: you get the assignment, you do it, you turn it in. Practice is not like that. You will be pulled off a project midstream for weeks or months, and when you come back, the log is what tells you what you asked, what you found, and what you were about to ask next. Be kind to your future self, and build the habit of logging your efforts.

One more thing before you build the habit: the entry template below is deliberately overly explicit. It is a scaffold for learning, and seven fields is more than most working researchers keep forever. There is no single right format for a research log. The right format is whatever gets your future self back into the research fastest, and that is personal. Start with the full scaffold, notice which fields are doing real work for you, and adjust until the log fits your process. What cannot be cut is the function: process and results, captured well enough that you can leave the work and return to it cold.

The research log opens with your macro question, written once at the top of your document so you can always check that you have not drifted onto a tangent, and then entries that capture each step in your research with seven fields:

  • Research Question. Write your current question here.
  • Question Type. Definitional, Exploratory, Focusing, or Application (each defined in Types of Questions, below).
  • Method. The research method you used to locate the source you used to answer the question. E.g., secondary source, annotated code, citator, key numbers, search, lead from the map, etc.
  • Findings. The key information, holding, rule, or definition, in your own words.
  • Source Consulted. Citation.
  • Next Question. What do you still need to know?
  • Notes. Anything else: related sources to check, flags, connections to other questions.

Filling in the Question Type field is not busywork. As you will see, naming the type forces the diagnostic step that tells you which source to consult and roughly where you are in the arc of the research process. The Findings field does similar work if you let it. Begin each entry by saying whether the source fully answers your question, partially answers it, or only redirects you, and then record what it said. That one clause makes you say out loud whether you have an actual answer or only a lead, which is the difference between real progress and the comfortable illusion of it.

The Method field matters most when you return to the project after an extended period. Research in practice often has gaps large enough between efforts on a case that memory alone is not enough. The Method field tells you which searches you already ran, including the ones that found nothing, so you do not repeat them, and it lets you answer a supervisor who asks how you found a case. Chapter 9’s closing-out protocol asks you to document what you searched; a log with good Method entries has already done most of that work.

Here is what a completed entry looks like on the page, using a definitional question you will meet again in the Types of Questions section below: what is the federal definition of “organic”?

Field Entry
Research Question What is the federal definition of “organic”?
Question Type Definitional
Method Found in Annotated United States Code (Westlaw) under general
Findings Partially answers the question. The statute hands definitional authority for the organic program to the USDA; the authoritative definition lives in the agency’s regulation, not the statute.

Reverse Engineering the Question: A Demonstration #

Legal arguments are built by answering questions. Take a real paragraph from a federal opinion and turn each sentence back into the research question it answers.

Four sentences, four questions

In the vast majority of legal malpractice cases, the plaintiff was a client of the defendant-attorney, and the direct attorney-client relationship establishes the attorney’s professional duties to the client. Pelham v. Griesheimer, 92 Ill. 2d 13, 440 N.E.2d 96, 99 (Ill. 1982). There is a small corner of legal malpractice law, however, in which Illinois recognizes that attorneys owe professional duties to persons who are not their clients. In Pelham, the Illinois Supreme Court held that an attorney may owe a duty to a third party when “the primary purpose and intent of the attorney-client relationship itself was to benefit or influence the third party.” Id. at 100. Examples of third parties to whom attorneys owe a duty include third-party beneficiaries of wills, third-party beneficiaries of wrongful death actions, and third-party recipients of formal opinion letters. See In re Estate of Powell, 12 N.E.3d at 21; Geaslen v. Berkson, Gorov & Levin, Ltd. (Geaslen II), 155 Ill. 2d 223, 613 N.E.2d 702, 704 (Ill. 1993); McLane v. Russell, 131 Ill. 2d 509, 546 N.E.2d 499, 504 (Ill. 1989).

Oakland Police & Fire Retirement System v. Mayer Brown, LLP, 861 F.3d 644, 649–50 (7th Cir. 2017).

Notice that for several of these questions the answer comes from a single source, Pelham v. Griesheimer. That is common for settled points of law: elements, familiar principles, conclusions nobody is fighting about. The last sentence is different. It carries multiple citations because the answer requires a survey of different scenarios. When you see one point of law supported by a string of citations, that usually signals a survey question answered by looking across many cases.

Making the questions explicit shows you why some propositions might only need a single citation while others need several. Here we worked backward from a finished paragraph. When you research, you work forward toward one. The paragraph as a whole serves the macro question of the appeal. Each sentence answers a single research question. Assembled, the small answers resolve the big one, and every piece of legal writing you produce has this structure under its skin.

Finally, those four questions, in order, are a research map. The paragraph is the annotated outline written out in full sentences, each question replaced by its answer and its citation.

Types of Questions #

Naming the type of question you are asking is the diagnostic move that makes everything else more efficient. Once you know the type, you know where to look, which source will answer it best, and roughly how far into the cycle you are.

Definitional Questions #

Definitional questions ask what a word, phrase, or concept means. For your own orientation, a legal dictionary usually does the job. When the definition is load-bearing for your argument, you need primary authority: the statute’s definitions section, case law construing the term, or agency regulations. The diagnostic question is whether you need a working definition or an authoritative one. “What is the federal definition of organic?” leads you to the statute, which hands definitional authority to the USDA, so your next question becomes “What is the USDA’s definition of organic?” and you turn to 7 C.F.R. Part 205.

Exploratory Questions #

Exploratory questions ask what the legal landscape of an area looks like. There are three kinds, and each points to a slightly different source.

  • No-clue questions come up when you have no familiarity with the area at all. You do not know the relevant terms, the key cases, or even whether the issue is mostly statutory or mostly common law.
  • Foundational questions come up when you have a general sense of the area but need to understand the general structure of the law: the elements of a claim, the factors in a test, the major debates, the terms of art
  • Survey questions come up when you need to know how multiple jurisdictions have handled the same issue.

Focusing Questions #

Focusing questions ask what the specific legal rule governing your issue is, moving you from the general landscape to the particular standard, test, or sub-rule. This is usually where you cross over from secondary to primary authority. If you have been using secondary sources to get oriented, a focusing question pushes you to go find the actual statute, regulation, or case that states the rule. Focusing questions also breed sub-rules. “What is the standard for reasonable accommodation under the ADA?” leads to “What counts as undue hardship under that standard?”, which is itself a focusing question one level deeper. Every layer of the rule you uncover can expose another layer that needs its own question.

Application Questions #

Application questionsask how the rule works once you apply it to specific facts. Like exploratory questions, there are multiple kinds.

  • Fact-finding application questions go looking for cases with facts like your client’s, where the rule was applied in analogous circumstances.
  • Constructive application questions become necessary when no articulated rule yet governs your question, so the applicable rule itself has to be built from several sources, reasoning by analogy from related contexts. When the rule is articulated but no decision applies it to facts like yours, you are still fact-finding: gather the closest decided applications and argue the analogy.

The four types are not a strict sequence. You might circle back to an exploratory question when a focusing question turns up an unfamiliar doctrine. You might answer a definitional question mid-stream when a regulation throws an unexpected technical term at you. Treat the taxonomy as a diagnostic, not a checklist. Its value is helping you recognize, at any moment, what kind of inquiry you are actually in, and therefore where the answer is likely to live. A real case shows how unceremoniously the types trade places.

The Types in Motion: Nix v. Hedden

In Nix v. Hedden, 149 U.S. 304 (1893), a produce importer sued the collector of the Port of New York to recover import duties paid on tomatoes. The Tariff Act of 1883 taxed imported “vegetables” and admitted “fruit” free, and the importer’s argument was that, botanically, a tomato is a fruit. The threshold question is: which tariff category covers a tomato? That turns on a definitional question: how does the statute define “fruit” and “vegetable”? And here the definitional question comes back empty. The Act uses both words and defines neither. The next question is a focusing question, one level up: how have courts defined the terms when applying the Act? The Supreme Court answered that absent some contrary indication, words in the Tariff Act carry their ordinary meaning in common speech, not their botanical or scientific meaning, and dictionaries may aid the court’s memory and understanding but do not control. Now we can ask a closing application question: in ordinary speech, is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable? The Court held, unanimously, that tomatoes are vegetables, served at dinner in, with, or after the soup rather than as dessert, and the duty stood. The Court cited Robertson v. Salomon, 130 U.S. 412 (1889)(which found that beans are vegetables, not seeds, for the purposes of tariffs).

Reflection Point. A botanist, a chef, and the Tariff Act of 1883 each answer “is a tomato a fruit?” differently. What does that tell you about where a legal definition’s authority comes from? (A short answer appears in Appendix I.)

Name the type

  • What does “curtilage” mean? Definitional
    • It asks what a word means. A working definition is enough to keep researching.
  • How does the law treat security deposits when a tenant moves out? Exploratory
    • No rule is named and no standard is presupposed. It asks what the landscape looks like.
  • What is the test for when a school official may search a student’s backpack? Focusing
    • It asks for the specific governing rule. The word “test” is the tell.
  • Is a dog owner liable when the dog bites a guest who was teasing it? Application
    • Specific facts in the question (a bite, a guest, teasing) are the tell. It asks how a rule has operated on facts like these.
  • How do the fifty states handle recording a phone call? Exploratory
    • The survey kind of exploratory question: how many jurisdictions do what.
  • What are the elements of adverse possession in Michigan? Focusing
    • “The elements,” in a named jurisdiction: the governing rule itself.
  • What does “employee” mean under this state’s wage statute? Definitional
    • Still a what-does-it-mean question. “Under this statute” changes where you look (the statute’s definitions section, not a dictionary). It does not change the type.
  • Have courts treated taping a notice to the door as “delivery” under a three-day notice statute? Application
    • A rule exists. The question is how courts have applied it to facts like tape and a door.

Beginning Your Research: A Practical Start Protocol #

Questions are a key part of the research process. Before you get to ask them, it is important that you thoughtfully prepare and plan your efforts. Every research project, simple or complex, begins the same way. Spending a few minutes up front to clarify the assignment and take stock of what you already know pays off enormously later, because it sets the type of your very first question, which sets the source you reach for first.

Clarify the Assignment #

Before you open a database, make sure you understand what you are being asked to find. What is the scope? What will count as doing the job well? What are the deadlines, and what resources do you have? If the assignment comes from a supervisor, pin down the jurisdiction, the format they want (a quick email, a formal memorandum, a list of citations), and the depth of research expected. “Do some quick research on this” and “give me a comprehensive memo” are very different requests. This is also where the macro question gets written down. Capture the big question the finished document must answer, in one sentence, at the top of your research log. Also, make sure to note anything you are instructed to avoid. Managing attorneys will often split research tasks among a team of associates, and if you are not tracking the scope of your assignment, you are likely to waste time researching questions assigned to others.

Review the File #

Before you search, go through everything you already have. What do the documents tell you about the facts, the timeline, and the parties? It will not always be clear which facts are dispositive for your case. So capture the ones that are helpful to you now. You can revisit the case again as answers make it clear other facts matter. The legal terms you run across (the names of statutes, the titles of the parties, the types of claims) will seed your search terms and will often save you a no-clue exploratory step entirely.

Reflect on Your Current Understanding #

Take a moment to be honest about what you already know. How familiar are you with this area? Do you know the relevant terms and claims? Are you an expert who just needs to confirm a couple of points, or someone meeting this area for the first time? That honest self-diagnosis decides what type your first question should be, and getting it right keeps you from wasting time at the wrong level of generality.

Prepare Your Research Strategy #

With that assessment in hand, draft your first question. What is the very first thing you need to know? What in the file seems relevant to it? Where might that information live? If you are walking into unfamiliar territory, your first question should be exploratory. If you already have a handle on the landscape and just need a specific rule, it might be focusing. One move can come before even that choice. If you do not yet have the vocabulary the area uses, the terms of art you would need just to search, then your true first step is a plain-language or general search whose only job is to surface those terms. A term of art belongs in a question only after the file supplied it or an earlier step turned it up; it is something your research delivers, never something you assume at the outset. Writing that first question down is itself the first cycle of the method.

When Is Your Research Done? Research Closure #

Earlier in this chapter we saw that done usually means done for now. Here is how to make that judgment formally. Research closure is the professional judgment that your research is good enough for its purpose. It is a conclusion you can support with specific, documentable indicators, never just a feeling.

Analytical closure means your research is substantively complete. You have identified the governing rule. You can explain how it applies to your facts. You have found the counterarguments and dealt with them. Your annotated outline covers the full scope of the issue. More research at this point would polish your writing but would not change your analysis.

Practical closure means an external constraint forces you to stop: a budget cap, a client instruction, a supervising attorney’s direction. Practical closure is a fact of professional life. When it arrives before analytical closure, your job is to do three things: document where you stopped and what remains unresearched in your research log; tell your supervisor or client about the limitation; and flag any significant gaps that could affect the analysis.

Practical closures are intentional, not a result of poor planning. Running out of time before a deadline is not a practical closure; it is potential malpractice

One distinction keeps the judgment honest: running out of new sources is not closure. Closure arrives when your outline fully accounts for the legal question and more research would only change your wording rather than your analysis, and it gets finished off with a short ritual, a last sweep of the major sources, a citation update, and a check of the annotated outline for gaps. Chapter 9 builds that finish into a full closing-out protocol.

The method helps with both kinds of closure. Because each cycle is a recorded checkpoint—a question asked, a source consulted, an answer synthesized—you always know where you are. If you reach analytical closure, your research map confirms it by showing no open questions, your annotated outline shows an answer for each, and the judgment is defensible instead of intuitive, because you can point to a research log in which every question has been answered. If practical closure comes first, the questions still open on your map tell you exactly what is left.

The Question Method in Action: A Full Demonstration #

This demonstration runs the Question Method through a problem about student speech and the First Amendment. Read it for the thinking that drives the process, not just the sources consulted but the decisions made at each stage. One decision shapes the whole demonstration: a secondary source appears exactly once, at the start, to orient you and hand you a map. Every answer after that comes from primary authority.

The Assignment #

A supervising attorney asks: “Can a public high school principal remove articles he doesn’t like from the school-run student newspaper without violating the First Amendment?” You are a first-year law student. You know the First Amendment protects free speech, but you have never studied how it works inside schools.

Prepare to Research #

Before drafting a question, you orient yourself. This is a constitutional issue (First Amendment) in a specific setting (a public school, a student newspaper). You do not know much about this area. You review the file and note that the newspaper is produced as part of a journalism class, that the principal removed several articles before publication, and that the articles do not seem to involve profanity, threats, or illegal activity. There is more detail about the article topics and the timing of the removal, but you are not yet sure which details would bear on the legal question, so you leave them where they are. That self-diagnosis tells you something useful: your first question has to be exploratory. You are not ready to ask what the legal standard is, because you do not yet know which standard governs.

Q1: Explore the Legal Landscape #

Question (Exploratory, No-Clue): How has First Amendment free speech protection been applied to students in school environments?

You search the secondary sources in your database for “free speech” and students and schools. In your results, you find a legal encyclopedia entry titled “Students, Constitutional Rights.” The entry states that in Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), the Supreme Court held that students still have free speech rights while at school, and that those rights may be restricted only if the expression materially and substantially disrupts school operations. The entry then surveys the cases marking where that standard does and does not reach. Two matter here. Bethel School District No. 403 v. Fraser, 478 U.S. 675 (1986), permitted a school to discipline lewd and vulgar speech that conflicted with the school’s basic educational mission. And Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988), governs school-sponsored publications, with the entry offering the example of a newspaper produced in a journalism class, and permits educators to regulate them when the regulation is tied to “legitimate pedagogical concerns,” a phrase the entry uses but does not define.

Notice how much this single encyclopedia entry hands you beyond the question you are holding. Hazelwood is plainly the branch your facts sit on, and the temptation is to jump straight there. Resist it. The entry told you Tinker is the foundation the later cases build on, and a foundation you skip is a gap you will trip over the moment a later case assumes it. So you do two things at once: you keep one question open, and you map the routes you are not yet taking.

Mapping adding the rest to your research map logging the rest as later questions, with their citations noted as leads, before you can answer any of them. Reading Tinker is clearly next. Hazelwood goes on the map too, but it is not next, because at least one question sits between the first question and the research question Hazelwood will puportedly answer. At least one does: the entry’s description suggests Hazelwood builds on Fraser, so Fraser will need a read after Tinker. You note that in the log.

Example of what our research log entries look like at this stage:

Cycle 1 Research Question: How has First Amendment free speech protection been applied to students in school environments? Question Type: Exploratory (No-Clue) Method: Searched the secondary sources in my database for “free speech” and students and schools; opened the encyclopedia entry. Findings: Tinker starts the student free speech case law: rights survive the schoolhouse gate, and restriction requires material and substantial disruption. Later cases set context-specific standards. Hazelwood governs school-sponsored publications like a curricular newspaper and ties regulation to “legitimate pedagogical concerns.” Fully answers the question, for orientation. It also identifies which branch of the doctrine governs our facts. Source Consulted: Legal encyclopedia entry, “Students, Constitutional Rights” [citation] (Note: The entry is a fictitional one for this example) Next Question: What are the bounds of student free speech rights in school? Notes: Tinker is the foundational case. Our newspaper is produced in a journalism class, so the school-sponsored branch (Hazelwood) is the thread to pull, but Hazelwood seems to build on Fraser, so Fraser gets a read after Tinker. The entry used “legitimate pedagogical concerns” without defining it; that is a future definitional question.

Those later questions go on your research map, which you keep as a separate document from the log. Here is the map at this stage, with the leads the entry gave you. Your annotated outline is still empty. The exploratory answer does not make it into the outline because the answer came from a secondary source, not a primary one. As you answer each map question from primary authority, its synthesized answer moves into the outline and the question is marked answered.

Note that the future questions are jotted down simply so they can be revisited later. This can be on a separate document, to the side. You can also decide to track them in your annotated outline instead. An example of the annotated outline at this stage, with the other questions, is below. The exploratory answer does not make it into the outline because the answer came from a secondary source, not a primary one. As you progress, you’ll replace the questions with your synthesized answers.

  1. What are the bounds of student free speech rights in school? Leads: Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503 (1969)
  2. How do courts balance a student’s free speech rights against the school’s basic educational mission? Leads: Bethel School District No. 403 v. Fraser, 478 U.S. 675 (1986)
  3. What free speech protection applies to a school-sponsored student publication? Leads: Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260 (1988)
  4. What is considered a “legitimate pedagogical concern”? Leads: none yet

One more discipline before you move. One question per question mark. You might be tempted to draft the next question as “What is the Tinker standard, and does it apply to school publications?” That is two research questions sharing a sentence, one about the content of a rule and one about its reach, and each needs its own answer, its own source, and its own log entry. A skilled researcher can hold a bundle like that in her head and unpack it on the fly. You are building the judgment that makes bundling safe later. For now, split them every time.

Q2: Read the Foundation #

Question (Focusing): What are the bounds of student free speech rights in school?

You pull up Tinker and find that the Supreme Court held that students do not shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech at the schoolhouse gate, and that a school may restrict student expression only on a showing that the expression would materially and substantially interfere with the operation of the school or collide with the rights of others. An undifferentiated fear of disturbance is not enough.

Scope the answer to the question and stop. What you also record in your notes is what Tinker was not: the case involved students’ own expression, black armbands worn in protest, not anything the school itself published or sponsored. The rule you just verified is the baseline, and Tinker itself says student rights operate “in light of the special characteristics of the school environment.” That is exactly the balance your mapped Fraser question asks about, so the answer and the map point the same direction. You complete entry 2, , move its answer into your outline, mark the question answered on your map, and Fraser becomes entry 3.

Q3: Clear a Mapped Question #

Question (Focusing): How do courts balance a student’s free speech rights against the school’s basic educational mission?

Fraser answers in a paragraph. Reading the opinion, the constitutional rights of students in public school are not automatically coextensive with the rights of adults in other settings, and a school may discipline lewd, indecent, or vulgar speech (there, a sexually suggestive assembly speech) without any showing of Tinker disruption, because a school may insist that speech be consistent with its basic educational mission.

This cycle was short. Write it down anyway. Fast questions are still questions, and the entry takes two minutes. The Hazelwood question becomes entry 4.

Q4: Find the Rule for Your Setting #

Question (Focusing): What free speech protection applies to a school-sponsored student publication?

You read Hazelwood. The Court held that a curricular student newspaper is not a public forum unless school officials have opened it, by policy or by practice, for indiscriminate use by the general public or some segment of it. Absent that, educators may exercise editorial control over the style and content of student speech in school-sponsored expressive activities so long as their actions are reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. The Court distinguished Tinker: whether a school must tolerate particular student speech is a different question from whether it must affirmatively promote it.

That is the governing rule, and it arrives dense with working parts you cannot yet define: “school-sponsored,” a “public forum” opened “by policy or by practice,” “legitimate pedagogical concerns,” “reasonably related.” Each undefined term is a research question. You add them to the research map as sub-questions under the Hazelwood question, ordered to follow the rule’s own structure now that you know it, and then you take them one at a time.

Draft the next question before you read it

You have the rule, and the rule has working parts you cannot define. Write the single question you would log next, and name its type.

The demonstration takes the threshold term first: What makes a student publication “school-sponsored” under Hazelwood? (Definitional.) It comes first because every other part of the rule applies only if the publication is school-sponsored. If you chose “legitimate pedagogical concern,” you chose a real question in the wrong order: the rule’s own structure tells you which term is the gate. If your question mentioned your client’s newspaper, look again at what a database can and cannot answer.

Q5: Define the Threshold Term #

Question (Definitional): What makes a student publication “school-sponsored” under Hazelwood?

Stay inside the opinion, because it defines its own term. School-sponsored expressive activities are those that students, parents, and members of the public might reasonably perceive to bear the imprimatur of the school. They may fairly be characterized as part of the curriculum, whether or not they occur in a traditional classroom setting, so long as they are supervised by faculty and designed to impart particular knowledge or skills to student participants and audiences.

A Research Pause: Write the Application into the Outline #

Now be careful about a move that feels natural and is easy to get wrong. The next thing your brain wants to ask is: “Is our newspaper school-sponsored?” That is not a research question. No database can answer it, because no database has your file. An Application question in research asks how courts have applied a rule to facts, which sends you looking for decided cases. Measuring the rule against your own facts is a writing step, part of the analysis you will eventually draft.

You do not have to hold that analysis in your head until the memo, though. Take a research pause: stop the research, open the annotated outline, and write the application where it belongs, as a bracketed note under the element it addresses. Here is the outline after this pause. Each question you have answered now appears as your paraphrase of the verified law, with its citation, and the application notes sit in brackets so you can tell your analysis from your authority at a glance. The two questions still open stay on the map, not in the outline.

  1. Students retain free speech rights at school; restriction requires a showing of material and substantial disruption. Tinker (1969). [Personal-expression branch. Not our setting, but the baseline the later cases adjust.]
  2. Student rights are not coextensive with adults’ rights; a school may bar lewd or vulgar speech inconsistent with its educational mission. Fraser (1986).
  3. Educators may regulate the style and content of school-sponsored student expression when the regulation is reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns; a curricular paper is not a public forum unless opened by policy or practice. Hazelwood (1988).
  4. A publication is “school-sponsored” if it is one the public might reasonably perceive to bear the school’s imprimatur: faculty-supervised, curricular, designed to teach. Hazelwood. [Our facts: produced in a journalism class, supervised by a faculty advisor, published under the school’s name. Element almost certainly met; confirm no contrary facts in the file.]

Then resume. The map holds the questions still open. The log holds your questions and answers about the law. The outline is where the law meets your file. Keeping those three documents doing their separate jobs is most of what staying organized means in practice. The legitimate pedagogical concern question on your map is next.

Q6: Define the Operative Standard #

Question (Definitional): What is a “legitimate pedagogical concern” under Hazelwood?

The opinion illustrates more than it defines. Educators may act to ensure that participants learn whatever the activity is designed to teach, that readers are not exposed to material inappropriate for their maturity, and that the views of an individual speaker are not erroneously attributed to the school. A school may also decline to sponsor speech that is ungrammatical, poorly written, inadequately researched, biased, vulgar, or unsuitable for immature audiences. In Hazelwood itself, protecting the privacy of pregnant students described in one article, the fairness owed a parent criticized in another, and younger readers’ exposure to frank material all qualified.

A short pause at the outline. Nothing in the file’s account of the removal—articles the principal did not like, contained no profanity, threats, or illegality—maps onto any of those categories, and you note that. The note tells you what to research next. If the school’s stated reasons may fall outside the recognized concerns, you need the outer edge of the standard: the cases schools lost.

Q7: Find the Outer Limits #

Question (Application, Fact-Finding): In what circumstances have courts found that a school’s removal of a student newspaper article was not reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns?

Look at the shape of that question. It is an Application question in its research form. It asks how the rule has operated on facts in decided cases, and it sends you to case law, not to your file. You run searches and use the citator to trace how Hazelwood has been treated since 1988, looking specifically for negative or distinguishing treatment, the cases where the school lost. Because your map had no lead for this question, record that route in your log’s Method field. You find that the standard, permissive as it is, does not authorize viewpoint-based censorship or restrictions unconnected to any legitimate educational purpose. Dean v. Utica Community Schools, 345 F. Supp. 2d 799 (E.D. Mich. 2004), is the kind of case this search is built to surface: a federal court held that the student newspaper there had, by policy and practice, become a limited public forum, taking it outside Hazelwood’s deferential standard, and that even measured against Hazelwood, the principal’s removal of a well-reported student article about a lawsuit against the district could not stand.

Checkpoint, the false hit. One search also returns Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, 597 U.S. 507 (2022), a case about a coach’s personal prayer after football games. That case applies a different First Amendment doctrine entirely and does not answer the student-publications question. This is a classic research error, finding a case in the right area that decides the wrong issue. Recording your legal issue and research questions precisely is what allows you to quickly dismiss it without losing time analyzing it

Q8: Take Up the Question Dean Surfaced #

Question (Focusing): When is a school-sponsored student publication a public forum rather than a nonpublic forum?

Reading Dean, you learn school facilities are nonpublic forums unless school authorities have, by policy or by practice, opened them for indiscriminate use by the general public or some segment of it. Dean applied that inquiry to a student paper by examining both the written policy and the paper’s actual practice: who exercised control over content, whether students had made the publication decisions in fact, and how the paper had functioned over time.

When the Writing Step Leaves the Database #

Now the research requires a pause to uncover how our paper has actually been run. Who has decided what gets published? Has the advisor or the principal exercised control before this incident? Have contributions from outside the journalism class ever been accepted? The file does not say. And no research question will say either, because these are not questions about the law. They are questions about facts you do not have.

Facts you still need go on a task list, and this cycle ends with a call to the client. When the answers come back, they suggest the paper has been run as a nonpublic forum, so you continue researching within Hazelwood’s framework.

Q9: Fill a Gap During Drafting #

Question (Application, Fact-Finding, Late Gap Fill): Have courts recognized a legitimate pedagogical concern that justified removing student articles about cultural topics?

This one does not come up during the research phase at all. Later, drafting from the annotated outline, you notice that the removed articles are all about cultural holidays, events, and celebrations. That observation produces a new Application question, and it sends you back for one more targeted case law search, answered, like every question since Q1, from the cases themselves. This is normal and expected. The method treats gaps discovered while drafting as new research cycles rather than as failures of the initial research. Spot the gap, draft the question, add the question to your map, log it, run it.

What the Demonstration Shows #

A secondary source appeared exactly once, at Q1, and did the two things secondary sources are for: it oriented you, and it handed you a map. Everything after that was answered from primary authority: Tinker, Fraser, Hazelwood, Dean. Opinions are the law, and your verified paraphrase of them is what your writing will stand on. At no point did the researcher just type “student newspaper censorship First Amendment” into a search bar. That search would have returned thousands of results and no framework for sorting them.

An Application question in research asks how courts have applied the rule to facts, and it ends in case law. Applying the rule to your own facts is writing, and it lives in bracketed notes on the research outline until it becomes the analysis section of your memo. When that writing step revealed missing facts, the method did not stall. It produced a task list for the client.

The map filled up after one encyclopedia entry but kept expanding when new questions arose out of later stages of research. The log grew by one entry per cycle, in the order you worked. The outline held the answers as found in the relevant authority, most often primary authority, which becomes the Rule and Rule Explanation in most legal writing methods.

Watch also how fast some of the questions resolved. Some questions are so intuitive that you may not even notice you asked and answered one. Write them down anyway. The granularity is scaffolding, and with practice the compression comes on its own. One caution as the compression arrives: a question asked and answered quickly still has to be answered explicitly for the court. An element you satisfied silently is a gap on the page, and working through even the fast questions keeps those skips out of your draft, so you are not left hunting for holes to fill later.

For success in every class. The same discipline scores on a law school exam. Graders award points element by element, and the points most often lost are the ones that felt too obvious to write down. If the hypothetical asks whether the defendant committed first-degree murder and the victim is plainly dead, that element still belongs in your answer: one sentence stating that the victim died and the element is met. You saw it, you counted it as met, but if you did not write it, the point is lost. And every point stacks.

Common Pitfalls #

By far the most common mistake is collecting before thinking. It is easy to run search after search and let the sources pile up, and then to look down and realize you are holding a stack of material with no memory of why half of it mattered. Synthesize as you go, writing down what you learn, and you will find yourself better prepared for your full drafts.

A quieter version of the same problem is asking the wrong question. Jumping ahead to an element or factor before you laid the groundwork can leave you with gaps that not only have you backtracking in your research, but also create confusion as you try to grasp the legal argument.

Reflection Point. How might the research have been affected if the researcher had jumped straight to reading Hazelwood before reading Tinker and Fraser? (A short answer appears in Appendix I.)

Another trap is failing to capture the rules and rule explanations you found from your research in your annotated outline, instead capturing only how the rule applies to your facts. In so doing, you are missing key elements needed for your future drafts and making more work for yourself down the road.

Tangents are the last trap. When research turns up sub-issues, it is tempting to chase every tangent the second it appears. The method holds that impulse in check by asking you to record a tangential question on your research map rather than running after it now, keeping your current question in focus until it is answered. Tangents feel urgent because most of them really do bear on the macro question. That is exactly why the map exists. Everything eventually gets its turn; nothing cuts the line.

Chapter Recap #

The rest of the book is built on this chapter. The cycle is five steps with intermittent checkpoints: write the question, research it, write down what you found, identify the next question, and repeat, pausing to reflect in every cycle and once more, formally, before you close out. The steps keep you moving, and the checkpoints keep you thinking. Naming each question’s type (Definitional, Exploratory, Focusing, or Application) tells you which source to consult and roughly how far along you are. The research map holds the questions still to answer, the research log records the process, and the annotated outline records the product; together they make your thinking visible, which is the whole point. Closure, analytical or practical, is a documented judgment rather than a feeling. Every chapter that follows simply runs this cycle through a different source.

Key Takeaways

  • The cycle is simple: question, research, synthesize, next question, repeat.
  • The four question types (Definitional; Exploratory, in its No-Clue, Foundational, and Survey forms; Focusing; and Application) each call for different sources and signal where you are in the arc, and the cycle can loop back among them; a definitional question that comes back empty-handed gives you a focusing question about the rule that fills the gap.
  • One question per question mark. A compound question is two cycles. Split it.
  • The research map holds the questions that need to be answered; the research log records the process; the annotated research outline records the product.
  • Application questions in research ask how courts have applied the rule to facts. Applying the rule to your own facts is a writing step, captured in bracketed notes on the outline during a research pause.
  • Analytical closure means the research is substantively complete. Practical closure means an external constraint requires you to stop. Both are legitimate, and both should be documented.
  • Research and writing are iterative. Expect to return to research while drafting, and let the method absorb it.
  • Some questions are so intuitive you may not notice you asked and answered one. Build the detailed habit now, so that when it becomes second nature you can trust you thought critically at every step.

Reviewing Through Practice #

The following exercises test the core moves of the Question Method: classifying questions by type, deriving questions from answers, running a complete cycle, and drafting a first question from where your knowledge actually is.

Exercise 3.1 — Name the Type #

Classify each of the following as Definitional, Exploratory, Focusing, or Application, with one clause of justification: (a) What does “consideration” mean in contract law? (b) How does the law treat a landlord’s obligation to make repairs? (c) What standard governs a school’s control over a school-sponsored student newspaper? (d) Does any duty to retreat apply inside one’s own home? (e) How do my client’s facts compare to the facts of the leading cases in this jurisdiction? (f) How many states require all parties to consent before a phone call is recorded? (g) How cold did the apartment actually get last January? (h) Why was this statute enacted, and what problem was it built to solve? One of the eight is not a research question at all. Find it and say what kind of question it is instead.

Deliverable: the eight labels with justifications, plus the intruder identified.

Self-check: the intruder is a factual, investigative question; the law will never answer it, only evidence will. If you labeled all eight with a question type, revisit the distinction between what you research and what you investigate.

Exercise 3.2 — Reverse-Engineer the Paragraph #

Take a short paragraph, four to six sentences, from a judicial opinion your instructor assigns, or from any of the school-speech cases named in this chapter’s demonstration. Replace each sentence with the research question it answers, and name each question’s type as you go. You watched this move run in one direction earlier in the chapter; now run it yourself in the other.

Deliverable: the sentence-and-question pairs, each question typed.

Self-check: read your questions in order, ignoring the original sentences. They should form a coherent chain in which each question could plausibly follow from an answer to the one before. If they read as a disconnected list, some questions are answering the paragraph’s topic rather than its individual sentences.

Exercise 3.3 — One Full Cycle #

Choose one question from your Exercise 3.2 chain and run a single complete cycle on it: research it, record the answer, and let that answer hand you the next question. Complete every one of the seven Research Log fields for the entry, and honor the scoping discipline: the answer you record reports only what your question asked, even if the source offered you the whole landscape. Whatever else the source offered goes in the Notes field as later questions, not in the answer.

Deliverable: one complete seven-field Research Log entry.

Self-check: three tests. Could you have written the question with only what you knew at that moment? Does the Findings entry stay inside the question’s scope? Does the Next Question follow from the Findings entry rather than from thin air? A no on any test means the cycle needs a rerun, and the rerun is where the learning is.

Exercise 3.4 — Draft the First Question #

Three clients, three starting points. Client one hands you a document her former employer wants her to sign that would prevent her from ever suing them, and asks if she should. You do not know what this kind of document is called. Client two asks whether the city can make him tear down the shed he built without a permit; you know words like “permit” and “zoning” but have no grip on how that area of law is put together. Client three asks whether your state requires landlords to provide heat, and you have enough grip to suspect a statute answers it directly. For each client, diagnose your starting knowledge, name the right opening move, and draft only the first question. Nothing else.

Deliverable: three diagnoses and three first questions.

Self-check: client one’s first move is vocabulary acquisition, and the question you drafted should contain no term of art you do not yet have. Client two’s question should be exploratory. Client three’s can be focusing. And remember the tomato: what you already “know” about a word’s everyday meaning is not knowledge of what the law does with it.
