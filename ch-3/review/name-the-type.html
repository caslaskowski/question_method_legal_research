Chapter 3 review · Drill

Name the Type

Naming the type tells you where to look and roughly how far along you are. Sort these eight. One of them is not a research question at all; the feedback says why.

Definitional, exploratory, focusing, or application? (Quick check)

  • What does “consideration” mean in contract law? Definitional
    • It asks what a term means. For orientation a dictionary will do; if the definition is load-bearing, go to primary authority.
  • I have a landlord-tenant dispute; where do I even start? Exploratory
    • A no-clue question. An encyclopedia or a general search supplies the landscape and the vocabulary.
  • What are the elements of promissory estoppel? Exploratory
    • Foundational: the structure of a doctrine. A treatise or hornbook answers it.
  • What standard governs a school’s control over a school-sponsored student newspaper? Focusing
    • It asks for the specific rule. This is where you cross from secondary to primary authority.
  • How have courts applied the Hazelwood standard where a principal removed articles about cultural topics? Application
    • It asks how courts have applied the rule to facts in decided cases. Fact-finding application, answered in case law.
  • What counts as “undue hardship” under the ADA’s reasonable accommodation standard? Focusing
    • A sub-rule one level deeper. Focusing questions breed focusing questions.
  • How have other states handled grandparent visitation when the parents object? Exploratory
    • A survey question: the same issue across jurisdictions. An ALR annotation or 50-state survey.
  • Is our client’s newspaper school-sponsored? Application
    • A trap. No database can answer this; it is the writing step, done in a research pause. The research question behind it is how courts have applied “school-sponsored” to papers like ours.
    • Measuring the rule against your own facts is writing, not research. The research question behind it asks how courts applied the term to papers like yours.
