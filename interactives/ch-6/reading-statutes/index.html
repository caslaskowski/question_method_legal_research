Chapter 6 · Appendix C

How to Read a Statute

A statute states the rule without explaining it. Every word carries weight, every cross-reference pulls in another rule, and a defined term means what the act says it means rather than what you would guess. The hardest part is not understanding the sentence in front of you. It is working out which sentence you are supposed to be reading, and noticing when a subsection is answering somebody else’s question.

Before you read

The situation. Your client packed produce in a refrigerated warehouse for eleven years. She now has a chronic lung condition; her physician links it to years of cold, damp air and mold spores in the packing rooms. She is not a police officer, a firefighter, or any kind of emergency responder. Her employer’s insurer denied the claim, saying the illness did not arise out of the employment. Your supervising attorney wants to know what the statute requires before anyone talks settlement.

The source. Arizona’s workers’ compensation law, Title 23, Chapter 6 of the Arizona Revised Statutes. It runs to more than a hundred sections; your question touches two or three of them. Finding those is the work.

Workers’ compensation
A no-fault system: an employee hurt by the job gets benefits without proving the employer was negligent, and in exchange usually cannot sue. Coverage turns on whether the injury or illness is job-related.
Occupational disease
An illness caused by the work itself rather than by a single accident. The act has its own definition and its own extra requirements.
Presumption
A rule that treats something as established unless the other side disproves it. Statutes grant presumptions to specific groups; whoever is not named must prove the ordinary way.
Cross-reference
A statute pointing to another section. Following it is not optional; the section you are reading may be meaningless without it.
