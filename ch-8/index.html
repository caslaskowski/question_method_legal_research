Chapter 8

Researching Across Sources

Research is rarely a tidy matter of finding one statute and a few cases. Often the law governing a client’s issue is spread across many sources: a federal statute, a state common law rule, agency regulations, local ordinances, decades of judicial interpretation. To research efficiently, you have to understand how those sources intersect and when to move from one to another. To research completely, you have to do something more: identify every authority type that governs the issue, reach each one, and read it directly rather than through another source’s description of it. A researcher who treats each source type as a separate, sealed exercise produces a fragmented analysis. A researcher who lets one source type stand in for the others produces something worse, a confident analysis with a hole in it. This chapter teaches both skills: moving fluidly among sources while keeping a single legal question in view, and knowing, at the end of a complex project, that your coverage of the governing authority is complete.

Chapters 4 through 7 treated each source type on its own. This chapter shows how they work together in practice, with the Question Method as the organizing framework.

Learning Objectives

  • Recognize when a legal issue is governed by multiple overlapping sources of law
  • Build a research map that identifies every authority type in play, and maintain it as a coverage checklist
  • Apply the Question Method to move across source types as each answer hands off to the next question
  • Read statutes and regulations directly, including their definitions and related sections, rather than only as other sources characterize them
  • Recognize and document research gaps
  • Use a return pass through secondary sources to confirm coverage on complex, wide-ranging projects

Researching Across Sources and the Research Cycle #

In single-source research, the Question Method tells you which source to consult and how to read it. In multi-source research, it does something more: it tells you when to switch sources and why. The mechanism is the “Next Question” field of your Research Log. Each source you consult answers your current question and, in doing so, generates the next one, and the next question may be answered best by a different source type.

A secondary source orients you and points you to the governing statute. The statute states the framework and, through an undefined term or a delegation of authority, points you to the regulation. The regulation supplies the operative detail and, through its ambiguities, points you to the case law interpreting it. The case law applies the rule to facts and may send you back to a secondary source to make sense of a confusing line of decisions. Each handoff is a question type changing: exploratory to focusing, focusing to definitional, definitional to application. Being explicit in your method lets you flag when you have gotten all you can out of a source and need to move forward.

The method also decides which source comes next. The question in front of you picks the source, and the discipline of asking “did this fully answer my question, or only partially?” after every cycle keeps you moving wherever the answer actually lives. What the full landscape of authority contains, and how to be sure you covered all of it, is the work of the rest of this chapter.

The Statute-Regulation-Case Law Triangle #

In many areas of law, statutes, regulations, and case law all together set the law. The statute sets the framework and delegates authority to an agency. Regulations implement the statute’s mandate with technical and procedural detail. Case law interprets both the statute and the regulations, resolving ambiguities and setting their scope. Your research has to engage all three to give a complete answer. Missing any one of them leaves a gap, and often an error, because the missing source is frequently the very one that would have changed the conclusion.

Three sources, one rule

Select a corner to see the question that source answers and what it cannot tell you alone.

  • Statute | the framework. Answers: what does the law require, prohibit, or permit, and whom does it reach? It cannot tell you the technical detail it delegated to an agency, or how courts have read its undefined terms.
  • Regulation | the detail. Answers: how does the agency put the statutory mandate into operation? It is valid only within the authority the statute gave, so it cannot be read without the statute behind it.
  • Case law | the operation. Answers: how have the statute and the regulation worked on real facts, and are they valid? An opinion’s quotation of a statute or rule is a pointer to the text, never a replacement for reading it.

Go to the Source: Reading Primary Authority Directly #

Multi-source research creates a temptation that single-source research never did. When the cases you are reading quote the statute, it begins to feel as though you have read the statute. When a guidance document walks through the regulation, it feels as though you have read the regulation. You have not. You have read someone’s excerpt, selected for someone else’s purposes, and building your analysis on an excerpt is one of the quietest ways a multi-source project goes wrong.

Consider what an opinion’s quotation of a statute actually gives you: the language that mattered to that dispute, and nothing more. The definitions section that controls the operative terms almost never makes it into the quote. Neither do the exceptions, one of which may remove your client’s conduct from the statute’s reach entirely, nor the cross-referenced sections the provision incorporates. The quoted text may not even be current. Statutes get amended, and an opinion faithfully quoting the pre-amendment version will mislead you without containing a single inaccurate word. And a court’s paraphrase is not text at all; it is interpretation, valuable for what it is and dangerous the moment you mistake it for the thing interpreted.

Regulations invite the same shortcut and punish it the same way. Guidance summarizes. Preambles explain. Opinions excerpt. The operative CFR text is what binds your client, and the sections surrounding it in the same part, the definitions, the exemptions, the compliance detail a neighboring section supplies, are part of the rule whether or not any other source thought to mention them.

So adopt a bright-line habit: the moment a statute or regulation becomes load-bearing in your analysis, open it in the code and read it the way Chapters 6 and 7 taught. For a statute, that means the operative provision, the definitions section, the exceptions, and the cross-references. For a regulation, it means the section itself, the part’s definitions, the authority note, and the adjacent sections of the part. (Appendices C and D walk both reading protocols step by step.) Then make your Research Log honest about what happened. An entry that records the statute as a case quoted it is a case-law entry. The statute gets its own entry when you have read the statute.

The Single-Source Trap #

Open your Research Log and suppose you find five consecutive case-law entries. Is something wrong? Quite possibly not. If your question is a common-law question, the elements of negligence, the formation of a contract, the cases are where the rule lives, and a long run of case entries is the research working exactly as designed. The same is true when you are deep in application questions, comparing your client’s facts against a line of decisions, or following a citation trail through the cases that built a doctrine. A streak in the log measures the rhythm of your research. By itself, it tells you nothing about whether the research is complete.

The failure that deserves a name is a coverage failure: an authority type that governs your issue and that you never looked for, never found, or never read directly. Call it the single-source trap, and notice that it comes in two forms. In the first, a governing authority type is missing from the research altogether. The agency’s regulations sit unread while you work through a sixth case, or a local ordinance controls the client’s conduct and never appears in your log.

In the second, subtler form, the authority type appears in your log but only secondhand: every statutory point you hold arrived through a case’s characterization, every regulatory point through a guidance document, and the primary text itself was never opened. That is the failure the last section taught you to correct. You will see the trap again in Chapter 9’s diagnostic toolkit and in Chapter 14’s practice contexts.

Audit this log

Five consecutive entries in a Research Log on a wage claim list these as the source consulted: a court of appeals opinion; a second opinion from the same court; a district court opinion quoting the statute’s overtime exemption; a state supreme court opinion; a district court opinion summarizing the Department of Labor’s rule. What is the problem?

    • It may be thorough on one side of the triangle. Count the authority types this project is governed by, then count the ones in the log.
    • Weight matters, but it is not the gap here. Something that governs the claim was never opened at all.
    • The subtler form of the single-source trap. Two governing authority types are in the log only as quotations and summaries. Open both and read them directly, or record why you are not.

Answering Questions Across Multiple Sources #

Each source type answers a different kind of question about the same issue. The statute tells you the framework. The regulation tells you the technical detail. The case law tells you how both operate in practice. Your annotated outline should reflect all three, and your Research Log should track when you shifted from one source type to another and why.

Worked example: ADA website accessibility. Must a business make its website accessible under the ADA? One question, three source types, each answering what only it can. A secondary source orients and points to 42 U.S.C. § 12182, Title III’s public-accommodations provision; the statute, read directly, supplies the framework but never mentions websites, because it was enacted in 1990; the DOJ’s Title III regulations, 28 C.F.R. Part 36, turn out to contain no web-accessibility rule, only guidance and proposals, which makes the guidance’s weight its own question; and the case law reveals a circuit split on whether websites are “public accommodations” at all. The synthesis is an annotated outline holding all four findings, framework, regulatory gap, guidance and its weight, split, which is what an accurate answer to a genuinely contested question looks like. The full walkthrough appears in Appendix G.

Managing the Research Log Across Multiple Sources #

When you research across sources, the Research Log becomes especially important. Each source type may generate a new sub-question that leads down a different path, so record each question, source, and finding to keep track of how the sources connect. Two small habits make the log do coverage work for you. First, note the authority type of each entry (statute, regulation, case, secondary source, guidance), so that you can quickly see if you have yet to directly engage with a source. Second, when you switch source types, record why in the Next Question field. That “why” is the record of your research judgment, and it is what lets you, or the colleague who inherits the project, reconstruct the movement across sources later.

When Sources Conflict #

When a federal statute and a state common law rule appear to cover the same conduct, ask first whether the federal statute preempts state law expressly or impliedly. If it does not, both may apply, and your analysis has to address each. The priority rules in practice: the U.S. Constitution overrides all other authority; federal statutes and treaties override conflicting state law; regulations are valid only if within statutory authority; and case law interprets all of the above and cannot contradict clear statutory, regulatory, or constitutional text.

Confidence on a Complex Project: The Return Pass #

There is a question every new researcher asks on a big project, usually late at night: how can I know I have found everything? The issue sprawls across a statute, two sets of regulations, a stack of guidance, and case law from three jurisdictions. The log runs to dozens of entries. No search can prove a negative. The anxiety is reasonable. Confidence on a complex project is something you build, with specific moves.

Audit the research log, authority type by authority type, direct engagement or explicit ruling-out, as the trap section described. Consider going back to the secondary source that opened the project and reread the relevant sections now, at the end, with everything you have learned. Call it the return pass. Now you know the legal points, so additional sections might prove relevant where they were not before. Looking at these sections will hopefully confirm what you have found in your research. If nothing new surfaces, that silence is affirmative evidence that your coverage is complete, and it belongs in your Research Log as exactly that: “return pass through [the treatise], §§ [sections]; no new threads.” Checked and found nothing missing is a finding. It is what earned confidence looks like on paper. In the worst case, if you find another authority you did not realize was implicated or new research questions you had not asked, you have highlighted a gap in your research and you address it the same way you would any gap you surface, regardless of when you find it.

Whatever the result, you will be more confident when you reach the close out phase of your research process.

Common Pitfalls #

Complex research projects are subject to more error not because they are necessarily novel problems, but rather because the size of the project leads to more moments for small failures to compound. You did not validate a point of law and traced backward to other cases that cited that faulty point of law. Or you took a case’s overview of a statute as good enough, and in failing to look at the statute directly, did not find critical language in the section that changes the application of the law to your case, leading you to continue down a line of cases that are not on point for your purposes. The solution is to research thoughtfully and not to skip your research pauses. By engaging in this way, you are more likely to catch a misstep, misinterpretation, or gap that would cause you trouble later unchecked.

Another trap of complex projects is losing yourself in the research. There are so many authorities across various source types that you may continue searching for more and more to feel as though you have truly ‘completed’ the research. To avoid this, stick to the method and synthesize what you find into your outline. In the same way you would not look for 5 cases to cite to establish the elements of negligence in a common law research problem, more is not always better in a complex research project. By synthesizing as you go, you can best assess if you need more sources to either securely support a legal claim in your writing or address a gap in your argument.

Chapter Recap #

Multi-source research is the same cycle with two added judgments: when to switch sources, and how to know your coverage is complete. The research map supplies the checklist, and the statute-regulation-case law triangle is its regulatory core. Read every load-bearing statute and regulation directly, related sections included, because an excerpt is not the law and a paraphrase is someone’s interpretation of it. The single-source trap, properly scoped, is a coverage failure, a governing authority type unreached or reached only secondhand, and its diagnostic is the map, not the streak. On sprawling projects, build confidence structurally: audit the log against the map, run the return pass through your secondary sources, and triangulate your finding tools. The line between “the law is unsettled” and “I have not finished looking” remains the difference between a finding and a confession.

Key Takeaways

  • Most real legal issues are governed by an overlapping combination of statutes, regulations, case law, and constitutional or local provisions.
  • The research map identifies every authority type in play and, kept current, becomes the coverage checklist you audit against.
  • Read statutes and regulations directly, with their definitions, exceptions, and related sections; never rely on another source’s excerpt or paraphrase for a load-bearing rule.
  • The single-source trap is a coverage failure, an authority type unchecked or checked only secondhand; a streak of same-type log entries is not by itself a problem.
  • The return pass, rereading your orienting secondary source at the end with expert eyes, catches unpulled threads cheaply and turns “nothing new” into documented evidence of coverage.
  • Research gap identification, distinguishing “the law is unsettled” from “I haven’t found the answer yet,” is a critical professional skill.

Reviewing Through Practice: The Drone Capstone #

The following exercises test your ability to map the relationships among source types and to distinguish unsettled law from incomplete research, followed by the capstone of the drill set.

Exercise 8.1 — Build the Triangle #

Return to the organic-labeling question that has run through this book since Chapter 3, and build its research map as a statute-regulation-case-law triangle. At the statute corner, record what the delegating statute decided, located through the regulation’s authority note. At the regulation corner, record what the regulation itself supplies, located through the eCFR. At the case-law corner, identify at least one decision interpreting either, located through an annotated code or a citator. Then annotate each edge of the triangle: what question sends a researcher traveling from this corner to that one?

Deliverable: a one-page triangle map, corners filled, edges annotated with the traveling questions.

Self-check: the edges are the exercise. If your map has three full corners and empty edges, you have catalogued sources without mapping the movement between them, and movement between sources is what this chapter teaches.

Exercise 8.2 — Unsettled or Unfound? #

From any research question you have worked in this course (any of the Part I drills works, or your instructor may assign one), write two findings: one that honestly reads “the law is genuinely unsettled on this point,” and one that honestly reads “I have not yet found the answer.” Defend each in two sentences, naming what you searched and why that search entitles you, or does not, to the stronger claim. “The law is unsettled” is a professional conclusion; “I could not find any law” is a confession. Knowing which one you are holding is the skill.

Deliverable: the two findings with defenses.

Self-check: the unsettled finding must rest on a search thorough enough that a supervisor would accept it, and its defense should say so specifically. If both of your findings could trade labels without the defenses changing, neither defense is doing its job yet.

Exercise 8.3 — The Capstone: The Drone Question #

This is the capstone of the single-source drill set introduced at the end of Chapter 4, and it is the exercise this whole chapter has been building toward. Run the chain yourself first, from the scenario alone: draft every question, log every answer, and decide where closure is. Then, and only then, check your work against the walkthrough in Appendix E.

The scenario. Our client runs a small real-estate marketing business and wants to start flying a camera drone to shoot aerial photos and video of listed properties, for paying clients. The client asks: what do I have to do to operate the drone legally for this commercial work, and what legal exposure should I worry about? You do not yet know the area, and no one has narrowed it for you. Map and answer the question across whatever sources govern, using the Question Method to move among them.
