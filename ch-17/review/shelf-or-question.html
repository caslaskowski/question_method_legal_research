Chapter 17 review · Drill

Shelf or Question?

A paper starts from a question, not a shelf. Sort each candidate.

What are you looking at? (Quick check)

  • “Privacy law and drones.” A shelf
    • Two nouns and a conjunction. Nothing here can be answered. Narrow it until a thesis could be the answer.
  • “In declining to decide whether a camera drone hovering at 200 feet is a trespass, the court reserved the question. Is it?” An inventory entry
    • The court expressly reserved it: the model inventory entry, a question another text left open, logged with its provenance.
  • “Does training an AI model on licensed stock photography exhaust the license?” A question a paper could answer
    • Nobody knows yet, and a paper could work out the answer. The chapter’s own example of an unsettled question.
  • “The DMCA.” A shelf
    • A statute is a shelf. “How might we fix the notice-and-takedown process to protect creators without stifling creation?” is the question.
  • “Two circuits read ‘public forum’ in the anti-SLAPP context differently and have never engaged each other. Which reading is right?” An inventory entry
    • Two authorities reading a term differently without engaging each other: an inventory entry, and a live controversy once you pick it up.
  • “Everything the Supreme Court has said about agency deference.” A shelf
    • A literature summary with ambitions. What is the question your paper would answer about it?
  • “After Loper Bright, should courts give Skidmore weight to agency FAQ pages that no agency head signed?” A question a paper could answer
    • Specific, open, answerable. The thesis would be the answer; the one-hour preemption check comes next.
  • “The article’s last footnote says it does not address state analogues. What do the state statutes do?” An inventory entry
    • A point flagged as beyond an article’s scope: inventory, with provenance.
