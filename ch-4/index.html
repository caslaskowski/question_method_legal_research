Chapter 4

Secondary Sources Research

Secondary sources are materials that explain, interpret, or analyze the law. They help you understand an unfamiliar area and find your way to primary authority. Think of secondary sources as your map of the legal landscape.

Good researchers use secondary sources strategically, both to get oriented early and to deepen their understanding of complex legal frameworks that involve multiple legal authorities. Knowing which type to reach for, and when, is one of the most valuable skills you will build.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the major types of secondary sources used in legal research
  • Select the most appropriate secondary source for a given type of research question
  • Use secondary sources to locate primary authority efficiently
  • Critically evaluate the authority, reliability, and currency of a secondary source
  • Integrate secondary source research into the Question Method framework

Secondary Sources and the Research Cycle #

Secondary sources are the natural home of the early Question Method cycle, and understanding how the method shapes your use of them is what turns a secondary source from a thing you read into a tool you operate.

The type of question you are asking tells you both which secondary source to reach for and how to engage with it once you have it. A no-clue exploratory question sends you to an encyclopedia, where you read for breadth and vocabulary rather than depth. A foundational question sends you to a treatise, where you read for the structure of a doctrine: its elements, its tests, its contested points. A survey question sends you to an ALR annotation or a Restatement, where you read for how authority is distributed across jurisdictions.

The method also disciplines how you engage with a secondary source. So you navigate by table of contents and index, you stop reading the moment your question is answered, and you treat the footnotes as the most valuable part of the page, because the citations they contain are your bridge from the secondary source to the primary authority. Each primary authority a secondary source hands you is a candidate answer to your next question.

Questions This Source Answers #

Secondary sources are your primary tool for definitional questions (“What does res judicata mean?”) and no-clue exploratory questions (“I have a contract dispute, where do I even start?”). As your research moves along, they also help with foundational exploratory questions (“What are the elements of promissory estoppel?”) and survey questions (“How have different jurisdictions handled this issue?”). You will rarely use secondary sources for application questions since you will likely ask these long after you have moved beyond a secondary source in your research, but the citations inside secondary sources will flag leads for you to follow later on.

How Secondary Sources Are Made and What That Means for Research #

Secondary source authority varies enormously. The most well-regarded secondary sources are written by recognized experts: leading professors or practitioners (treatises), Restatement drafters selected by the American Law Institute after extensive vetting, and editorial teams at established legal publishers who keep their work continuously updated.

When you evaluate any secondary source, ask who wrote it, what their expertise is, what the publication venue is, when it was last updated, and who the intended audience is. You might use Wikipedia to get a lay explanation of a concept as background for your search terms, but you would not use Wikipedia, or any unvetted source, to answer your research questions. The reliability of the source determines how much weight you can place on what it tells you and how you might use it in your research.

Types of Secondary Sources in Legal Research #

Legal Dictionaries and Words & Phrases #

Legal dictionaries, like Black’s Law Dictionary, define legal terms and explain how they are used. Words & Phrases is a specialized tool that collects judicial definitions of specific terms from published opinions, which makes it more powerful than an ordinary dictionary when the precise judicial meaning of a term matters to your argument. Use these at the very beginning, when a term is unfamiliar or its precise judicial meaning is important. If you are reading a case that mentions “mens rea” or “preemption,” check Black’s for a quick definition before diving deeper. At times, they can be used as citable persuasive authority as proof of the common usage of a term with no legal definition

Legal Encyclopedias #

American Jurisprudence 2d (Am. Jur. 2d) and Corpus Juris Secundum (C.J.S.) are the two national encyclopedias. Many states have their own, like Texas Jurisprudence, California Jurisprudence, and Florida Jurisprudence. Legal encyclopedias cover nearly every topic with brief summaries of legal principles and citations to leading cases and statutes. Use them at the outset, when you need an overview or do not yet know which specific laws apply.

Encyclopedias give you breadth, not depth. They summarize, but they do not analyze or critique, and they will not tell you which way a contested question is likely to come out. Use one to learn the basic language and framework of your issue, then move on to sources with more depth.

Treatises, Hornbooks, and Nutshells #

A treatise is a detailed book written by an expert, often a professor or senior practitioner, that covers one area of law in depth. Some span many volumes; Wright & Miller’s Federal Practice and Procedure fills shelves. Others are a single volume. Hornbooks are single-volume treatise-style texts written mainly for law students. Nutshells are even more condensed and are best for initial orientation rather than deep research.

Reach for a treatise once you have identified your topic and want to understand how the law actually works: the internal logic of a doctrine, how its elements developed, how different courts have approached contested points. If you are studying torts and want to understand how courts analyze “duty of care,” you might turn to Prosser and Keeton on Torts. The author will explain the doctrine, give examples, and cite the most influential cases, and a good treatise will also tell you where the doctrine is unsettled, which is precisely where your hardest research questions will arise.

Restatements of the Law #

Restatements are published by the American Law Institute (ALI) to clarify and organize the common law. Each section summarizes a legal rule, explains its reasoning in comments, and cites supporting cases. Examples include the Restatement (Second) of Contracts and the Restatement (Third) of Torts. Restatements are persuasive, not binding, but courts cite them frequently, and some sections carry especially strong persuasive weight, particularly where a state’s highest court has expressly adopted them. Use them when you want the majority rule or a sense of how courts across the country generally handle a specific issue.

American Law Reports (ALR) #

American Law Reports sits between encyclopedias and treatises. Each ALR article, called an annotation, focuses on a narrow, specific issue and provides an in-depth discussion supported by extensive case citations from many jurisdictions. If you are researching whether social media posts can support a defamation claim, an ALR annotation titled something like “Defamation Based on Internet Communications” might lay out the general principles, summarize cases from various states, and flag patterns or disagreements among courts.

ALR annotations are best when you have a specific question of law and want to see how it has been treated across jurisdictions, which is exactly what a survey question demands. They summarize cases rather than interpret them, so they do not offer original analysis. Always check when an annotation was last updated, because coverage is selective and an older annotation may miss recent developments.

Congressional Research Service (CRS) Reports #

The Congressional Research Service is a nonpartisan office within the Library of Congress that prepares research reports for members of Congress. CRS reports explain complex policy and legal issues in accessible, factual terms, with citations to statutes, regulations, and related legislative materials and nonpartisan context connecting law to real-world policy. They are free at crsreports.congress.gov. Use them when you want the background or policy context behind a federal law. They are not legal authority, but they are an unusually trustworthy, well-cited map of a federal statutory scheme.

Law Review and Journal Articles #

Law reviews and journals are scholarly publications produced by law schools, with articles written by professors, practitioners, judges, and sometimes students. They analyze legal issues, propose reforms, and discuss trends and controversies. Use them in the middle or later stages of research, once you understand the basics and want to explore deeper arguments, theories, or policy debates, and use them early when you are doing scholarly writing and need to map the academic landscape. Law review articles can be highly academic, and some lean more toward theory than application, so weigh whether the article actually answers your question or merely contextualizes it.

Practice Guides and Practitioner Materials #

Practice guides go beyond the legal doctrine to the practical steps of representing a client in an area of law. They often include forms, checklists, and step-by-step instructions. Examples include the Rutter Group Practice Guides on the West Coast, the Federal Civil Rules Handbook, and state-specific practice guides. Reach for one when you are ready to take your research and put it into practice. If you are helping draft a motion for summary judgment, a practice guide will walk you through the procedural steps, deadlines, and sample language, the “how do I actually do this” answers that doctrinal sources never give.

Other Secondary Sources: Government Websites, Blawgs, and More #

Not every useful secondary source comes from a law library or a commercial database. Government websites maintained by courts, legislatures, and agencies are authoritative sources that can offer guidance, explain procedures, and point you to primary authority. The Department of Education, for example, has detailed information on individualized education plans aimed at both families and educators.

Law blogs (“blawgs”) are written by legal professionals, often attorneys or professors, in their areas of expertise. SCOTUSblog posts same-day summaries of major Court opinions; the Volokh Conspiracy covers constitutional and administrative law; Lawfare covers national security. These are valuable for recent developments, but they are not peer-reviewed, so always verify with primary sources.

News, think tank, and nonprofit reports from organizations like the Brennan Center for Justice, the Pew Research Center, or ProPublica provide context, statistics, and public-impact analysis. They are excellent when you are framing a question or trying to understand the social or political backdrop of an issue, but they are not authority.

Searching for Secondary Sources #

Once you know which type of secondary source fits your question, you can find it through several paths. LibGuides are research guides created by law librarians nationwide that identify the most authoritative sources in specific areas; to locate one, you can search [topic] research guide.

In commercial databases, Westlaw’s Secondary Sources tab, Lexis+’s Secondary Materials section, and Bloomberg Law’s Secondary Sources collection all let you browse and keyword-search within secondary-source collections. HeinOnline is the most comprehensive database for law review and journal articles and offers historical depth you cannot get elsewhere. For working papers and forthcoming articles, SSRN is invaluable. Google Scholar gives broad coverage but is best used for research outside the legal domain.

In print, use call numbers and subject indexes. Many of the most authoritative treatises are available only in print or through commercial databases, and some are not freely accessible online. When in doubt, ask a law librarian. They are among the most underused research resources available to law students. If you are not sure what asking looks like, copy this: “I’m researching X, and my current question is Y. Which treatise should I start with?”

Breadth against depth

Every secondary source trades breadth for depth. Select a source to see the question it answers best and how much weight a court gives it.

  • Law review article | 12, 88. One argument, examined exhaustively. Best for unsettled or grey areas and for the critique of a rule. Persuasive only on the strength of its reasoning and its author.
  • ALR annotation | 20, 66. One narrow issue, traced through the cases of many jurisdictions. The tool for a survey question. Rarely cited; mined for its cases.
  • Treatise | 50, 80. One field, explained in depth by an expert. The tool for a foundational question. A leading treatise is among the few secondary sources courts cite with respect.
  • Restatement | 54, 56. The common law of one field, stated as black-letter rules with comments and illustrations. Highly persuasive, and binding once your jurisdiction’s courts adopt a section.
  • CRS report | 22, 42. One federal policy question, explained neutrally for Congress. Strong on statutory schemes and legislative background. Not authority.
  • Practice guide | 48, 32. One practice area, organized around the steps of doing the work: forms, checklists, deadlines. Answers “how do I actually do this?”
  • Legal encyclopedia | 78, 18. Nearly every topic, summarized. The tool for a no-clue question. It will not analyze or critique, and you cite the cases in its footnotes, never the entry.
  • Legal dictionary | 90, 5. Every term, a few lines each. A working definition to keep you moving. The definition that binds comes from the statute or the cases.

Answering Questions with Secondary Sources #

Matching Source Type to Question Type #

The mapping runs from breadth to depth: encyclopedias for no-clue questions, treatises for foundational ones, survey tools for the law across jurisdictions, practice guides when you need to apply the law, and law reviews when primary authority leaves real grey areas. Here it is in one table for reference:

Question type Reach for Example
No-Clue Exploratory Legal encyclopedia Am. Jur. 2d; C.J.S.; a state encyclopedia
Definitional (working definition) Legal dictionary; Words & Phrases Black’s Law Dictionary
Foundational Exploratory Treatise or hornbook Prosser and Keeton on Torts
Survey ALR; Restatement; 50-state survey; CRS report An ALR annotation on your issue
Practical application Practice guide Rutter Group; Federal Civil Rules Handbook
Unsettled or grey areas Law review and journal articles Diane Leenheer Zimmerman, Copyright and Social Media: A Tale of Legislative Abdication, 35 Pace L. Rev. 260 (2014).

Identifying Relevant Materials and Synthesizing Into Your Writing #

Navigate a secondary source strategically by starting with the table of contents or index instead of reading cover to cover. Follow citation trails to primary authority, since the footnotes of a treatise section are often as valuable as the text itself. Pull out the key framing, definitions, and synthesized rules, but verify each claim against the primary source it cites, because a secondary source is a starting point, not a substitute for reading the law. Record your findings in your annotated outline as sub-points under the relevant question heading, with citations noted for later drafting.

As you evaluate a secondary source, ask who wrote it, when it was last updated, whether it is the most current edition, and whether it covers your jurisdiction. The most recent edition of a major treatise published five years ago may already be badly out of date in a fast-moving area like data privacy or AI regulation.

Example: Illinois Attorney Malpractice Research #

Return to the reverse-engineering demonstration from Chapter 3, built from Oakland Police & Fire Retirement System v. Mayer Brown. Our first question asks, Under Illinois law, what is the general scope of legal malpractice law? (foundational) As a broad background question, we begin with Illinois Jurisprudence. The section on attorney malpractice gives a general overview, noting that an attorney’s duties are generally limited to the client, but there are exceptions , and it cites Pelham. This gives us the foundation to begin mapping out the legal rules in this area of law.

Before we pivot to Pelham to answer the first question for our annotated outline, we mine the secondary source for all the answers it gives, converting them into questions for future research: To whom can an attorney be held liable? Why might an attorney owe a duty to non-clients? What are some situations where courts have found an attorney to owe a duty to a third party? By logging these future research questions and the sources it cites to answer them, we create a preliminary plan for our research. Once we have logged this information, we are ready to turn to Pelham.

Common Pitfalls #

The classic secondary-source error is treating the secondary source as the answer instead of the map. With rare exceptions, your argument rests on the primary authority the source led you to, not on the secondary source. Follow the footnotes to the primary authority, verifying each claim there.

Be wary of losing yourself in the secondary source for too long. Once you have your foundational understanding and mapped the research questions it shows you, pivot to the primary sources. Otherwise you might find yourself lost on tangents.

Lastly, secondary sources can quietly fall out of date. That currency problem catches people often, so check the edition date and the pocket part before you trust what a source tells you.

Beyond the Basics #

When to Return to Secondary Sources Later in Research #

You will sometimes circle back to secondary sources well into a project: when primary authority is vague, and you need persuasive arguments and scholarly context; when you need to find cases in jurisdictions your initial search did not cover; when a tangential issue you did not anticipate turns up; or when a line of cases is hard to follow and you need it broken down more accessibly. If it helps you keep your forward momentum in a research project, it is a valid step.

Citing Secondary Sources in Legal Writing #

In most court filings, secondary sources are not cited as authority. In persuasive writing, a Restatement or a leading treatise may occasionally be cited for a proposition that on-point primary authority does not support. In scholarly writing, secondary sources are cited extensively. Know your context. The same source that would be out of place in a brief may be exactly what a law review article needs to establish its scholarly foundation.

Chapter Recap #

Secondary sources are the researcher’s roadmap. They orient, explain, contextualize, and point toward primary authority. Each type serves a distinct function keyed to the type of question you are asking. Use them strategically: start broad, go deeper, and always follow the citation trail to primary law. Return to them as needed throughout a project, because the move from secondary to primary authority is not a one-way door.

Key Takeaways

  • Secondary sources explain the law but do not constitute it; they are roadmaps, not destinations.
  • Different types serve different functions at different stages: encyclopedias for no-clue questions, treatises for foundational questions, ALR and Restatements for survey questions, practice guides for practical questions.
  • Always evaluate for author expertise, publication venue, currency, and intended audience.
  • Secondary sources are always persuasive, never binding.
  • Follow the footnotes; secondary sources lead to primary authority.
  • Return to secondary sources as needed throughout the process; the shift to primary authority is not permanent.

Reviewing Through Practice: Single-Source Drills #

Every experienced researcher eventually reads a statute, a case, and a regulation in one motion, barely noticing where one source ends and the next begins. That fluency is the goal. It is also the wrong place to start. The hypotheticals that close this chapter and the next four each wall off a single source type so you can drill the moves that source demands, hands at ten and two, before you ever combine them. I call these single-source drills, and Part I is where they belong.

There is an honest tension to name up front. The Question Method tells you to begin most projects in a secondary source, where you acquire the vocabulary and the lay of the land. A case-only or a statute-only exercise cannot start there without leaking into another source. So these drills hand you the orientation a secondary source would normally supply: the supervising attorney has already framed the claim, named the governing source, and given you the terms of art. And it is faithful to how the job actually works: a supervising attorney often hands you a task exactly this way, already framed, and it lets you practice one source in isolation. The cost is that you skip the orientation step, and the capstone in Chapter 8 is where you put it back.

Work each chain the slow way. Draft every question, even the obvious ones, and write the scoped answer down before you reach for the next question. When you feel the urge to leap three questions ahead, that leap is exactly the move a novice cannot yet make safely. Make the steps visible instead. Keep a Research Log for every drill, the same one you will keep in practice.

The first two exercises test your ability to match secondary source types to research situations and to use a secondary source to acquire vocabulary. The third is the drill itself.

Exercise 4.1 — Match the Source to the Question #

Four researchers, four situations. Researcher one has never heard of the area she was just assigned and needs plain-language orientation and vocabulary. Researcher two knows the area exists but needs its full doctrinal structure laid out, with the leading authorities organized. Researcher three needs to know how the fifty states divide on a single narrow rule. Researcher four needs the newest scholarly thinking on an unsettled question no treatise has caught up with. For each, name the secondary source type from this chapter best suited to the job, and one sentence on why.

Deliverable: four pairings with justifications.

Self-check: if you gave two researchers the same source type, at least one of them is mismatched. Each situation in this exercise has a distinct best answer, and the chapter’s survey of source types maps onto them one to one.

Exercise 4.2 — The Vocabulary Harvest #

A client’s relationship with her adult son has collapsed, and the son is now refusing to let her see her two young grandchildren. She asks whether a court can order that she get time with them. You do not know what this area of law is called. Spend no more than thirty minutes in a single legal encyclopedia entry, and harvest: every term of art you encounter, a plain-language definition of each in your own words, and where in the entry you found it. Then write the one focusing question the harvest now permits you to ask, a question you could not have written thirty minutes ago.

Deliverable: the term list and the single focusing question.

Self-check: your question should contain at least one harvested term and should be answerable by primary authority. If your question could have been written before the harvest, it is not using what you learned; sharpen it until it could not have been.

Exercise 4.3 — The Chapter 4 Drill (Secondary Sources Only): Where Do I Even Start? #

The scenario. A friend was fired the day after she posted on social media criticizing her manager for refusing to pay overtime. She wants to know whether she has any recourse. A senior associate has asked you to orient the team to the area before anyone touches primary authority: identify the governing framework and the authorities worth chasing. Stay entirely in secondary sources. Do not yet read the statutes or the cases.

Run the chain yourself, every question drafted, every answer logged. When you have it, check your work against the walkthrough in Appendix E.
