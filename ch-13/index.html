Chapter 13

Dockets and Court Document Research

Court documents give you a window into how the law operates in practice. Judicial opinions tell you what courts decided; the pleadings, briefs, and motions show you how lawyers argued, what facts they marshaled, and how courts reasoned through contested issues before issuing a published opinion. Court document research is especially valuable for finding arguments in cases with similar facts, understanding the procedural history of a precedent-setting case, locating unreported orders, and mining briefs for citation lists in novel areas. Court documents are a research tool, not a source of authority, and keeping that distinction clear is the first discipline of this chapter.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain what a court docket is and what types of information it contains
  • Locate federal court dockets using PACER and free alternatives
  • Identify and retrieve key court documents: complaints, briefs, motions, and orders
  • Evaluate the research value of court documents and their limitations
  • Locate state court documents using state-specific resources
  • Integrate court document research into the Question Method framework

Court Documents and the Research Cycle #

Court documents occupy an unusual place in the Question Method. They are almost never the answer to a research question, but they are one of the most efficient accelerators of the research cycle. Understanding how the method treats them prevents the beginner’s error of citing a brief as if it were authority.

The method shapes how you find court documents, because they are most useful once a specific question has already narrowed your inquiry. Early exploratory questions are answered by secondary sources, not dockets; reaching for PACER before you know the governing standard wastes money and time. But once a focusing or application question is in front of you (“what is the full range of arguments on this contested point?” or “how have lawyers in my circuit framed this exact claim?”), a well-researched brief in a major case becomes a shortcut, because its author has already done the work of assembling the leading authorities on the point. The citations in that brief become candidate answers to your next several questions.

The method shapes how you engage with court documents, because it forces you to treat them as leads, not findings. A brief tells you what one party argued, not what the law is. The method’s Step 3, “write down what you found,” applies with a twist: what you found in a brief is a set of citations and a framing, both of which have to be independently verified against the primary authority before anything enters your annotated outline as an answer. A docket entry that looks like an answer is really a new question: “does the authority this brief cites actually say what the brief claims?” Running that verification is the cycle continuing, not concluding.

Questions This Source Answers #

Court document research answers Application questions about how lawyers have argued specific issues (“How have attorneys framed Eighth Amendment cruel-and-unusual-punishment claims in conditions-of-confinement cases?”), procedural questions (“What motions have been filed in this case, and when?”), and strategic questions (“What evidence did the opposing party rely on in similar cases?”). Briefs in major cases are particularly efficient for identifying leading authorities on contested points; the citations in a well-researched appellate brief can accelerate your research substantially.

Reflection Point. A well-researched brief seems like a shortcut to everything at once. Why does the method still send you to dockets only after your question has narrowed? (A short answer appears in Appendix I.)

How Dockets and Court Documents Are Created #

When parties file documents in court, each one is assigned a number and recorded on the docket, the official log of all filings. The federal courts use CM/ECF for filing, accessible through PACER (pacer.uscourts.gov). Document types include pleadings (complaints, petitions, answers), motions and responses, briefs, amicus briefs, orders and opinions, and judgments.

Understanding a Docket #

Each PACER entry shows the date, docket number, document type, and filer. Key information includes party names and counsel, the assigned judge, the case number and district, and the filing sequence from complaint to judgment. The documents most worth locating are the complaint (the scope of the dispute), the dispositive motions and supporting briefs (the legal arguments), and the court’s orders resolving those motions (the court’s reasoning, sometimes more detailed than any published opinion).

Do not confuse docket entries with the documents themselves. The docket lists filings; you have to open each entry to read the actual document.

Example

What only the docket holds. The sanctions decision in Mata v. Avianca (Chapter 15) is a single document. The docket runs to dozens of entries, and it is where the research actually lives: the order to show cause, the affidavit attaching the fabricated opinions, the transcript of the June 8 hearing where the lawyer explained what he had assumed about the tool, and the letters the court later required. A researcher who reads only the published decision knows the holding. A researcher who pulls the docket knows how the failure unfolded, which is the part that answers “how do I keep this from happening in my office?”

Read a docket, newest entry first

A fictional federal docket, Marsh v. Golden Rentals, LLC, shown the way a docket is displayed: latest filing at the top, the complaint at the bottom. Select an entry to see what it tells a researcher.

  • 12 | Minute order. Minute order setting oral argument on [8] Motion for Summary Judgment. Short, procedural, and often published nowhere else. It answers “what happens next in this case?”, which is why the freshest fact sits at the top.
  • 11 | Reply. Reply in support of [8] Motion for Summary Judgment, filed by Golden Rentals. The reply closes the briefing sequence: motion, response, reply. Entries cross-reference one another by number. Following the bracketed numbers is how you reconstruct which paper answers which.
  • 10 | Response. Response in opposition to [8] Motion for Summary Judgment, filed by Marsh. For a researcher with a similar case, the opposition brief is often the best free collection of contrary authority available. It is advocacy, so it is a list of leads to read, not a statement of the law.
  • 8 | Motion. Motion for Summary Judgment. Attachments: (1) Memorandum in support; (2) Statement of facts; (3) Exhibits A–C; (4) Proposed order. One entry, five documents. The motion itself is often a two-page formality. The argument lives in the memorandum, the evidence in the exhibits, and the proposed order shows exactly what relief is sought. Stop at the entry line and you miss all of it.
  • 7 | Scheduling order. Scheduling order: discovery closes 04/24; dispositive motions due 05/01. The case’s calendar. It tells you when a ruling you could use is likely to issue.
  • 6 | Order. Order denying [4] Motion to Dismiss. An order with reasoning, and quite possibly unreported. It decided a real legal question and may never appear in a reporter. The docket is how you find rulings the published record does not show.
  • 2 | Answer. Answer to [1] Complaint, filed by Golden Rentals. Admissions, denials, and defenses, paragraph by paragraph. Read against the complaint, it maps what is actually in dispute.
  • 1 | Complaint. Complaint with jury demand, filed by Marsh. Where the case begins, at the bottom of the display. Allegations are unproven assertions, not findings. They are still the best single statement of what the plaintiff says happened and how counsel framed the claims.

Reading the log is a skill, and on a metered system it is also a budget. PACER charges by the page, with a cap per document, so every entry you open without needing it is a line on a client’s bill. Here is the same fictional docket as a route. You need one thing: the court’s reasoning for denying the motion to dismiss. Choose where to click and watch the bill. Then start over and find the cheapest path.

Find the ruling without paying for the docket

Every document in this exercise is fictional, and the fee figures are a stand-in for whatever PACER charges on the day you read this.

  • begin | $0.00. You have the case number for Marsh v. Golden Rentals, LLC. The supervising attorney wants the court’s reasoning for denying the motion to dismiss, and nothing else. The docket has twelve entries. Where do you start?
    • sheet. Pull the docket sheet, the two-page log of every filing.
    • complaint. Open the complaint. The case begins there, so start there.
    • all. Download every document. Reading it all is thorough.
  • sheet | $0.20. Two pages, twenty cents. Now you can read the log: entry 4 is the Motion to Dismiss, entry 5 the Response in opposition, and entry 6 reads “Order denying [4] Motion to Dismiss.” The bracketed number tells you which paper answered which. What next?
    • order. Open entry 6, the order.
    • motion. Open entry 4 first, to understand what was argued before you read the ruling.
    • facts. Open the complaint first, to get the facts straight.
  • complaint | $3.00. Forty-one pages of allegations, capped at three dollars. You now know what the plaintiff says happened. You still do not know what the court decided, or why, and because you skipped the log you do not know which entry holds the ruling.
    • sheet2. Pull the docket sheet now.
  • sheet2 | $0.20. The log, at last: entry 6 is the “Order denying [4] Motion to Dismiss.” Twenty cents would have told you that first.
    • order. Open entry 6, the order.
  • facts | $3.00. Forty-one pages of allegations. Allegations are unproven assertions; they tell you how counsel framed the claims, which was not the question. The order will recite the facts it relied on anyway.
    • order. Open entry 6, the order.
  • motion | $1.80. Eighteen pages of the defendant’s argument. Useful if you later want the contrary authority, and worth nothing for today’s question, because a motion tells you what was argued, never what was decided.
    • order. Now open entry 6, the order.
  • all | $14.70. Twelve entries, several at the three-dollar cap. The order is in the pile somewhere, and you will not know which file it is until you open each one. At a firm, this line on the bill gets questioned, and “I wanted to be thorough” is the answer that gets the research reassigned.
    • found. Find the order in the pile and read it.
  • found | $0.00. You find it on the fourth try. Nine pages, already paid for. The court held that the implied warranty of habitability claim was adequately pleaded and that the lease’s waiver clause was unenforceable as against public policy. Unreported, in no reporter, and exactly what you were asked for. Compare your total with the cheapest route: the docket sheet and the order, $1.10.
  • order | $0.90. Nine pages. The court held that the implied warranty of habitability claim was adequately pleaded and that the lease’s waiver clause was unenforceable as against public policy. This order appears in no reporter. The docket is the only way anyone will ever find it, and the log is the only way to find it cheaply. Look at your trail. The cheapest route was the docket sheet and the order, $1.10. Everything else you opened taught you something, and none of it was the question.

Finding a Docket #

  • Federal courts, PACER. Comprehensive; charges per page, though if your quarterly charges stay under the current threshold the fees are waived automatically, so most light users never owe a dime; separate research exemptions exist but are narrow and granted court by court; requires account registration.

  • CourtListener (free). Free access to many federal court documents, and home to the RECAP archive, a freely accessible repository of PACER documents uploaded by users.
  • Justia and Google Scholar. Partial free coverage, especially of published opinions.
  • Bloomberg Law and Westlaw. Comprehensive commercial docket access for many jurisdictions.
  • State courts. Each state runs its own electronic filing system. Accessibility varies a lot; most state appellate courts offer more comprehensive online access than trial courts.

Answering Questions with Court Documents #

Complaints answer “How have lawyers framed similar legal theories?” Briefs answer “What is the full range of arguments on this issue?” Amicus briefs answer “What perspectives do non-parties bring?” Orders and unpublished opinions answer “How has the court reasoned through procedural or preliminary issues in similar cases?” Each document type has a distinct research use. Use briefs to identify authority, complaints to understand factual framing, and orders to understand procedural reasoning. Record your findings in your annotated outline: the case, the document type, the key argument or passage, and the docket entry number.

Court documents are also valuable in several practical situations: to review filings in a pending or recent case that has not yet been fully reported; to run due diligence on a client, company, or opposing party; to research procedural history and understand all prior proceedings before an appeal; and to find expert reports, deposition transcripts, or exhibits attached to filed motions.

Common Pitfalls #

The error this chapter exists to prevent is citing a brief or a complaint as though it were authority; it records what one side argued, and the proposition it asserts is only as good as the primary source behind it, which you have to verify yourself. A second is going to PACER too early, before a secondary source has told you the governing standard, which spends money and time chasing documents you cannot yet evaluate. And do not blur published opinions, which carry binding or persuasive weight, with the unpublished orders and filings around them, which are research tools and nothing more.

Beyond the Basics #

Court Rules Research #

Court documents are created under the authority of court rules, and those rules are themselves an essential research target. Before you file anything, research the applicable rules at every level. The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, Criminal Procedure, Evidence, and Appellate Procedure govern federal practice nationwide and are available at uscourts.gov. Advisory Committee Notes function as legislative history for procedural rules. Local court rules supplement the Federal Rules and govern page limits, formatting, scheduling, and much more, available on each court’s website. Judge-specific standing orders supplement local rules further. Work through the rules hierarchy top to bottom before filing, because a perfectly researched motion filed in violation of a local formatting rule can still be rejected.

Argument Mining from Briefs #

In landmark or frequently litigated areas, searching the briefs filed in major cases, including amicus briefs, is an efficient way to canvass the whole debate on an issue. Many law school libraries and commercial databases maintain brief banks.

Pending Cases #

Searching dockets for pending cases on the same issue can reveal that the law is about to change. A case on appeal or pending certiorari may overrule current law. Always check whether any leading case in your research has been granted certiorari or is on appeal. A citator check accomplishes part of this, but a docket search can surface a challenge before any citator flag appears.

Chapter Recap #

Dockets and court documents are accelerators, not authority. A docket is the map of a litigation; the filings on it show how lawyers actually argued, and a well-researched brief can hand you the leading authorities on a contested point in minutes. But every proposition you take from one is a lead to verify, and the reframing to remember is that a docket find is a new research question: does the authority this brief cites say what the brief claims? Come here after your question has narrowed, not before.

Key Takeaways

  • A docket is the official log of all filings in a case; PACER provides federal access, and CourtListener provides free partial coverage.
  • Briefs, complaints, amicus filings, and orders each provide different research value.
  • Brief mining is an efficient strategy for identifying leading authorities on contested issues.
  • Court rules research is mandatory before any filing: Federal Rules, then local rules, then judge standing orders.
  • Always distinguish published opinions (binding or persuasive authority) from unpublished orders and briefs (research tools, not authority).

Reviewing Through Practice #

The following exercises test your ability to read a docket, verify claims made in briefs, and trace the court rules that govern filings. All three run in free sources.

Exercise 13.1 — Read the Docket #

Choose a recent federal civil case, filed within the last few years so its docket lives in the electronic systems this chapter covers; your instructor may assign one, or take one from recent legal news. Find its docket on CourtListener. Read the docket as a map, and identify, by entry number, the complaint, the dispositive motion with its supporting brief, and the order resolving that motion. Do not open the documents yet. The point of this pass is learning to read the log itself.

Deliverable: the three entry numbers with one line each on how you recognized them.

Self-check: if you had to open documents to find them, note where the docket text alone failed you; on a metered system, every one of those opens would have cost money, and the skill of reading the log is the skill of not paying for what the log already says.

Exercise 13.2 — The Brief Autopsy #

Locate a brief supporting a dispositive motion in any recent federal case on CourtListener, or use a brief your instructor assigns. Harvest three authorities it cites for its central argument, and pick one to run this chapter’s defining question on: does the authority this brief cites actually say what the brief claims? Read the primary source and log the verdict. That verification is the cycle continuing, not concluding.

Deliverable: the three harvested authorities, and one Research Log entry recording the claim as the brief made it, what the primary source actually says, and your verdict.

Self-check: your entry must quote or pincite both the brief’s characterization and the source’s own language. A verdict without both sides on the page is an impression, and impressions are what got the brief’s author into the habit you are checking.

Exercise 13.3 — The Rules Ladder #

For any federal district court (the one nearest you works well), trace a single filing requirement, page or word limits work well, down the ladder: the governing Federal Rule, the district’s local rule, and any judge-specific standing order. Note where each level lives and what each adds or tightens.

Deliverable: the three-rung ladder with citations and locations.

Self-check: if two rungs say the same thing, read again; local rules and standing orders exist to add or tighten, and finding the delta is the exercise. And where a judge has no standing order on the point, record that finding; knowing a rung is empty is knowing something.
