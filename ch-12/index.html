Chapter 12 Ethics and Professional Responsibility in Legal Research

3 interactives in the text

Legal research is a professional obligation, not just a technical skill. The quality of your research bears directly on your clients’ outcomes, the integrity of legal proceedings, and your standing as a licensed professional. A missed case is not just a lower grade. In practice it can be a forfeited argument, a sanctioned attorney, and a harmed client.

When an appellant provides no citation to legal authority supporting his contentions, those contentions are waived.

Shields v. Town of Perrysville, 136 N.E.3d 309, 312 n.2 (Ind. Ct. App. 2019).

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the professional responsibility rules that govern legal research obligations
  • Recognize the consequences of inadequate legal research for clients, courts, and counsel
  • Apply the duty of competence and the duty of candor to common research scenarios
  • Evaluate the ethical dimensions of using AI and emerging technologies in legal research
  • Describe the standards courts use when sanctioning attorneys for research failures

The Duty of Competence: Model Rule 1.1 #

ABA Model Rule of Professional Conduct 1.1 requires lawyers to provide competent representation, which includes “the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation reasonably necessary for the representation.” Comment 8 speaks directly to technology: a competent attorney must keep abreast of “changes in the law and its practice, including the benefits and risks of relevant technology.”

Competent legal research means knowing which sources govern, locating them efficiently, reading and synthesizing them accurately, and updating them to confirm they are still valid. Failing to find controlling authority is a breach of the duty of competence, not merely an oversight. Relying on outdated or overruled authority can amount to incompetent representation. The duty demands the current, controlling answer. Recall the following excerpt from Chapter 5.

[H]e did not conduct legal research beyond [an older decision on the issue], nor did he reread any of the cases cited to or relied on in . . . the most recent First Circuit decision addressing the contours of [the issue]. Thus, I conclude that [his] erroneous interpretation of the controlling law at the time constituted a breach of the professional duty.

ST Engineering Marine, Ltd. v. Thompson, MacColl & Bass, LLC, 633 F. Supp. 3d 354, 361 (D. Me. 2022), aff’d, No. 22-1844 (1st Cir. Dec. 6, 2023) [pull the bound F.4th cite at the check].

This case shows the core competence failure: the attorney relied on outdated authority without checking whether the law had moved. The closing-out protocol’s second step—updating every case and statute through a citator (Chapter 9)—is designed to prevent exactly this. And the finding held up: the First Circuit affirmed, so the competence holding is not one trial court’s view of how much research is enough. The habit the method builds is the same habit the rule requires.

The Duty of Candor Toward the Tribunal: Model Rule 3.3 #

Rule 3.3 prohibits false statements of law to a tribunal and requires disclosure of directly adverse controlling authority the opposing party has not cited. That creates an affirmative research obligation: you must search for controlling adverse authority, not only authority that helps you. Rule 3.3 also prohibits citing false or fabricated authority, a concern of heightened urgency in the era of AI-generated research.

The rule reaches authority that is in the controlling jurisdiction (not just persuasive authority from elsewhere), directly adverse (not merely unfavorable or tangential), and known to the lawyer, which includes authority you should have found through competent research. Ignorance born of inadequate research is no defense. The duty of candor therefore reaches back into the quality of your research. You cannot disclose adverse authority you never found, and “I didn’t find it” is no answer when competent research would have.

Sort it Does Rule 3.3 require you to tell the court?

  • A decision of your state’s supreme court squarely against your position. Opposing counsel has not cited it. Disclose
    • Controlling jurisdiction, directly adverse, not disclosed by the other side: every condition is met.
  • A neighboring state’s supreme court decision squarely against your position. No duty under the rule
    • Not from the controlling jurisdiction. You may still choose to address it before your opponent does.
  • A controlling decision that criticizes arguments like yours in passing while deciding a different issue. No duty under the rule
    • Unfavorable is not the same as directly adverse.
  • A controlling, directly adverse decision that opposing counsel has already cited and discussed. No duty under the rule
    • The court has it. Your job now is to distinguish it honestly.
  • A directly adverse decision of the intermediate appellate court whose decisions bind the trial court you are in. Opposing counsel missed it. Disclose
    • It controls the tribunal you are before. That your opponent researched poorly does not relieve you.

The Duty to Supervise: Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3 #

Supervising attorneys are responsible for making sure subordinate lawyers and non-lawyer staff comply with the conduct rules. In research, that means senior attorneys must review delegated research for accuracy, completeness, and proper citation. The Research Log serves a professional function here. It documents what was searched, what was found, and why the researcher concluded the research was complete. If a supervisor questions your work, the log gives a complete, defensible record. If you are the supervisor, the log is what lets you actually evaluate a subordinate’s research rather than simply trusting it.

Sort it Which duty did the lawyer breach?

  • Relied on a twenty-year-old decision without checking whether it was still good law. 1.1 Competence
    • Stale authority is the core competence failure.
  • Found a controlling, directly adverse case the other side missed and left it out of the brief. 3.3 Candor
    • The research was competent. The filing was not candid.
  • Signed and filed a junior associate’s memo with no way to see what she searched or read. 5.1 and 5.3 Supervision
    • Delegated research no one can review. The Research Log is what makes review possible.
  • Adopted a new research tool for the office without learning how it produces its answers. 1.1 Competence
    • Competence includes understanding the benefits and risks of the technology you use.
  • Filed a paralegal’s case summaries without any attorney reading the cases. 5.1 and 5.3 Supervision
    • Responsibility for a nonlawyer’s work stays with the supervising lawyer.

Consequences of Inadequate Legal Research #

Sanctions by courts. Courts have broad sanctioning authority when counsel fails to research adequately. Sanctions include monetary penalties, attorney fee awards, adverse rulings, and public reprimands in published opinions.

[B]ecause I find that Defendants incurred these unnecessary fees in major part due to inadequate legal research on the part of Plaintiffs’ counsel, I find that both Plaintiffs and their counsel are jointly and severally liable to Defendants.

Royal Oak Ent., L.L.C. v. City of Royal Oak, 486 F. Supp. 2d 675, [pincite] (E.D. Mich. 2007).

Waiver of arguments. Courts routinely hold that arguments unsupported by citation to legal authority are waived. Inadequate research does not merely weaken an argument. It can forfeit it entirely, as the Shields court’s note at the opening of this chapter makes plain.

Malpractice exposure. An attorney who fails to find controlling authority and thereby harms a client’s case may face a legal malpractice claim. The competence failure that begins as a research shortcut can end as a civil judgment against the lawyer.

AI and Emerging Technology Considerations #

[A]t this point, no lawyer should be using ChatGPT or any other generative AI product to perform research without verifying the results. Period.

In re Martin, 670 B.R. 636, 649 (Bankr. N.D. Ill. 2025).

Generative AI tools produce output that can look authoritative but may contain fabricated case citations, incorrect legal propositions, and misattributed quotations. Courts have sanctioned attorneys for submitting briefs citing AI-generated, nonexistent cases. The professional responsibility implications are clear: the duty of competence requires verification of all research, whatever its source. AI tools do not relieve attorneys of the obligation to confirm that cited authorities exist, say what they are claimed to say, and remain valid law.

Emerging court rules and bar guidance increasingly require disclosure when AI tools are used to generate any part of a brief or memorandum. Watch your jurisdiction’s developing rules. Chapter 15 addresses AI tools and legal research in depth; this section supplies the ethical framework that governs their use.

Read closely Pull every citation before you sign

A residential landlord must provide adequate heat during the cold months. Marsh v. Golden Rentals. A tenant left without heat may withhold rent at once, without notifying the landlord. Prewitt v. Stonegate LLC. And a tenant who goes thirty days without heat is entitled to treble damages. Hollis v. Ironwood Residential.

Build Verification Into Your Research Workflow #

As you research, keep a running citation log: every source you intend to cite, with its full citation and the database where you verified it. Before you finalize any document, run every case citation through KeyCite or Shepard’s to confirm that the points of law you cite it for remain valid. Before you finalize any statute citation, verify the current text against the eCFR or the official code and check for pending amendments. If you used any AI tool at any stage, independently verify each source it identified before it goes into your work product. These are not separate ethics chores bolted onto research. They are the closing steps of the Question Method, performed with the awareness that the rules of professional conduct require them.

Rule The research failure it targets The method step that prevents it
Rule 1.1 (competence) Stale or overruled authority; citations that do not support the claim they are offered for; inadequate or no research at all The citator update and the read-before-you-cite check in the closing-out protocol
Rule 3.3 (candor) A one-sided search that misses adverse authority The adverse-authority search in the closing-out protocol’s final sweep (Chapter 9)
Rules 5.1 and 5.3 (supervision) Delegated research no one can review The Research Log as the reviewable record

Common Pitfalls #

The competence failures that get lawyers sanctioned tend to share a shape: relying on the first answer rather than the current, controlling one. Leaning on an old case without running it through a citator is the classic version, and ST Eng’g Marine is what it looks like in a published opinion. A second pitfall is researching only your own side; Rule 3.3 obligates you to find and disclose directly adverse controlling authority, so a one-sided search is not just weak advocacy, it is an ethics problem. A third is trusting AI output without verification, since a fabricated citation is a Rule 3.3 violation whether it came from a chatbot or anywhere else. And do not treat the Research Log as busywork; it is the record that protects you and your supervisor if the quality of the research is ever questioned.

Chapter Recap #

Research quality is an ethical obligation with names attached. Rule 1.1 demands competent, current research; Rule 3.3 demands candor about adverse authority; Rules 5.1 and 5.3 make supervisors answerable for research they delegate. Courts enforce all of it through sanctions, waived arguments, and malpractice exposure, and AI has raised the stakes on verification rather than lowering them. And here is the good news: the citator step, the adverse-authority question, the Research Log—the habits this book has been building in you all along—are exactly the habits the rules require.

Key Takeaways

  • Legal research is a professional obligation, not merely a technical skill.
  • Model Rule 1.1 (competence) and Model Rule 3.3 (candor) are the primary rules governing legal research.
  • Courts sanction attorneys for inadequate research; arguments unsupported by authority may be waived.
  • AI tools create heightened verification obligations; they do not substitute for professional research judgment.
  • Supervising attorneys are responsible for the research quality of their subordinates.
  • The Research Log documents your process; it is your professional record of diligence.

Reviewing Through Practice #

The following exercises test your ability to connect the professional conduct rules to specific research practices: verification, adverse-authority searching, and documentation.

Exercise 12.1 — The Sanctions Autopsy #

Pull ST Engineering Marine; the competence excerpt in this chapter gives you the citation. Read the court’s account of what the attorney failed to do, and then rebuild this chapter’s closing table from the opinion itself rather than from memory: for each failure the court names, write the rule it violates and the method step that would have prevented it.

Deliverable: your rebuilt three-column table, failure, rule, preventing step, with a pincite to the opinion for each failure.

Self-check: compare your table against the chapter’s. Where they differ, the difference is the finding: either you caught a failure the chapter’s table generalized over, which is good reading, or you attributed to the opinion something it does not say, which is the exact error the autopsy exists to catch.

Exercise 12.2 — The Adverse-Authority Question #

Return to any completed drill from Part I and run the one question your first pass almost certainly skipped: what controlling authority cuts against my conclusion? Draft the question, run the search, and log the result in your Research Log even if the search comes back empty, because Rule 3.3 does not accept “I didn’t look” as an answer.

Deliverable: the Research Log entry, including the searches run.

Self-check: an empty result logged with its searches is a passing answer. An empty log is not. The difference between those two is the whole of the candor duty’s research obligation.

Exercise 12.3 — The Verification Pass #

Run a full verification pass on any completed drill from Part I: build the citation log, one line per source you would cite, run every case through a citator, and confirm every statute and regulation’s currency against the official or current source.

Deliverable: the citation log with a verification status for every line.

Self-check: every line has a status and a date. The log you produce is not homework. It is the record that answers for you when someone asks whether the research was done, and Rules 5.1 and 5.3 mean someone is allowed to ask.
