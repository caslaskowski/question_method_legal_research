Chapter 2’s first rule: if you already have a citation, you do not search, you retrieve, using only the location parts. Sort each moment.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Address in hand, or not yet?" bins="Retrieve | Search"}
- The assigning memo cites A.R.S. § 12-821.01 **Retrieve**
    - Title and section are an address. Type them in and pull the section.
- A treatise footnote gives 484 U.S. 260 **Retrieve**
    - Volume, reporter, page. Enter those three parts, nothing else.
- Your client asks whether a landlord must provide heat **Search**
    - No citation yet. This is a question, and questions are searched (or, better, taken to a secondary source first).
- You remember a case about a coach praying after football games but not its name or cite **Search**
    - A memory of facts is not an address. A terms-and-connectors or natural language search finds the citation; then you retrieve.
- A case you are reading cites 28 C.F.R. § 36.303(a) **Retrieve**
    - A regulation citation is an address in the CFR. Pull it directly, in the eCFR if you like.
- You want every Ninth Circuit case on remote work as an ADA accommodation **Search**
    - A set of unknown documents, defined by concepts. Build the search with synonyms joined by OR and concepts joined by AND.
- Opposing counsel’s brief cites Robles v. Domino’s Pizza, 913 F.3d 898 **Retrieve**
    - Use 913 F.3d 898. Leave the party names out; they can interfere with a clean retrieval.
- You need to know whether “fired,” “terminated,” or “discharged” is the term courts use **Search**
    - That is a vocabulary question. A natural language search, or a secondary source, surfaces the terms of art before you build the precise search.
:::
