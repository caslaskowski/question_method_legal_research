Court documents are research tools, not authority. Sort each need by the document that serves it.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Which document?" bins="Complaint | Brief | Amicus brief | Order or opinion | The docket sheet itself | Not a court document"}
- How have lawyers framed this legal theory in similar cases? **Complaint**
    - Complaints show how claims are pleaded and the facts marshaled.
- What is the full range of arguments on this contested point, with the leading authorities? **Brief**
    - A well-researched brief hands you the leading authorities in minutes. Each is a lead to verify.
- What perspectives do non-parties bring to the issue? **Amicus brief**
    - Amicus filings canvass the wider debate, especially in landmark cases.
- How did the court reason through a procedural question that never reached a published opinion? **Order or opinion**
    - Orders resolving motions sometimes carry more reasoning than any published decision.
- What has been filed, in what sequence, and when? **The docket sheet itself**
    - The docket is the official log of filings. Read it as a map before you open anything.
- What is the controlling rule in my jurisdiction? **Not a court document**
    - A brief tells you what one side argued, not what the law is. The rule comes from primary authority.
- Did the lawyer in Mata v. Avianca ever check whether the cases were real, and what did the court require afterward? **The docket sheet itself**
    - The order to show cause, the affidavit, the hearing transcript, and the required letters are all docket entries.
- Has this leading case been appealed or granted certiorari? **The docket sheet itself**
    - A docket search can surface a challenge before any citator flag appears.
:::
