Good researchers do not work by the seat of their pants, even when it looks that way from the outside. They have run the planning and the process so many times that the steps have gone underground, below conscious thought. This book digs those steps back up so you can learn them on purpose.

Here is what skilled researchers actually do. They ask a question. They find an authoritative source that answers it. They write the answer down in their own words. Then they look at that answer and notice the next gap in what they know, and that gap becomes the next question. Then they do it again. The question drives the whole cycle. The sources are just where you stop along the way.\ Most legal research textbooks are really source guides. They tell you what databases exist, how to read an annotated code, how to run a citator check. That is useful, and you will find all of it in this book too. But most of those texts treat the research question as a planning step, something you settle before you begin and then file away once the “real” research starts.

That framing leaves out the most important part. It also blurs two different questions. The question those texts have you settle up front is the big one, the legal issue your finished document must resolve. The questions that drive the actual research are smaller, and there are many of them. Experts research in loops. Each answer raises a new question. Each source exposes a new gap. The inquiry keeps narrowing and deepening until it runs out of questions to ask. When this book says research question, it means those smaller, moving questions; the Introduction lays out the full vocabulary. This is also why you cannot fully plan a research question in advance when you are working in an unfamiliar area of law. You do not yet know which facts matter, or which sources will help, because you do not yet know what the law requires. Relevance is something the cycle uncovers as you go. You cannot load it in at the start.

The Question Method is built on that idea. It puts the iterative question at the center of how you learn legal research, ahead of the database, the source type, and the Bluebook. You will learn to ask a question, find and synthesize an answer, write it down, and ask a sharper question next. You will still learn each of the legal research sources thoroughly, and I would argue you will come out knowing them better, because each chapter shows you how a particular source answers a particular kind of question. A case might give you the elements of a tort. A statute might hand you the definition of a term you have been chasing. A regulation will tell you how to certify produce as organic. A law review article might flag an emerging issue your lobbying clients need you to see coming.

The book also tries to make the invisible visible. An experienced researcher makes dozens of small decisions every session. Which source do I check next? When do I stop reading this case and move on? Is this result actually useful, or does it just look useful? Has this promising lead turned into a dead end? Those decisions are hard to teach precisely because the people who are best at them have stopped noticing they make them. If you already have some research experience, you may make a few of these calls on instinct, and some of what I ask you to do here will feel like a step backward. Take the step anyway. The granularity is scaffolding, and scaffolding comes down. Work every step explicitly now, and you will compress the chain later, the way every expert eventually does, knowing exactly which steps you are skipping because you once performed each one on purpose. If you are new to this, the book will help you build that judgment one deliberate move at a time. Work through it carefully, and you will come away with more than a map of where the law lives. You will have a practiced way of thinking about how to find the law, how to use it, and how to know when you are finished.
