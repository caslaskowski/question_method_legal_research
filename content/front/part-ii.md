Part I introduced the process. Part II provides the knowledge to apply everything you have learned to more complex and varied legal research processes. The sources get more specialized, the contexts more demanding, and the stakes more explicitly professional.

Part II opens where Part I left off, with statutes and regulations. Chapter 10 takes up legislative history, the record of what a legislature said while it was writing the law. Chapter 11 takes up the materials agencies produce beyond the regulations themselves, including where agency interpretations stand after *Loper Bright*. Both answer the question Chapters 6 and 7 leave behind: the text is on the page, so what does it mean in practice? Each follows the familiar template, so you will know exactly what to ask of it.

Chapter 12 then turns to the rules that govern all of it. Research quality is a professional obligation with rules attached, and lawyers are sanctioned for failing it. The chapter shows that the verification habits, the citator checks, and the Research Log as a reviewable record are the method’s own closing steps, performed with the rules of professional conduct in view. Read it as the frame for everything else in Part II, and return to it whenever the stakes rise. Chapter 13 adds the last source most first-year courses never reach: dockets and court documents.

Chapters 14 through 17 then shift from sources to contexts. Litigation, transactional, and compliance practice each bend the method to their own rhythms. Generative AI tools demand the cycle’s verification discipline more than any source before them. The NextGen bar exam tests the skills this book has been naming all along. And scholarship hands you a new kind of macro question, one where the thesis is the answer your paper exists to defend.

Unlike Part I, this part does not need to be read straight through. After Chapter 11, take the chapters as your coursework or your practice demands them, but read Chapter 12 before you rely on any of them in practice.
