A few years ago, in the middle of a legal research class, a student asked me a question that changed how I teach. We had spent the session on secondary sources: how to find them, how to navigate a table of contents, how to scan a section’s footnotes for the citations that point to cases on your issue. We had even worked through a passage together and flagged the footnotes worth chasing. Then the student looked at the half-dozen cases those footnotes pointed to and asked, “But which one do I read first?”

It was a simple question. It was also exactly the right one, because it exposed a gap, not in what the student understood, but in what I had taught. I had shown them how to find a secondary source, how to navigate it, and how to spot the passages whose footnotes led somewhere useful. I had not shown them how to decide which lead to follow first. I had assumed that choice was obvious. It is not, and it happens to be the judgment that separates a novice from an expert. Handed six plausible citations, a novice reads all of them and drowns, or picks one at random and hopes. An expert reads the one that answers the question they are holding right now. The footnotes do not put themselves in order. The researcher’s current question orders them. Knowing which gap to close next, and therefore which lead to chase first, is the skill that source-guide instruction tends to leave out. This textbook is what happened when I took that realization seriously.

## The Question Method in Brief

The Question Method is a structured, repeating approach to legal research. The cycle is simple. You define the question you are working on right now. You research to answer it. You write down what you found. Based on what you found, you identify the next question. Then you run the cycle again, and you keep going until no real question is left. None of this is new. It is what skilled researchers already do. The method just names each move so you can learn it on purpose instead of hoping to absorb it by osmosis.

Naming each move is what makes it teachable. Because every step has a name, you can watch yourself do it, and you can catch yourself when it goes wrong. When the research starts to feel unproductive, you can stop and ask, “What kind of question am I actually asking right now?” More often than not you will find the question is poorly scoped, pointed at the wrong source, or already answered. Naming the moves also answers my student’s question. When a source hands you several leads at once, the one to follow first is the one that answers your current question. The rest become later questions, which you write down and come back to in turn. (Appendix A returns to this exact moment when it shows you how to work a secondary source’s footnotes.) The method threads that discipline through every stage of research and writing, so the judgment an expert applies without thinking becomes a habit you build on purpose.

## One Issue, Many Questions

Before the method gets to work, three terms need pinning down, because the rest of this book leans on them. The [legal issue]{.gloss} is the problem the law must resolve, the reason the file landed on your desk. The [macro question]{.gloss} is that issue framed as the single big question your document exists to answer: the question presented in a memo, the issue on appeal in a brief, the thesis of a seminar paper. A [research question]{.gloss} is the micro question you ask and answer along the way, one of the many subquestions whose answers, assembled, answer the macro question. When this book says research question, it always means the micro question.

The distinction matters because the macro question is a terrible research companion. If it is the only question you hold, every source has to be weighed against all of it at once, and every passage looks potentially relevant, because everything ultimately bears on the macro question. That is not how brains process information. We work through one concept at a time. Nobody learns a dance routine by running the whole thing at every rehearsal. You drill it in sections, then you assemble the performance. Research questions are the sections.

This is also why it is not enough to write a research question at the planning stage and never revisit it. A researcher who does that is really carrying the macro question through the whole project, trying to process everything a source says all at once, and feeling buried by it. The method asks for the opposite discipline: write the macro question down where you can see it, hold exactly one research question at a time, and let each answer hand you the next. The macro question is the destination. The research questions are the steps.

## How to Use This Textbook

If you are in a first-year legal research course, read Part I straight through, in order. The method shows up in Chapter 3 and runs through every chapter after it. Start each chapter with its Chapter Objectives. They tell you what you should be able to do by the end, not just what you should know. The Research Log is the single most useful practical tool in this book. Use it for every exercise here, and eventually for every research task you take on in practice. The habit you build in the safety of a course is the same one that will protect you and your clients later.
