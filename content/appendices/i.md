The Reflection Points scattered through the chapters are meant to be sat with before they are checked; the thinking is the exercise, and these answers are deliberately short. Each is one defensible way to put the point, not the only way. They are keyed by chapter and location.

**Chapter 1 — after Binding vs. Persuasive Authority.** *When would a persuasive decision be worth more to your client than a binding one?* When your jurisdiction has not answered the question. Where binding law is silent or unsettled, the persuasive trend is the best available evidence of where your court will land, which is often the answer the client actually needs.

**Chapter 2 — after Practical Search Strategy.** *Why does the method have you draft the research question before you choose a database or a search syntax?* Because the question’s type is what tells you which source, and therefore which database and which search, fits. Start from the database instead and you will quietly reshape the question into whatever that tool answers well.

**Chapter 3 — after The Types in Motion.** *A botanist, a chef, and the Tariff Act of 1883 each answer “is a tomato a fruit?” differently. What does that tell you about where a legal definition’s authority comes from?* A definition is authoritative only for a purpose, and legal meaning takes its purpose from the enacting scheme, or, where the scheme is silent, from the rule courts use to fill the gap. Expertise about the thing defined is not authority about the word’s legal meaning.

**Chapter 3 — after Common Pitfalls.** *How might the research have been affected if the researcher had jumped straight to reading *Hazelwood* before reading *Tinker* and *Fraser*?* The researcher would have met the governing rule without the baseline it modifies. *Hazelwood* distinguishes *Tinker* and builds on *Fraser*’s premise that student rights are not coextensive with adults’ rights, so reading it first means taking the exception before the rule. “School-sponsored” and “legitimate pedagogical concerns” would have arrived as vocabulary with nothing to push against, and the researcher might not have known which sub-questions to ask. **Chapter 4 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *If secondary sources answer exploratory questions this well, why does Chapter 3’s demonstration stop consulting them after its first question?* Because they point rather than bind: once the landscape is mapped, authority has to come from the primary sources themselves. The demonstration uses the encyclopedia for the map and the opinions for every answer, which is the division of labor this chapter teaches.

**Chapter 5 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *Why might a case be a poor source for answering exploratory questions, especially no-clue questions?* An opinion answers the dispute in front of it, in the parties’ vocabulary, and assumes the doctrinal context rather than mapping it. A researcher who does not yet have the terms or the landscape cannot even find the right case, let alone learn the area from one.

**Chapter 6 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *Why might a statute be a poor source for answering a no-clue exploratory question, even when the area turns out to be statutory?* You cannot look up a provision without the vocabulary the code files it under, and a no-clue researcher does not yet have the terms of art, or even know whether the answer lives in a statute at all rather than in common law. And a located section states its rule bare, without the context or interpretation that makes the landscape legible.

**Chapter 7 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *Why might a regulation be a poor source for answering a no-clue exploratory question, even in a heavily regulated area?* Regulations sit downstream: you find them through the statute that authorized them and the agency that wrote them, both of which a no-clue researcher has not yet identified. They are also the most granular layer, drafted for parties who already know the scheme; they answer what exactly must be done, never what this area is.

**Chapter 8 — after The Statute-Regulation-Case Law Triangle.** *Chapters 4 through 7 walled you into one source type at a time. Why does that discipline make multi-source research easier rather than harder?* Because you now know each source’s native questions, crossing between them becomes a deliberate move the question’s type prompts, not a drift the database causes. The walls taught you what each room is for; this chapter hands you the doors.

**Chapter 9 — after The Closing-Out Protocol.** *Your annotated outline already answers the question. Why does the closing-out protocol still make you run a search aimed at authority that cuts against you?* Because an outline built from confirming searches can be complete and still wrong. The adverse-authority search tests the outline instead of extending it, and Rule 3.3 will eventually hold you to having run it (Chapter 12).

**Chapter 10 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *Why is legislative history a poor first stop even when your question is about what the legislature meant?* Because purpose questions only become answerable once the enacted text and its specific ambiguity are in hand; history read before the text is a haystack with no needle defined. The text is the law and the frame; the history is evidence about it, weighed only after the frame exists.

**Chapter 11 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *If guidance documents are not binding, why does this chapter treat finding them as a required step rather than an optional one?* Because the client’s question is usually what the regulation means in practice, and the answer often lives in guidance. Guidance tells you how the agency will enforce the rule, which is where the client’s exposure sits, and a court may weigh it under *Skidmore* in proportion to its reasoning. You must find it to give the practical answer, and you must label its authority correctly to give the legal one. Leave it out and the rule you hand the client is incomplete. **Chapter 12 — after The Duty to Supervise: Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3.** *The Research Log is a research tool. Why does this chapter also call it a professional record?* Because the rules judge process as well as result. A supervisor deciding whether delegated research was adequate, or a court asking whether counsel searched for adverse authority, can evaluate only what was written down. A log that lists the searches you ran, including the ones that found nothing, is evidence of diligence. Without it, you have the research but not the proof of it. **Chapter 13 — after Questions This Source Answers.** *A well-researched brief seems like a shortcut to everything at once. Why does the method still send you to dockets only after your question has narrowed?* A brief is one side’s argument, and its citations are only useful once you know what you are asking. With a narrow question in hand, you can tell which of its authorities bear on the point, and each becomes a candidate answer to verify against the primary source. Before that, you cannot sort advocacy from authority, and you may spend money and time on documents you are not yet equipped to evaluate. [\[Draft answer; edit freely\]]{.flag}

**Chapter 14 — after Practice-Specific Research and the Research Cycle.** *When the practice context changes, why does the closure criterion matter more than the question type or the first source?* Question type and first source change how you work. Closure decides when you stop, and stopping by the wrong measure is the costly error. “Controlling authority found for each issue” is the right finish line in litigation and the wrong one in a deal, where the question is whether every requirement and risk has been identified. In practice work an outside limit such as a deadline or a budget often forces the stop before you reach the analytical finish line, which is why the closure note, recording what remains, matters as much as the research. **Chapter 15 — after Generative AI Tools and the Research Cycle.** *A generative tool answers your narrow question and cites a case that looks perfect. What does the method have you do before the answer goes anywhere, and why does keeping the question narrow help?* Retrieve the case and read it for the proposition it is cited for, and log the answer only after it has been confirmed. The tool predicts what a good citation looks like; it cannot tell a real one from a plausible one. A narrow question yields one claim to check against one or two sources. A sprawling question yields dozens, and the cost of checking rises faster than the cost of asking. [\[Draft answer; edit freely\]]{.flag}

**Chapter 16 — after Five Core Research Skills.** *Which of the five skills does a single Question Method cycle already exercise, even with no bar exam in sight?* Nearly all of them, in small form. Defining a term you do not yet understand is ambiguity spotting. Weighing the source you found is source ranking. Reading a case for the facts that mattered is dispositive-fact identification. Writing the next question names a gap. Deciding the answer is good enough for now is a small sufficiency call. The exam compresses the cycle; the method has been rehearsing it. **Chapter 17 — after Mapping the Scholarly Landscape.** *Why run a one-hour preemption check before the full literature search rather than after it?* Because the check is cheap and a late discovery is expensive. An hour on the claim’s distinctive terms tells you whether the paper already exists before you have built an outline around it. A full map of the literature, drawn around an argument someone else has already made, is a semester spent in the wrong direction. The full sweep still comes later, and again before submission, but only for a claim that survived the cheap test. [\[Draft answer; edit freely\]]{.flag}
