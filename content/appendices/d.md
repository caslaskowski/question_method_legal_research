If reading a statute calls for close attention to text, reading a regulation calls for even more. Regulations tend to be more detailed, more technical, and more intricately structured than statutes, because their job is to translate a statute’s broad mandate into specific, enforceable requirements. The same reading principles apply (read in context, check definitions, follow cross-references, look for exceptions), but regulations carry a few extra features that deserve their own attention. As with every source in this book, reading one is answering the questions the source exists to answer: what is required, of whom, on what authority, and since when. Keep your current question in the foreground and the extra apparatus becomes a checklist rather than clutter.

## Components of a Regulatory Section

**Part Heading.** The general subject of the Part. Use it to navigate to related provisions.

**Authority Note.** Cites the statute or statutes that authorize the regulations in that Part. This is your link back to the enabling legislation, and it is the starting point for any argument that a regulation exceeds the authority Congress granted the agency.

**Source Note.** Cites the Federal Register issue where the regulation was first published or most recently amended. It is your pathway to the Federal Register preamble, where the agency explains what the regulation means.

**Definitions Section.** Usually at the start of a Part or Subpart. Regulatory definitions control over ordinary meaning, so always check.

**Regulatory Text.** The operative rules: what is required, prohibited, permitted, or conditioned.

**Cross-References.** Regulations cross-reference other sections even more often than statutes do. Follow every one.

**Tables, Appendices, and Figures.** These are part of the regulation and carry the force of law. Do not skip them, especially in environmental, workplace-safety, or financial regulation, where they often hold the specific numbers and thresholds that determine compliance.

## How to Engage With the Source

**Read in context, starting with the statute.** Begin with the authority note, because the enabling statute sets the limits of what the agency can require. Then read the Federal Register preamble for the agency’s own explanation of the regulation’s meaning. Check for case law interpreting the regulation (via citators) and for agency guidance interpreting it (Chapter 11 covers this in depth). Taken together these sources give you a complete interpretive picture; any one alone is partial.

**Read the operative language precisely.** “Shall” and “must” create mandatory obligations. “May” grants discretion. “Should” signals a recommendation. Courts enforce these distinctions, so they are worth slowing down for.

**Know whether you are reading a performance standard or a design standard.** A performance standard specifies an outcome; a design standard specifies a method. Which one you are reading determines what your client has to do to comply: meet the outcome any way it likes, or follow the prescribed method exactly.

**Follow incorporations by reference.** Regulations sometimes incorporate outside standards (ASTM, ANSI, ISO) by reference. The incorporated standard has the force of law even though its text never appears in the CFR, so “what does the incorporated standard require?” is a real research question, not a detail you can skip.

**Confirm currency before you rely on it.** Regulations change frequently. Verify you are reading the current version using the eCFR, the List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA), the Federal Register, and a citator. Stopping at the CFR text without checking for recent amendments is one of the most common regulatory research errors.
