## The Foster Mother as Bystander (Chapter 5)

**The scenario.** Dana has been the licensed foster parent of nine-year-old M. for the past three years. M. has lived in Dana’s home that entire time, and Dana is the only mother M. has known across those years. One afternoon, as the two walk to the corner store, a driver runs a red light and strikes and kills M. in front of Dana. Dana is not physically hurt, but the experience leaves her with severe, lasting emotional trauma. She wants to know whether she can recover from the driver. The jurisdiction is California.

**Where the researcher’s knowledge actually is.** Our researcher has heard that a person who witnesses a terrible accident can sometimes sue for the emotional harm, and has run across the phrase negligent infliction of emotional distress. That is the whole of it. She does not yet know the elements of the claim, she does not know there is a special rule about the witness’s relationship to the victim, and, this matters, she does not yet know that Dana’s status as a foster parent is the fact the whole case will turn on. She knows it as background, the way she knows M.’s age. Whether it is legally relevant is something the research has to tell her. We start, then, not by cataloguing facts, but by writing the first question we can actually write.

**Question 1 (Exploratory, orienting).** If you witness a negligent accident that injures someone else, can you sue for your own emotional distress?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** Yes. A secondary source on tort claims for emotional distress, an encyclopedia entry or a California practice guide, supplies the orientation: in California a person who suffers no physical injury can sometimes recover for the emotional distress of witnessing injury to another, under what the courts call a bystander theory of negligent infliction of emotional distress. (Notice the discipline: the question asked only whether such a claim is possible. The answer says yes, sometimes, and hands us the name. It does not yet list what the claim requires; that is the next question’s job, not this one’s.)

The claim exists and now has a name. A claim that succeeds only “sometimes” is a claim with requirements. The next question asks what they are.

**Question 2 (Focusing).** What must a bystander prove to recover for NIED in California?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** The secondary source’s citations point to the leading case, and you cross from secondary to primary authority to read it. Under *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 644, the plaintiff must show three things: (1) that she is closely related to the injury victim; (2) that she was present at the scene of the injury-producing event when it occurred and was then aware that it was injuring the victim; and (3) that she suffered serious emotional distress beyond what a disinterested witness would feel.

Three requirements, each a potential question of its own. Two are not in real doubt on these facts: Dana watched the collision happen (requirement 2), and a foster mother who sees her child killed plainly suffers distress beyond a stranger’s (requirement 3). The genuinely contested requirement is the first, whether a foster parent is “closely related” to her foster child. And here is the moment a fact becomes relevant: Dana’s foster status, a piece of background a moment ago, is now the pivot of the case, because the first requirement is the one that asks about it. This is the method’s central claim in miniature: we did not list “Dana is a foster parent, not a biological parent” as a relevant fact at the outset. It became relevant the instant the research surfaced a requirement that turns on it.

**Question 3 (Focusing / Definitional).** What relationship counts as “closely related to the injury victim”?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** *Thing* itself draws the line. Absent exceptional circumstances, it limits recovery to relatives residing in the same household, or to the victim’s parents, siblings, children, and grandparents, and it describes the qualifying relationships as those of blood or marriage. Two more decisions in the line, one *Thing* itself discusses and one surfaced by running *Thing* through a citator, apply the same boundary to relationships that fall outside it. In *Elden v. Sheldon* (1988) 46 Cal.3d 267, the court held that an unmarried cohabitant, a romantic partner living as a spouse, is not “closely related.” In *Moon v. Guardian Postacute Services* (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 1005, the court held that a son-in-law was not “closely related” to his mother-in-law even though he had lived with her and was close to her.

The decided cases sort relationships along a single line: blood or marriage. A foster parent has neither, no blood tie, no marital tie, to the foster child. But no case in this line decides a foster relationship. Note what that gap is and is not. The rule that governs Dana is articulated, *Thing* states it, and *Thing* itself leaves the door ajar with “absent exceptional circumstances.” What is missing is a decision applying the rule to this relationship. That recognition, that the relationships courts have addressed do not include this one, is itself a research finding, and the express exception is what makes the next question arguable at all: a hedged line invites the question of what the line is for.

**Question 4 (Application, fact-finding by analogy).** Given how California courts have drawn the “closely related” line, is a long-term foster parent “closely related” to her foster child?

**Answer, argued from the decided line, and honestly marked as open.** No California case decides this, so the answer cannot be looked up; it must be argued by analogy, and it remains genuinely unsettled. The synthesis goes like this. The decided cases protect relationships defined by blood or marriage, and the reason the court gives is administrability: a bright line that can be applied without inquiring case by case into how close any particular relationship really was, and that keeps a negligent defendant’s exposure predictable (*Thing*; *Elden*). And the categories that make the line administrable are not arbitrary: blood and marriage are statuses that already come bundled with legal rights each party holds in the other, which is what lets a court apply them at a glance. Read strictly, that line excludes a foster parent. Foster status is neither blood nor marriage, and *Elden* and *Moon* establish that deep affection and a shared household are not, by themselves, enough to cross it.

Yet the foster relationship differs from the relationships rejected in *Elden* and *Moon* in a way that may matter. It is not an informal arrangement; it is a formal, state-recognized, legally created caregiving relationship in which the foster parent functionally occupies the role of parent, the very relationship that sits at the center of *Thing*’s protected list. And *Thing* did not slam the door: it limited recovery “absent exceptional circumstances.” A lawyer for Dana would argue from what the line is for: the categories the court chose are exactly the statuses that come bundled with legal rights each party holds in the other, and foster parenthood is such a status, a formal, state-created relationship carrying legally imposed caregiving duties and recognized standing in the child’s life, so a multi-year foster parent-child relationship either falls within the principle of the protected parent/child category or is exactly the exceptional circumstance *Thing* left room for. The rejected relationships fail that principle, not just the list. An in-law relation fails it even though a marriage sits somewhere in the chain, because the marriage is the son-in-law’s to the daughter, not a marriage between the two of them, and the relation it creates affords neither any legal rights in the other: a mother-in-law could not sue for a son-in-law’s wrongful death and holds no rights or duties respecting him. And *Elden*’s cohabitants had, by definition, declined the legal status that would have vested them. A lawyer for the driver would answer that the entire point of *Thing* was to refuse this kind of functional, relationship-by-relationship inquiry, and that a line drawn at blood or marriage means what it says. That the question is genuinely open is not a guess: California practitioners describe whether a foster parent qualifies as “closely related” as unsettled and likely to be resolved by future case law.

**Closure.** The analysis is now as far along as the authority allows. The governing test is identified; the contested element is isolated; the decided relationships are synthesized into a blood-or-marriage line with a stated rationale and an “exceptional circumstances” hedge; and the foster-parent question is placed as genuinely open, with the argument available to each side laid out. More research would sharpen the wording of those arguments; it would not change the picture. This is analytical closure on a question that is open, and it is a legitimate stopping point. (If the assignment demanded a predicted outcome, the researcher would now make the best-supported prediction and flag the risk. That is an argument step, not another research question.)

**What this example is meant to teach.** Relevance was discovered, not pre-loaded: Dana’s foster status became the decisive fact only when Question 2 surfaced a requirement that turned on it, which a planning-first approach that front-loads every fact cannot teach. Every term of art arrived as an output: “bystander,” “closely related,” “blood or marriage,” each entered the chain only after an answer delivered it. The gap was a finding, not a dead end: discovering that no case addresses a foster parent is the result that pushes an ordinary fact-finding question to its analogical edge. And synthesis can yield an open answer: the skill in Question 4 is synthesizing the decided line and stating, fairly, the strongest argument on each side, and an express exception is an invitation to argue what the line is for, a rationale the parties will characterize differently, because manufacturing false certainty here would be the error, not the goal. One more thing students should not lose: the blood-or-marriage line buys administrative certainty, a real virtue, and it also means a person who has been a child’s only parent for years may have no remedy at all for watching that child die. Both halves are true at once, and a good researcher holds them together rather than letting the tidiness of the rule hide its cost.

## Borrowed Statutes and the Neighbor’s Answer (Chapter 5)

Sometimes your jurisdiction has no law on the precise question, but a neighboring state does, and the two states are not strangers. Your state borrowed the governing statute from that neighbor, the frameworks run in parallel, and your courts have a habit of reading the borrowed provisions the way the neighbor’s courts read them. When that is the situation, the neighbor’s resolution carries far more weight than an ordinary out-of-state opinion. It is unusually strong predictive authority, for a reason with a name: under the prior-construction (or “adopted-statute”) canon, a legislature that adopts a statute from another jurisdiction is presumed to take it with the construction the source jurisdiction’s courts had already placed on it. The researcher’s job is to recognize the silence, find the neighbor, and then weigh, carefully, how much that neighbor’s answer is really worth.

**The scenario.** A client in Nevada has been sued for defamation over things they posted in a closed, invitation-only online forum, a private professional listserv with a few dozen members. The client wants to file an anti-SLAPP special motion to dismiss, arguing the posts were protected speech on a matter of public concern. The threshold question is whether posts in a small, closed forum like this one count as protected activity under Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute.

**Where the researcher’s knowledge is.** The researcher knows the client wants to use Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute and has a rough sense that such statutes give defendants an early way out of speech-based suits. That is the starting point. The researcher does not yet know the statute’s structure, does not yet know that Nevada modeled the statute on California’s, and, importantly, has no reason yet to care about California at all. Whether California matters is something the research will reveal, and only once Nevada turns up empty.

**Question 1 (Focusing).** What must a defendant show to prevail on a Nevada anti-SLAPP special motion to dismiss?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** Nevada’s anti-SLAPP statute (NRS 41.635-41.670) sets up a two-step special motion. First, the defendant must show the claim is based on a protected “good faith communication in furtherance of the right to petition or the right to free speech in direct connection with an issue of public concern.” If the defendant carries that burden, the second step shifts to the plaintiff to show a probability of prevailing on the claim.

The motion turns first on whether the client’s posts are protected. The relevant protected category here is speech on a matter of public concern made in a public forum. So the next question is whether posts in this particular forum qualify, and here the facts about where and to whom the client spoke start to matter.

**Question 2 (Focusing / Application, deeper).** Does Nevada treat statements made in a small, closed, invitation-only online forum as protected speech “in direct connection with an issue of public concern” in a “public forum”?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** Neither the statute nor any Nevada appellate decision addresses this specific kind of forum. Nevada’s courts have resolved some forum questions at the other end of the spectrum, a message broadcast to a roughly 50,000-person listserv, posts on a public neighborhood app, but not a small, closed group. On this precise application, Nevada law is silent: it is a question of first impression here.

When the home jurisdiction has not spoken, the next question is whose reading of this kind of provision a Nevada court would be most likely to follow. Note what just happened: until Nevada turned up empty, there was no reason to look anywhere else. The silence is what makes the next question worth asking.

**Question 3 (Focusing, choosing the persuasive authority).** Whose construction of this kind of provision would a Nevada court find most persuasive?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** Two findings. First, California has a large, developed body of law on what counts as a “public forum” and an “issue of public interest” for anti-SLAPP purposes, reaching limited- and closed-audience settings as well as public ones. Second, Nevada did not write its anti-SLAPP statute on a blank slate: it modeled the statute on California’s (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16); it amended the statute in 2015 to require plaintiffs to meet “the same burden of proof” required under California’s anti-SLAPP law as of June 8, 2015 (NRS 41.665(2)); and the Nevada Supreme Court has repeatedly adopted California’s framework, including a set of public-interest considerations drawn directly from California’s appellate decisions.

Because Nevada borrowed the statute from California and its courts read the parallel provisions the way California does, California’s resolution of this kind of forum question is unusually strong persuasive authority, far stronger than an out-of-state case would ordinarily be. Notice the terms that just arrived: persuasive authority, the borrowed-statute relationship, the prior-construction canon. None was assumed at the start; each surfaced only after the prior answer made it relevant.

**Question 4 (Focusing, the neighbor’s rule, with the parallelism check).** What is California’s rule for a forum like this one?

**Answer, scoped to the question.** \[Illustrative; research would supply the precise holding.\] California’s courts analyze “public forum” and “public interest” through a developed set of factors, and research would show how they have treated a closed, limited-membership setting like this one. On parallelism, a caution: Nevada and California share the public-forum/public-interest framework, but their statutes are not identical. Nevada additionally requires the defendant to show the communication was truthful or made without knowledge of its falsehood, a requirement California’s statute does not impose, and the Nevada Supreme Court has expressly noted points of divergence (see *Kosor v. Olympia Cos.*). Before importing California’s answer, the researcher must confirm that the divergence does not touch the public-forum question.

**Question 5 (Constructive application, predicting the home-state rule).** Given the borrowing relationship and the parallel framework, how would a Nevada court likely resolve this application?

**Answer, constructed from the relationship, and marked as a prediction.** The strongest prediction is that a Nevada court would follow California’s construction of the public-forum/public-interest requirement, for two reinforcing reasons: the prior-construction canon and the Nevada Supreme Court’s demonstrated practice of reading these provisions in step with California. But the prediction comes with limits the researcher should state plainly. Persuasive is not binding: a Nevada court remains free to part ways, and Nevada’s own decisions and statutory differences come first. Timing affects weight: the adopted-statute presumption is strongest for California constructions that predated Nevada’s adoption (and its 2015 amendment pegged to California law as of a fixed date); a later California decision is persuasive but not presumptively adopted, so its date matters to how much it is worth. And divergence narrows the borrowing: where Nevada’s statute departs from California’s, the truth/good-faith element is the clearest example, California authority carries little weight.

**Closure.** The Nevada framework is identified; the gap is established as a question of first impression; the most persuasive authority is located and weighted through the borrowing relationship; and the predicted rule, with its confidence qualified, is stated. That is analytical closure on a first-impression question. This is not a hypothetical phenomenon, either: in May 2026, the Nevada Supreme Court resolved two previously unsettled anti-SLAPP questions in *Rosenbrook v. Lloyd*, 142 Nev., Adv. Op. 36, and on one of them California had worked out its approach years earlier, which is part of why the answer was foreseeable. A researcher facing that question before Rosenbrook would have run essentially this chain, and would have predicted the result Nevada in fact reached.

## ADA Website Accessibility (Chapter 8)

**Research Question:** Must a business make its website accessible to persons with disabilities under the ADA?

**Step 1, Secondary Source (Orientation).** Begin with *Am. Jur. 2d* or a disability law treatise. The secondary source identifies 42 U.S.C. § 12182 (Title III, public accommodations) as the key statutory provision and notes that courts are divided on whether websites count as “public accommodations.” Next question: does Title III apply to websites?

**Step 2, Statute (Primary Framework).** Read 42 U.S.C. § 12182. The text does not mention websites, because it was enacted in 1990. The annotations show many cases divided between circuits. Next question: what do the DOJ’s regulations say?

**Step 3, Regulation (Implementing Rules).** Review 28 C.F.R. Part 36 (DOJ’s ADA Title III regulations). No specific web-accessibility regulation exists, though the DOJ has issued guidance documents and proposed regulations. That surfaces two questions, logged and taken one at a time: what does the DOJ’s guidance say? And, after it, what weight do courts give it?

**Step 4, Case Law (Judicial Interpretation).** Find a leading circuit decision on ADA website accessibility. Use headnotes and key numbers for “Public Accommodations, Disability, Website” to locate additional cases. Check the citator for pending Supreme Court consideration. Note the circuit split.

Step 5, Synthesize. Your annotated outline now holds the statutory framework, the current regulatory gap, the agency guidance and its weight, and the state of the case law including the circuit split. You can now write an accurate, complete analysis of a genuinely contested question. Notice that each step’s “next question” pointed to a different source type. That is multi-source research working as designed.
