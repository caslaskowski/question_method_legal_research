Reading statutory text is a different skill from reading case law. Opinions explain their reasoning; statutes do not. A statute tells you what the rule is without telling you why. It does not walk you through hypotheticals. It states the law in the most compressed, precise language the legislature could manage and leaves the interpretive work to the courts and to you. Every word carries weight. Every comma may matter. Every cross-reference pulls in another rule. The Question Method’s premise holds here with full force: a statute exists to answer the question what does the law require, and reading it well means interrogating the text with the sub-questions this appendix walks through, one defined term, exception, and cross-reference at a time.

## Components of a Statutory Section

**Section Number and Heading.** Every section has a number (for example, 42 U.S.C. § 1983) and a descriptive heading. The heading gives you a general sense of the subject, but headings are sometimes added by codifiers rather than the legislature, and courts generally treat them as non-binding descriptions rather than operative text.

**Operative Text.** The binding law: the substantive rule that creates rights, imposes obligations, prohibits conduct, or grants authority. Every word here matters. “*No employer*,” who counts as an employer? “*Shall discriminate*,” what counts as discrimination? “*Because of*,” what causal standard applies? Each of those is its own Question Method cycle.

**Definitions.** Many statutes include a definitions section that controls how key terms are used throughout. A statutory definition overrides ordinary meaning, so always check whether a term you are reading is defined elsewhere in the act.

**Structural Provisions.** Scope and applicability clauses, general rules, specific obligations, exceptions, enforcement and penalty provisions, and effective dates. Knowing where a provision sits in that structure tells you how it interacts with the rest. Give exception language particular respect: phrases like “except that,” “provided, however,” and “unless” carry as much weight as the rule itself, because a client whose conduct falls within an exception is not subject to the statute no matter how squarely the general rule seems to apply.

**Cross-References.** Statutes refer to other sections constantly. Each cross-reference incorporates additional rules, so follow every one rather than reading a section in isolation.

**History and Credits.** Found at the end of the statutory text or in the annotations, the history notes tell you when the section was enacted and how it has been amended over time. They also supply the Public Law number and Statutes at Large citation you will need if an ambiguity sends you into legislative history research (Chapter 12).

## How to Engage With the Source

**Read in context.** A single subsection rarely tells the whole story. Read it alongside the definitions, the exceptions, and the cross-referenced provisions before you conclude what it requires.

**Anticipate how courts will read it using the canons of construction.** When text is ambiguous, courts rely on established interpretive principles. Knowing the common ones helps you predict the reading a court is likely to adopt, and therefore which interpretation to build your analysis around.

- **Plain Meaning Rule.** If the text is unambiguous, courts apply it as written; legislative history and policy arguments do not override clear text.
- **Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius.** The expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others. If a statute lists specific items, courts may infer that unlisted items are excluded.
- **Ejusdem Generis.** When a general term follows a list of specific terms, the general term covers only things similar to the specific ones. “Cars, trucks, motorcycles, and other vehicles” probably does not include airplanes.
- **Rule Against Surplusage.** Every word should be given effect; courts avoid readings that make any word meaningless.
- **In Pari Materia.** Statutes on the same subject are read together and interpreted consistently.
- **Rule of Lenity.** Ambiguous criminal statutes are read in favor of the defendant.

**Spot ambiguity and turn it into your next question.** Finding ambiguity is not a failure of your research; it is a core research skill. Common types include undefined terms, vague standards (“reasonable,” “substantial,” “undue,” “appropriate”), terms of art whose legal meaning differs from everyday meaning, structural ambiguity where the grammar allows more than one reading, and scope ambiguity about what falls inside or outside the statute. Each ambiguity becomes a distinct next question pointing to a distinct source.

**Confirm the law by going outside the text.** When you hit an ambiguity, ask: Have courts in your jurisdiction interpreted this term? Does the legislative history address it (Chapter 12)? Has the implementing agency issued guidance or regulations interpreting it (Chapters 7 and 13)? Each is a separate next question. Then make sure the statute is current; statutory text changes, so confirm you are reading the version in force for your matter.
