Secondary sources are the ones that talk *about* the law rather than *being* the law: encyclopedias, treatises, Hornbooks, Restatements, ALR annotations, practice guides, and law review articles. They are usually where a research project should start, especially in an unfamiliar area, because a good secondary source hands you the landscape and the citations to the primary authority all at once. The thing to keep in mind while you read one is that it is a map, not the territory. You use it to find and understand the law, but with rare exceptions you do not cite it as the law itself. And reading one is the method you have been practicing, applied to a page. Every source exists to answer certain kinds of questions. A secondary source exists to answer the exploratory and definitional questions that open a project, so you read it holding the question you brought.

## Components of a Secondary Source

**Finding Aids.** The table of contents and index are how you locate the right discussion inside a long work. Learning to navigate them quickly is half the skill of using treatises and encyclopedias well.

**The Narrative Text.** The author’s explanation of the doctrine: how the area is structured, what the elements and tests are, where the debates lie, and which terms of art you need to know. This is what orients you and supplies the vocabulary your later questions will require.

**Footnotes and Citations.** The most valuable part for a researcher. The footnotes point you to the primary authority (the cases, statutes, and regulations) that the narrative is describing. A single well-placed footnote can hand you the leading case on your issue.

**Currency and Supplementation Information.** Print treatises and encyclopedias update through pocket parts and supplements; online versions show a “current through” date. Knowing how a source updates, and when it was last updated, tells you how far to trust it before you verify against primary authority.

**Editorial Apparatus.** Cross-references to related sections, research references to other secondary sources, and (in annotated works like ALR) summaries organized by jurisdiction or by factual scenario.

## How to Engage With the Source

**Match the source type to your question type.** A no-clue exploratory question wants an encyclopedia. A foundational question wants a treatise. A survey question across jurisdictions wants an ALR annotation, a Restatement, a CRS report, or a 50-state survey. Choosing the right secondary source for the question you are holding is the first efficiency the method buys you.

**Navigate, do not read end to end.** Use the table of contents and index to jump to the relevant section. You are looking for the discussion that answers your current question, not trying to absorb the whole treatise.

**Mine the footnotes for primary authority, in the order your question dictates.** This is where my student’s question comes back. When a passage’s footnotes point to half a dozen cases, you do not read all six. You read the one that answers the question you are holding right now, and you log the rest as later questions with their citations noted. The narrative tells you what the law is; the footnotes tell you where to confirm it.

**Confirm against primary authority, and check currency.** A secondary source can be out of date, can oversimplify, or can simply be wrong. Use it to find and understand the law, then go read the primary authority it points to and verify the rule yourself. Note the source’s “current through” date or check its pocket part, because doctrine described in an older edition may since have changed.

**Cite primary authority, not the secondary source.** With narrow exceptions (a Restatement section a court has adopted, a leading treatise on an unsettled point), you build your argument on the primary authority the secondary source led you to, not on the secondary source itself.
