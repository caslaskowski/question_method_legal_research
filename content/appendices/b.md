Reading a judicial opinion is harder than it sounds, and it helps to say that out loud. Opinions are written by judges for other lawyers and judges, who are assumed to already know the terminology, the procedural context, and the analytical conventions. The disorientation you feel at first is normal, and it passes. Once you can recognize the parts, you can move through any opinion efficiently, reading for the components that answer your question and moving quickly past the rest. That is the Question Method restated for opinions. Every part of an opinion answers a question: what was the dispute, what rule resolved it, why, and which facts the resolution turned on. Only one of those is usually the question you brought. Bring your current research question to every opinion, and let it tell you which part deserves the close read.

## Components of a Judicial Opinion

**Caption.** The case name, court, date, and citation at the top of the opinion. It tells you who is suing whom, which court decided the case, and when. In appellate opinions, the first-named party is usually, though not always, the appellant (the party who lost below and is asking the higher court to reverse). The citation tells you where to find the opinion in a reporter.

**Procedural History.** How the case got to the court writing the opinion. Was it appealed from a trial court? Is this a ruling on a motion to dismiss, a summary judgment decision, or an appeal after a full trial? The posture controls what standard the court applied and how broadly you can read the result.

**Facts.** The events that gave rise to the lawsuit. Courts present facts selectively, including what is relevant to the legal question and leaving out the rest. Read them carefully, because they are the foundation for everything that follows.

**Issue(s).** The legal question or questions the court has to decide. Sometimes the issue is stated outright (“The question before us is whether . . .”). Sometimes you have to infer it from the discussion. Identifying the issue is one of the most important reading skills you will build, and it is the court’s version of Step 1 of the Question Method.

**Holding(s).** The court’s answer to the issue, and the part with precedential effect. When the court’s issue matches your research question, the holding is the answer to your question, and it belongs at the center of your Findings entry.

**Reasoning.** The court’s explanation of why it reached its holding. In research terms, the reasoning answers a different question from the holding. The holding answers “what is the rule?” The reasoning answers “why is that the rule?” or “why did these facts satisfy it?” Two cases can reach the same holding through different reasoning, and when you need to argue from policy or by analogy, the reasoning is where that argument comes from.

**Disposition.** What the court actually orders: affirmed, reversed, remanded, vacated, or some combination. “Affirmed” means the lower court’s decision stands. “Reversed” means it is overturned. “Remanded” means the case goes back down for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

*Two further distinctions, holding versus dictum and how to read concurrences and dissents, are covered in Chapter 5, Beyond the Basics.*

## Reading for an Answer, Not for Class

Law school teaches you to read a case for class. You brief it: every part, in order, so you can recite the facts, state the issue, and defend the holding when called on. That is a fine way to learn what an opinion is. It is a slow way to do research, and it is not what a working researcher does. In research you arrive at an opinion holding a question, and you read for the part that answers it.

Most of the time that part is the holding. If the court’s issue matches your research question, the holding is your answer. Paraphrase it, cite it, put it in your Findings, and move to the next question.

The reasoning is not a second copy of the answer. It is the answer to a different question. Take the duty question in *Markowitz*. The holding answers “does the State owe park visitors a duty of care?” The reasoning answers “why does that duty exist, and why did the size of the park not defeat it?” That second question is a policy question. You ask it, and go back to read the reasoning closely, only if a policy argument would be useful in your case: if the State argues, say, that a duty is unworkable across thousands of acres of wild terrain. If no one is making that argument, the reasoning is background. Note where it is and keep going.

The same goes for the rest of the opinion. The caption tells you whether the answer binds you. The issue tells you whether the answer is an answer to *your* question. The facts tell you how far the answer reaches. The disposition tells you who won this round. None of those is the answer, and the interactive above lets you read the same opinion both ways so you can feel the difference. Reading for an answer is the Question Method applied to a single document: one question at a time, and the current question decides what deserves the close read.

## How to Engage With the Source

**Determine the weight before you invest the time.** Start with the caption. The jurisdiction and court level tell you whether the case is mandatory or persuasive authority in your matter, which decides whether the opinion is worth a close read at all.

**Read with your research question as the lens.** Research reading is strategic, not cover to cover. Use your current question as a filter. If your question is “What are the elements of a breach of fiduciary duty claim in Delaware?”, you are reading for the court’s statement of those elements. Note other useful passages, but do not chase them now; log them as next questions instead.

**Identify the dispositive facts.** Dispositive facts are the ones the court actually relied on, the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome. Record them next to the holding. When you reach the writing step, they are what you will compare with your own facts, and a case in the same area of law whose dispositive facts look nothing like yours is worth far less than its topic suggests. Read the court’s analysis and watch for signal phrases like **“critically,”** **“because,”** **“here,”** **“in this case,”** and **“unlike,”** which mark the court telling you which facts mattered.

**Confirm the points you rely on are still good.** Run the case through a citator (KeyCite or Shepard’s) before you rely on it, and read the citing opinions rather than trusting the flag alone. A negative flag may reflect a treatment that has nothing to do with your point. Treatment attaches to specific points of law, never to the opinion as a whole.

**Record and move on.** In your Research Log, capture the citation, the holding as the answer to your current question, the dispositive facts, and your next question. If the reasoning answered a question you may need later, log that question on your map with the citation beside it. The Next Question field is what keeps the cycle moving.
