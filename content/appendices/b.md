Reading a judicial opinion is harder than it sounds, and it helps to say that out loud. Opinions are written by judges for other lawyers and judges, who are assumed to already know the terminology, the procedural context, and the analytical conventions. The disorientation you feel at first is normal, and it passes. Once you can recognize the parts, you can move through any opinion efficiently, reading for the components that answer your question and moving quickly past the rest. That is the Question Method restated for opinions. Reading a case is answering the questions the opinion exists to answer: what was the dispute, what rule resolved it, and which facts the resolution turned on. Bring your current research question to every opinion, and let it tell you which components deserve the close read.

## Components of a Judicial Opinion

**Caption.** The case name, court, date, and citation at the top of the opinion. It tells you who is suing whom, which court decided the case, and when. In appellate opinions, the first-named party is usually, though not always, the appellant (the party who lost below and is asking the higher court to reverse). The citation tells you where to find the opinion in a reporter.

**Procedural History.** How the case got to the court writing the opinion. Was it appealed from a trial court? Is this a ruling on a motion to dismiss, a summary judgment decision, or an appeal after a full trial? The posture controls what standard the court applied and how broadly you can read the result.

**Facts.** The events that gave rise to the lawsuit. Courts present facts selectively, including what is relevant to the legal question and leaving out the rest. Read them carefully, because they are the foundation for everything that follows.

**Issue(s).** The legal question or questions the court has to decide. Sometimes the issue is stated outright (“The question before us is whether . . .”). Sometimes you have to infer it from the discussion. Identifying the issue is one of the most important reading skills you will build, and it is the court’s version of Step 1 of the Question Method.

**Holding(s).** The court’s answer to the issue, and the part with precedential effect. When you record a case in your Research Log, the holding belongs at the center of your Findings entry.

**Reasoning.** The court’s explanation of why it reached its holding. This is where the analytical work happens, and it is what you will use most often in your own writing. A holding gives you the rule; the reasoning shows you how the court applied it. Two cases can reach the same holding through different reasoning, and the reasoning is what tells you which case is more useful for your facts.

**Disposition.** What the court actually orders: affirmed, reversed, remanded, vacated, or some combination. “Affirmed” means the lower court’s decision stands. “Reversed” means it is overturned. “Remanded” means the case goes back down for further proceedings consistent with the opinion.

## How to Engage With the Source

**Determine the weight before you invest the time.** Start with the caption. The jurisdiction and court level tell you whether the case is mandatory or persuasive authority in your matter, which decides whether the opinion is worth a close read at all.

**Read with your research question as the lens.** Research reading is strategic, not cover to cover. Use your current question as a filter. If your question is “What are the elements of a breach of fiduciary duty claim in Delaware?”, you are reading for the court’s statement of those elements. Note other useful passages, but do not chase them now; log them as next questions instead.

**Identify the dispositive facts.** Dispositive facts are the ones the court actually relied on, the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome. This skill matters enormously for predicting how a precedent applies to your client. The question is not “is this case in the same area of law?” but “are the facts the court relied on close enough to my client’s facts to expect the same result?” Read the court’s analysis and watch for signal phrases like **“critically,”** **“because,”** **“here,”** **“in this case,”** and **“unlike,”** which mark the court telling you which facts mattered.

**Confirm the points you rely on are still good.** Run the case through a citator (KeyCite or Shepard’s) before you rely on it, and read the citing opinions rather than trusting the flag alone. A negative flag may reflect a treatment that has nothing to do with your point. Treatment attaches to specific points of law, never to the opinion as a whole.

**Record and move on.** In your Research Log, capture the citation, the holding, the dispositive facts, how the case answers your current question, and your next question. That last field is what keeps the cycle moving.
