Every term the book defines, in one place. Flip through the terms as flashcards, or filter the full list beneath them. In the chapters, a dotted underline marks the spot where each term is first defined.

::: {.try kind="flashcards" title="Every defined term"}
administrative adjudication
:   An agency's case-by-case process for resolving disputes under its statutes and regulations, decided by agency judges rather than courts; within the agency, the decisions can carry precedential weight. (Chapter 11)

administrative agency
:   A government body created by statute to administer a regulatory area; agencies issue regulations and guidance and adjudicate disputes within their delegated authority.

ALR annotation
:   An article in American Law Reports collecting and analyzing the case law of many jurisdictions on a narrow legal issue; a primary tool for survey questions. (Chapter 4)

ambiguity spotting (Skill 1)
:   Reading a legal text and identifying the terms, phrases, or provisions whose meaning requires interpretive research: undefined terms, vague standards, terms of art, structural ambiguities, scope questions. (Chapter 16)

amicus brief
:   A brief filed by a non-party, a “friend of the court,” offering arguments or perspectives the parties may not raise. (Chapter 13)

analytical closure
:   The judgment that research is complete because every question has been answered or identified as genuinely open, the rule and how courts have applied it are covered, and further research would only refine wording. (Chapter 3)

annotated code
:   A statutory code enhanced with editorial notes: case annotations, cross-references to regulations and secondary sources, and history notes. (Chapter 6)

annotated outline
:   The running document that records the product of research: each question’s synthesized answer, organized under the structure the final document will take. For research it is the R and E of CREAC, the base you write from. (Chapter 3)

application question
:   A question asking how courts or other authorities have applied a rule to facts in decided matters, answered by locating decisions with facts like the client’s (fact-finding) or, when no decision states a rule that fits, by building the rule from several sources (constructive application). Applying the rule to the client’s own facts is a writing step, taken in a research pause, not a research question. (Chapter 3)

authority note
:   The note accompanying a CFR part that cites the statute empowering the agency to issue it. (Chapter 7)

binding authority
:   Law a court must follow: the constitution, statutes, and regulations of its own jurisdiction, the decisions of higher courts in its own system, and federal law applicable within it. Mandatory authority is another term for the same concept. (Chapter 1)

Bluebook
:   The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation, the citation manual that governs most U.S. legal writing.

Boolean connectors
:   The logical operators (AND, OR, NOT) that require, alternate, or exclude search terms; the grammar of terms-and-connectors searching. (Chapter 2)

brief
:   A party’s written argument to a court, marshaling authority and applying it to the facts; in docket research, a map of the arguments and citations on each side of an issue. (Chapter 2)

candor to the tribunal (Rule 3.3)
:   The duty not to knowingly misstate the law to a court, and to disclose controlling authority directly adverse to the client’s position when opposing counsel has not. (Chapter 12)

canons of construction
:   Established interpretive principles courts use to read ambiguous statutory text, such as the plain meaning rule, expressio unius, ejusdem generis, and the rule against surplusage. (Chapter 6)

caption
:   The heading of a judicial opinion: the parties, the court, the docket number, and the decision date. (Chapter 2)

case law
:   The body of published judicial opinions; the source of common law and of authoritative interpretations of constitutions, statutes, and regulations. (Chapter 1)

certiorari
:   Discretionary review by a higher court, most familiarly the U.S. Supreme Court; a grant of certiorari means the court has agreed to hear the case. (Chapter 13)

Checkpoint
:   The Question Method’s reflect-and-evaluate pause: confirming that sources are current, authoritative, and from the right jurisdiction, and that the sequence of questions covers the full issue. Built into every cycle and performed formally before close-out. The Checkpoint asks whether the research is working; the Quick Check asks whether you are. (Chapter 3)

Chevron deference
:   The now-overruled doctrine under which courts accepted an agency’s reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute; overruled in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024), so courts now determine the best reading themselves.

circuit split
:   Disagreement among the federal courts of appeals on the same legal question; a marker of unsettled law and a common reason the Supreme Court takes a case. (Chapter 8)

citator
:   A tool (KeyCite or Shepard’s) that tracks an authority’s subsequent history and treatment, showing whether its points of law remain valid (colloquially, whether it is “still good law”). (Chapter 5)

closed universe
:   The provided-materials exam format: a fixed set of sources over which you run a compressed Question Method cycle; nothing outside the set may be relied on. (Chapter 16)

closing-out protocol
:   Chapter 9’s finish for a research project, in six steps: run a final sweep, update every authority, confirm that each authority supports the proposition you cite it for, review the research map for open questions, look for gaps in the outline, and name and document the type of closure. (Chapter 9)

code
:   Statutes arranged by subject as currently amended, in an official or annotated compilation. (Chapter 1)

Code of Federal Regulations (CFR)
:   Federal regulations arranged by subject into fifty titles; the regulatory counterpart to the United States Code. The eCFR is its continuously updated online edition. (Chapter 1)

committee report
:   The report accompanying a bill out of committee; generally the most authoritative legislative history document. (Chapter 10)

common law
:   Judge-made law developed case by case through judicial decision. (Chapter 1)

competence (Rule 1.1)
:   The duty to bring the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation the matter requires, making research quality itself an ethical obligation; Comment 8 extends it to understanding the benefits and risks of technology. (Chapter 12)

compiled legislative history
:   A pre-assembled collection of the legislative history documents for a major statute; always check for one before gathering the documents yourself. (Chapter 10)

complaint
:   The filing that opens a civil lawsuit, stating the claims and the facts behind them; in docket research, the frame of the dispute. (Chapter 3)

concurrence
:   A separate opinion agreeing with the result but reaching it by different reasoning; not binding, but useful for spotting the limits of the majority’s holding. (Chapter 5)

conference report
:   The report reconciling the House and Senate versions of a bill before final passage; among the most authoritative legislative history documents. (Chapter 10)

Congressional Research Service (CRS) report
:   A nonpartisan report prepared for Congress; a free, reliable orientation to a policy area and its legal framework.

constitution
:   The supreme law of its jurisdiction: it sets up the structure of government, defines the limits of government power, and guarantees individual rights. (Chapter 1)

constructive application question
:   An application question that no decision squarely addresses, answered by building the rule from the existing cases and reasoning by analogy from related contexts. The argument that the built rule reaches the client’s facts belongs to the writing step. (Chapter 3)

definitional question
:   A question asking what a word or phrase means. Read the statute’s or regulation’s definitions section first, then case law, for a definition that binds; use a legal dictionary for a working or persuasive definition. (Chapter 3)

dictum
:   A statement in an opinion not necessary to the decision; persuasive at most, never binding. (Chapter 5)

disposition
:   What the court orders as a result of its decision: affirmed (the lower court stands), reversed (overturned), remanded (sent back for further proceedings), vacated (set aside), or a combination. (Chapter 5)

dispositive fact identification (Skill 3)
:   Reading a judicial opinion to identify the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome, then comparing those facts to a client’s facts. (Chapter 16)

dispositive facts
:   The facts a decision actually turned on, the ones that, changed, would change the outcome. Record them beside the holding; comparing them with your client’s facts is the writing step. (Chapter 5)

dissent
:   A separate opinion disagreeing with the result; not binding, but sometimes the seed of future law. (Chapter 5)

docket
:   The court’s running record of a litigation: every filing, order, and event, in order. (Chapter 13)

editorial enhancements
:   Publisher-created research tools layered on top of the law in commercial databases, such as headnotes, key numbers and topic headings, citator signals, annotations, and summaries. They exist to help you find and navigate authority, and they carry no authority of their own; only the underlying text of the law can be cited. (Chapter 6)

element
:   A required component of a legal claim or defense. Because every element must be satisfied, each one heads its own chain of research questions, run down through its sub-issues before you move sideways to the next element. (Chapter 3)

enabling statute
:   The statute in which a legislature delegates authority to an agency (also called the authorizing statute); a regulation is valid only within its scope. (Chapter 7)

exploratory question
:   A question asking what the legal landscape looks like, answered by an encyclopedia or general search (no-clue), a treatise (foundational), or ALRs, Restatements, and 50-state surveys (survey). (Chapter 3)

fact-finding application question
:   An application question answered by locating decisions that applied the rule to facts like the client’s and recording how the courts applied it. Contrast constructive application. (Chapter 3)

Federal Register
:   The daily federal publication of proposed and final rules, notices, and presidential documents. (Chapter 7)

focusing question
:   A question asking what the specific governing rule, standard, or test is, answered from the statute, regulation, or case that states the rule and its sub-rules; usually the move from secondary to primary authority. (Chapter 3)

FOIA request
:   A request under the Freedom of Information Act for agency records not otherwise published, a research route to materials the agency has not made public. (Chapter 11)

foundational exploratory question
:   An exploratory question asked when you know the general area but need its internal structure: the elements, the factors, the major debates. Treatises answer it best. (Chapter 3)

frequency operator
:   A search operator requiring a term to appear at least n times in a document (ATLEAST3(preemption)), retrieving documents that discuss the term substantively rather than in passing. (Chapter 9)

generative AI
:   Software that produces text by predicting likely word sequences rather than retrieving stored documents; useful for drafting and orientation, never a source of law without verification. (Chapter 2)

guidance document
:   Agency material, such as manuals, opinion letters, and policy statements, that explains the agency’s interpretation without the force of law. (Chapter 7)

hallucination
:   A generative AI output that is fabricated, including invented citations, quotations, and holdings. A more specific taxonomy that names each kind of error is developing; this book keeps the term general and simple. (Chapter 15)

headnote
:   An editor-written summary of a single point of law in an opinion; a research tool, not authority. (Chapter 5)

holding
:   The court’s resolution of the issue necessary to its decision, the portion of an opinion carrying precedential weight. When the court’s issue matches your research question, the holding is the answer you record. (Chapter 5)

hornbook
:   A one-volume treatise summarizing an area of law, written primarily for students. (Chapter 4)

incorporation by reference
:   A regulation’s adoption of an external standard, often technical, as binding law; you must retrieve the incorporated document to know the full rule. (Appendix E)

intra-jurisdiction source ranking (Skill 2)
:   Given a collection of authorities from a single jurisdiction, ranking them correctly by binding effect: a supreme court over a court of appeals; a statute over common law on the same subject. (Chapter 16)

jurisdiction
:   A court’s power to decide a matter and, in research, the government and court system whose law governs your question; the first filter on every search. (Chapter 1)

legal issue
:   The problem the law must resolve; the reason the matter is on your desk. Framed as the question a document exists to answer, it becomes the macro question. (Chapter 3)

legislative history
:   The documents produced as a bill moves through the legislature, used to uncover legislative intent when statutory text is ambiguous. (Chapter 10)

legislative intent
:   The idea that the legislature passed a law with a shared sense of what it should accomplish. Something of a legal fiction, since a body of many members rarely has one collective intent; still, it is what legislative history research tries to uncover when the text is ambiguous. (Chapter 10)

List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA)
:   The monthly record of which CFR sections have changed since the last annual update. (Chapter 7)

litigation research closure
:   The litigation closure criterion: controlling authority identified for each issue, adverse authority identified and addressed, procedural requirements confirmed, and all cited authorities updated and validated. (Chapter 14)

macro question
:   The legal issue framed as the single big question a document exists to answer: the question presented in a memo, the issue on appeal in a brief, the thesis of a paper. Resolved by assembling the answers to research questions, never researched all at once. (Chapter 3)

majority opinion
:   The opinion joined by a majority of the participating judges; the one that states the court’s holding and carries precedential weight. (Chapter 5)

natural language searching
:   A search method that lets the user submit conversational sentences or questions instead of rigid keywords, and leaves the ranking of results to the database. (Chapter 2)

no-clue exploratory question
:   An exploratory question asked with no vocabulary or map at all; answered by encyclopedias and general searches whose first job is to supply the terms of art. (Chapter 3)

Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM)
:   The Federal Register notice proposing a rule and opening the public comment period; the record of the agency’s intent. (Chapter 7)

notice-and-comment rulemaking
:   The standard process for making regulations: a proposed rule published in the Federal Register, a public comment period, and a final rule published with an explanatory preamble.

nutshell
:   A compact one-volume overview of a legal area written for students; orientation, not authority. (Chapter 4)

opinion letter
:   An agency’s written answer to a specific question, often from a specific regulated party; narrow by design, and revealing of how the agency applies its own rules to concrete facts. (Chapter 11)

ordinance
:   A law enacted by a local government such as a city or county; the local layer of the authority hierarchy. (Chapter 8)

persuasive authority
:   Authority a court may consider but need not follow: primary authority from other jurisdictions, lower or coordinate courts, secondary sources, and dicta. (Chapter 1)

plurality opinion
:   The lead opinion when no single opinion commands a majority; binding only as to its narrowest common ground. (Chapter 5)

pocket part
:   The supplement tucked into the back cover of a print volume to update it between editions; checking it is the print form of a currency check. (Chapter 6)

practical closure
:   Stopping research because an external limit (a deadline, a budget, or an instruction) forces the stop, with what remains to be researched documented in the log and communicated with the final product. (Chapter 3)

practice guide
:   A practitioner-oriented secondary source offering forms, checklists, and procedural guidance for a specific practice area or jurisdiction. (Chapter 4)

preamble
:   The explanatory statement published with a proposed or final rule in the Federal Register; the agency’s own account of what the regulation means and why. (Chapter 2)

precedent
:   A decided case that guides or controls later decisions; the raw material of stare decisis. (Chapter 5)

precision trap
:   Seizing a source from the right area of law that answers a different question than the one you are holding; a precisely framed question is the defense. (Appendix E)

preemption
:   Federal law displacing state law in areas where Congress has constitutional power, either expressly or impliedly. In scholarship, preemption research instead means checking whether your argument has already been made. (Chapter 1)

primary authority
:   The law itself: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties. (Chapter 1)

procedural history
:   The path a case took to the deciding court: who sued, what happened below, and who appealed. (Chapter 2)

proximity connector
:   A search operator (/n, /s, /p) that requires terms to appear within a set number of words of each other. /s and /p are shorthand for default distances the platforms do not publish, not tests of the same sentence or paragraph. The workhorses of precision searching. (Chapter 9)

Public Law number
:   The sequential number assigned to each federal statute as enacted, such as Pub. L. No. 101-336; the key to finding session laws and legislative history. (Chapter 6)

Question Method
:   The research method this book teaches: write a single research question, name its type, research it, record the answer scoped to that question, map what else the source raised, check in on yourself (the Quick Check) and on the research (the Checkpoint), and let the answer produce the next question, repeating until closure. (Chapter 3)

Quick Check
:   The pause before each new question that asks whether you are in a headspace to keep researching; if not, write the next steps for future you and pivot to writing or another task. (Chapter 3)

regulation
:   A rule issued by an administrative agency under authority delegated by statute; within that authority, it carries the force of law. (Chapter 1)

regulatory research closure
:   The compliance closure criterion: all applicable regulations identified at every level, agency guidance consulted for interpretive questions, recent enforcement actions reviewed, and compliance status assessed against each requirement. (Chapter 14)

reporter
:   A publication that collects judicial opinions in numbered volumes; the volume and page numbers in a case citation point into a reporter. (Chapter 2)

research closure
:   The professional judgment that research is good enough for its purpose; it comes in analytical and practical forms. (Chapter 3)

research gap identification (Skill 4)
:   Recognizing when a source set does not fully answer the question and articulating what would fill the gap, distinguishing ‘the law is genuinely unclear’ from ‘I have not yet found the answer.’ (Chapter 16)

Research Log
:   The running record of the research process, one entry per question. The scaffold has seven fields: Research Question, Question Type, Method, Findings, Source Consulted, Next Question, and Notes. There is no single right format; what cannot be cut is enough of the process and the results to pick the work back up cold. (Chapter 3)

research map
:   The running list of the questions you need to answer to resolve the macro question, built over time from the assignment, the file, and the sources you encounter, with leads noted beside each question. It holds what is still to be answered; the Research Log holds what has been done. (Chapter 3)

research pause
:   The deliberate break in a research chain taken to write the application of a just-verified rule to the client’s facts into the annotated outline as a bracketed note; the move that keeps writing steps out of the Research Log and keeps analysis visibly distinct from authority. (Chapter 3)

research question
:   The micro question asked and answered in a single research cycle; one of the many subquestions whose assembled answers resolve the macro question. (Chapter 3)

research sufficiency and closure (Skill 5)
:   The professional judgment that research is complete: every question answered or identified as open, all authorities validated, the framework covered. (Chapter 16)

Restatement
:   The American Law Institute’s synthesis of common-law rules in an area; among the most persuasive secondary authorities. (Chapter 4)

root expander
:   The search operator (!) that retrieves every word sharing a root, such as negligen! for negligent, negligence, and negligently. (Chapter 2)

secondary source
:   Material about the law, such as encyclopedias, treatises, ALR annotations, and law review articles, used to find and understand primary authority but rarely cited as law. (Chapter 4)

session laws
:   Laws as enacted, published in chronological order, such as the United States Statutes at Large. (Chapter 1)

single-source trap
:   A coverage failure: an authority type that governs the issue and was never looked for, never found, or reached only secondhand through another source’s description of it. A run of same-type log entries is not by itself the trap. (Chapter 8)

slip law
:   The first official publication of a newly enacted statute, issued as a standalone document. (Chapter 1)

source note
:   The note citing the Federal Register document that created or last amended a CFR part; the thread you pull to reach the preamble. (Chapter 7)

stare decisis
:   The doctrine that courts stand by precedent: their own prior decisions and those of the courts above them. (Chapter 1)

statute
:   A law enacted by a legislature; published chronologically in session laws and by subject in a code. (Chapter 1)

Statutes at Large
:   The federal session laws: every statute as enacted, printed in chronological order. (Chapter 6)

statutory scheme
:   The organized structure a statute lives in: titles, chapters, definitions, exceptions, enforcement; no section reads correctly in isolation from its scheme. (Chapter 4)

supervisory duties (Rules 5.1 & 5.3)
:   The duties requiring lawyers with supervisory authority to make reasonable efforts to ensure that subordinate lawyers’ and nonlawyers’ work, including delegated research, conforms to professional obligations. (Chapter 12)

survey question
:   An exploratory question asking how many jurisdictions address an issue and how; answered by ALR annotations, Restatements, and fifty-state surveys. (Chapter 3)

terms-and-connectors searching
:   The precision search mode built from Boolean operators, proximity connectors, expanders, and phrase searching. (Chapter 2)

textualism
:   The interpretive approach that gives statutory text its ordinary meaning and distrusts legislative history; contrast purposivism, which looks to the statute’s aims. Its rise is why courts weigh legislative history less than they once did. (Chapter 10)

transactional practice
:   Law practice centered on deals and documents (contracts, financings, and mergers) rather than disputes; its research runs toward compliance and market practice. (Chapter 14)

transactional research closure
:   The transactional closure criterion: all applicable regulatory requirements identified, tax implications analyzed, comparable transactions reviewed for market terms, risk factors addressed in the deal documentation, and due diligence complete. (Chapter 14)

treatise
:   A comprehensive scholarly treatment of a single area of law, often multivolume, organized to give the field’s structure. (Chapter 4)

treaty
:   A formal agreement between the United States and foreign or tribal nations; once ratified, it has the force of federal law and can preempt conflicting state law or older federal law. (Chapter 1)

unpublished opinion
:   A decision the court designates as non-precedential; whether and how you may cite one depends on the jurisdiction’s rules. (Chapter 2)

waiver
:   Losing an argument by failing to raise or adequately support it; contentions offered without supporting authority can be treated as waived. (Chapter 12)
