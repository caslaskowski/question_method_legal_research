## ch01 · The Hierarchy of Legal Authority

*Reason: mechanics now taught by the Hierarchy of Authorities interactive; condensed to a summary*

## The Hierarchy of Legal Authority

### Primary vs. Secondary Authority

**[Primary authority]{.gloss}** is the law itself: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties. These are what courts rely on to decide cases. Secondary sources explain, interpret, or analyze the law, but they hold no authority to bind government or citizen action. They are like road maps that help you find where you need to go, but they are not the destination. In most court filings you will cite primary authorities, not secondary sources, with a few exceptions later chapters cover. For research, the practical consequence is that secondary sources are usually where you *start* and primary authorities are usually where you *end*. You read the map to find the road, then you travel the road.

### Binding (Mandatory/Controlling) vs. Persuasive Authority

Not all primary authority carries the same weight. Some of it is **binding** (also called mandatory or controlling), meaning a court must follow it. Some of it is **persuasive**, meaning a court may be influenced by it but is not required to follow it. Telling the two apart is one of the most consequential judgments a researcher makes, because it decides whether a source ends your inquiry or merely informs it.

Whether a primary authority binds depends on jurisdiction. An authority binds your court only when it comes from home: your jurisdiction’s own constitution, statutes, and regulations, the decisions of the courts above yours in the same system, and the federal law that applies there. California’s state laws have no binding effect in Arizona. For courts specifically, a judicial opinion binds only if it comes from a higher court in the same court system.

Run one state through the whole structure. An Arizona trial court must follow the Arizona Supreme Court on issues of Arizona law. A federal district court sitting in Arizona must follow Ninth Circuit precedent on issues of federal law. But when that same federal court has to decide a question of Arizona law, the direction of authority reverses: it takes its cue from Arizona’s own courts, especially the Arizona Supreme Court, because Arizona’s courts are the final word on what Arizona law means. (Exactly how federal courts predict state law is a wrinkle with rules of its own; we will leave those to your civ pro class.) All must comply with U.S. Supreme Court decisions interpreting the U.S. Constitution.

Persuasive authority is everything else: primary authorities from other jurisdictions, decisions from courts at the same level, decisions from lower courts, and all secondary sources. A court is not required to follow persuasive authority, but it is more likely to when the issue is new or unsettled in the controlling jurisdiction, when another court’s reasoning is particularly strong, or when there is a general trend of the law changing across the country. Persuasive authority matters most when you are arguing for a position that is unsettled. Say no Arizona court has ever addressed whether drone photography can violate someone’s privacy. An Arizona judge might look at how courts in New Mexico or California handled similar cases. Those decisions would not bind the court, but they could persuade it, and the more closely the other jurisdiction’s legal framework resembles Arizona’s, the more persuasive they become.

Persuasion can also travel further than a single case. The shift from contributory to comparative negligence in tort law shows how persuasive authority can gradually reshape binding law. The change started slowly, was advocated in secondary sources like the Restatement of Torts, was adopted by a few courts, and eventually became the majority rule across the country. What began as merely persuasive became, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, binding.

**In practice: Watching Persuasive Become Binding.** California’s switch is a clean specimen. For decades the state followed contributory negligence, under which a plaintiff even slightly at fault recovered nothing. Nothing binding required a change. What existed was persuasive material: scholarly criticism, other states’ decisions, and comparative rules already in force elsewhere. In Li v. Yellow Cab Co., 13 Cal. 3d 804 (1975), the California Supreme Court drew on exactly that body of non-binding authority and replaced the rule with comparative fault. The moment the opinion issued, the persuasive material had done its work and the new rule was binding on every court in the state. If you had researched this question in 1974, the best answer would have been “contributory negligence controls, but the persuasive weight is running the other way,” and that second clause is the one a client needed.

### Federal Preemption

Under the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution (Article VI), federal law overrides conflicting state law in areas Congress is empowered by the Constitution to pass laws on, such as patent law and large parts of immigration law. This is called **[preemption]{.gloss}**, and it can be express (Congress says so directly) or implied (the structure or purpose of the federal scheme leaves no room for state regulation). In areas where federal law sets only a floor, though, such as many environmental or labor protections, states may pass laws stricter than the federal requirement. Whenever you research a state-law question in an area that is also federally regulated, ask whether federal law preempts the state rule before you rely on it.

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test your understanding of which sources of authority are involved in different scenarios and why. No searching is required.

**Exercise 1.1 — The Layered-Authority Fact Pattern.** A client comes to you with this: she was fired from her job at a private company in your state after a routine drug test detected marijuana. She has a prescription-backed medical authorization to use it under your state’s law; she used it only at home and never worked impaired; and her employee handbook says nothing about the situation either way. She wants to know whether the firing was legal. Identify every layer of authority that might bear on it. Work through the chapter’s map deliberately: could a federal statute be involved? A state statute? An agency’s regulations at either level? Judge-made common law? A constitutional provision? For each layer you identify, write one sentence on what that layer might contribute to the eventual answer.

*Deliverable:* a table with one row per authority, three columns: the level of government (federal, state, or local), the type of source (constitution, statute, regulation, case law), and your one-sentence prediction of its role.

**Exercise 1.2 — Binding or Persuasive?** A trial court in Arizona is hearing a negligence claim, an area the chapter told you is governed mostly by state common law. The parties have put seven authorities in front of the court. Classify each as binding or persuasive for the Arizona trial court, with one sentence explaining your reasoning, and for the authorities you classified as persuasive, when a court might choose to follow the authority:

1. a decision of the Arizona Supreme Court on the duty at issue;
2. a decision of the Arizona Court of Appeals on the same duty;
3. a decision of the California Supreme Court on nearly identical facts
4. a decision of a different Arizona trial court; (e) a decision of a federal district court sitting in Arizona discussing Arizona negligence law
5. a section of a well-regarded treatise on negligence;
6. a decision of the U.S. Supreme Court interpreting the U.S. Constitution on a point raised in the case.

*Deliverable:* the seven classifications with justifications and explanations.

**Exercise 1.3 — Which version governs?** When reading a case for your research, you come across this quote: “In cases, like this, where the two versions conflict, the rule is that the Statutes at Large version controls.” *Midland Power Co-op. v. Federal Energy Regulatory* Com’n, [774 F.3d 1](https://www.ferc.gov/legal/court-cases/opinions/2014/13-1184.pdf), at 3 (D.C. Cir 2014) (finding discrepancy between 16 U.S.C. 825l(b) & 49 Stat. 860 that arose when the publisher changed a word when adding it to the US Code). The court was comparing a statute as Congress enacted it, 49 Stat. 860 (using “under this Act.”), with the same statute as it appears in the United States Code, 16 U.S.C. § 825l(b)(using under this chapter). The wording of the two is not identical. In two or three sentences, using only what the chapter told you about how a statute is made and published, explain why the enacted version might control over the codified version.\ *Deliverable:* one paragraph, three to five sentences.

## ch03 · Reverse Engineering the Question: A Demonstration

## Reverse Engineering the Question: A Demonstration

To see how legal arguments get built by answering questions, look at this passage from *Oakland Police & Fire Retirement System v. Mayer Brown, LLP, 861 F.3d 644, 649-650 (7th Cir. 2017)*:

In the vast majority of legal malpractice cases, the plaintiff was a client of the defendant-attorney, and the direct attorney-client relationship establishes the attorney’s professional duties to the client. *Pelham v. Griesheimer*, 92 Ill. 2d 13, 440 N.E.2d 96, 99 (Ill. 1982). There is a small corner of legal malpractice law, however, in which Illinois recognizes that attorneys owe professional duties to persons who are not their clients. In Pelham, the Illinois Supreme Court held that an attorney may owe a duty to a third party when “the primary purpose and intent of the attorney-client relationship itself was to benefit or influence the third party.” Id. at 100. Examples of third parties to whom attorneys owe a duty include third-party beneficiaries of wills, third-party beneficiaries of wrongful death actions, and third-party recipients of formal opinion letters. *See* *In re Estate of Powell*, 12 N.E.3d at 21; *Geaslen v. Berkson, Gorov & Levin, Ltd.* (Geaslen II), 155 Ill. 2d 223, 613 N.E.2d 702, 704, 184 Ill. Dec. 385 (Ill. 1993); *McLane v. Russell*, 131 Ill. 2d 509, 546 N.E.2d 499, 504, 137 Ill. Dec. 554 (Ill. 1989).

Now replace each sentence with the research question it answers:

- Under Illinois law, what is the general scope of legal malpractice law? (foundational)
- Are attorneys ever found to owe a duty to non-clients? (focusing)
- What are the requirements for that exception to the general rule? (focusing)
- What are some examples of non-client parties an attorney might owe a duty to? (application)

Notice that for several of these questions the answer comes from a single source, *Pelham v. Griesheimer*. That is common for settled points of law: elements, familiar principles, conclusions nobody is fighting about. The last sentence is different. It carries multiple citations because the answer requires a survey of different scenarios. When you see one point of law supported by a string of citations, that usually signals a *survey* question answered by looking across many cases.

Making the questions explicit shows you why some propositions might only need a single citation while others need several. Here we worked backward from a finished paragraph. When you research, you work forward toward one. Notice the paragraph as a whole serves the macro question of the appeal. Each sentence answers a single research question. Assembled, the small answers resolve the big one, and every piece of legal writing you produce has this structure under its skin.

Finally, those four questions, in order, are a research map. The paragraph is the annotated outline written out in full sentences, each question replaced by its answer and its citation.

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test the core moves of the Question Method: classifying questions by type, deriving questions from answers, running a complete cycle, and drafting a first question from where your knowledge actually is.

**Exercise 3.1 — Name the Type.** Classify each of the following as Definitional, Exploratory, Focusing, or Application, with one clause of justification: (a) What does “consideration” mean in contract law? (b) How does the law treat a landlord’s obligation to make repairs? (c) What standard governs a school’s control over a school-sponsored student newspaper? (d) Does any duty to retreat apply inside one’s own home? (e) How do my client’s facts compare to the facts of the leading cases in this jurisdiction? (f) How many states require all parties to consent before a phone call is recorded? (g) How cold did the apartment actually get last January? (h) Why was this statute enacted, and what problem was it built to solve? One of the eight is not a research question at all. Find it and say what kind of question it is instead. *Deliverable:* the eight labels with justifications, plus the intruder identified. *Self-check:* the intruder is a factual, investigative question; the law will never answer it, only evidence will. If you labeled all eight with a question type, revisit the distinction between what you research and what you investigate.

**Exercise 3.2 — Reverse-Engineer the Paragraph.** Take a short paragraph, four to six sentences, from a judicial opinion your instructor assigns, or from any of the school-speech cases named in this chapter’s demonstration. Replace each sentence with the research question it answers, and name each question’s type as you go. You watched this move run in one direction earlier in the chapter; now run it yourself in the other. *Deliverable:* the sentence-and-question pairs, each question typed. *Self-check:* read your questions in order, ignoring the original sentences. They should form a coherent chain in which each question could plausibly follow from an answer to the one before. If they read as a disconnected list, some questions are answering the paragraph’s topic rather than its individual sentences.

**Exercise 3.3 — One Full Cycle.** Choose one question from your Exercise 3.2 chain and run a single complete cycle on it: research it, record the answer, and let that answer hand you the next question. Complete every one of the eight research log fields for the entry, and honor the scoping discipline: the answer you record reports only what your question asked, even if the source offered you the whole landscape. Whatever else the source offered goes in the Notes field as later questions, not in the answer. *Deliverable:* one complete eight-field research log entry. *Self-check:* three tests. Could you have written the question with only what you knew at that moment? Does the What I Found entry stay inside the question’s scope? Does the Next Question follow from the What I Found entry rather than from thin air? A no on any test means the cycle needs a rerun, and the rerun is where the learning is.

**Exercise 3.4 — Draft the First Question.** Three clients, three starting points. Client one hands you a document her former employer wants her to sign that would prevent her from ever suing them, and asks if she should. You do not know what this kind of document is called. Client two asks whether the city can make him tear down the shed he built without a permit; you know words like “permit” and “zoning” but have no grip on how that area of law is put together. Client three asks whether your state requires landlords to provide heat, and you have enough grip to suspect a statute answers it directly. For each client, diagnose your starting knowledge, name the right opening move, and draft only the first question. Nothing else. *Deliverable:* three diagnoses and three first questions. *Self-check:* client one’s first move is vocabulary acquisition, and the question you drafted should contain no term of art you do not yet have. Client two’s question should be exploratory. Client three’s can be focusing. And remember the tomato: what you already “know” about a word’s everyday meaning is not knowledge of what the law does with it.

## Reviewing Through Practice: Did the Store Owe a Duty?

The following exercises test your ability to read a case for a specific answer and to identify its dispositive facts, followed by a longer research problem.

**Exercise 5.1 — The Five Moves, Run Once.** Bring this question to *Hazelwood School District v. Kuhlmeier*: what standard governs a school’s control over a school-sponsored student newspaper? Run the chapter’s five-move protocol on it, and keep the question, not the case, in the foreground the whole way. Read for the answer, synthesize it in your own words into an annotated outline entry, and then, this is the move students skip, log the collateral questions: the other questions the opinion answers that are not yours today, each with the case’s citation and the cases it cites for that point. *Deliverable:* one annotated outline entry answering the question, plus a collateral-questions list with citation pathways. *Self-check:* your outline entry should be one question’s answer, scoped and in your own words, and your collateral list should hold at least three questions you did not synthesize. If your outline entry tries to summarize the whole opinion, you read the case instead of reading for the answer, and those are different acts.

**Exercise 5.2 — Dispositive Facts.** Stay with the same opinion. List the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome, and test each candidate with a single sentence: “if instead \[the changed fact\], then \[the different result\], because \[the court’s reasoning that the change disturbs\].” A fact that survives the test unchanged was background, not dispositive. *Deliverable:* your tested list. *Self-check:* every entry’s test sentence must point at a specific passage of the court’s reasoning. If a test sentence works without citing the reasoning, you are guessing at what mattered rather than locating it, and guessing is the habit this exercise exists to break.

**Exercise 5.3 — Did the Store Owe a Duty?** Our client owns a small convenience store. Late one night, a customer was assaulted and robbed by a stranger in the store’s parking lot, and the customer is now suing the store, claiming it should have provided security. The supervising attorney has framed this as a negligence claim and asked you one thing: did the store owe this customer a legal duty to protect her from a stranger’s criminal act? In our state, that duty question is governed by judge-made common law, so your research lives entirely in case law.

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test your ability to map a statute’s anatomy and to use annotations as research pathways, followed by a longer research problem.

**Exercise 6.1 — Anatomy of a Section.** Take a statute your instructor assigns, or choose any substantive section of your state’s code that regulates conduct. Map its anatomy: the operative provision (who must or must not do what), every defined term it uses and where the definition lives, every exception and where it lives, the penalty or consequence provision, and the currency information telling you the text is current. Some of these will live in neighboring sections rather than the one you started in; finding them is the exercise. *Deliverable:* a labeled anatomy map of the section, with a citation for each component you located outside it. *Self-check:* if your map shows no defined terms and no exceptions, one of two things is true: you chose an unusually self-contained section, or you did not look in the neighboring sections. It is almost always the second. Statutes are schemes, and reading one section alone is how their traps get sprung.

**Exercise 6.2 — Annotations Are Pathways.** In an annotated code, take any substantive statute (your instructor may assign one, or reuse a statute you have already read this term) and use its Notes of Decisions or case annotations to identify two cases interpreting the section’s most important term. Do not read the cases yet. Log each in your Research Log as a pathway: the question it appears to answer, the citation, and where the annotation pointed you. This is the discipline of the Chapter 5 protocol’s fourth move, applied from the statute side: annotations hand you leads, and a lead recorded is not an answer synthesized. *Deliverable:* two Research Log entries, each honestly marked as a lead in the How This Answers My Question field. *Self-check:* if either entry’s What I Found field states what the case held, you exceeded the exercise. All you know is what the annotation claims, and an annotation is an editor’s summary, not the case.

**Exercise 6.3 — Can the Podcaster Record the Call?** A client runs a small podcast and wants to record phone interviews with sources. Some sources are in California. The client asks: can I record a phone call with a California source without telling them I am recording? The supervising attorney tells you the governing law is California’s recording-consent statute and asks you to answer from the statute.

## Reviewing Through Practice: The Drone Capstone

The following exercises test your ability to map the relationships among source types, to read primary authority directly, to distinguish unsettled law from incomplete research, and to run the return pass, followed by the capstone of the drill set.

**Exercise 8.1 — Build the Triangle.** Return to the organic-labeling question that has run through this book since Chapter 3, and build its research map as a statute-regulation-case-law triangle. At the statute corner, record what the delegating statute decided, located through the regulation’s authority note. At the regulation corner, record what the regulation itself supplies, located through the eCFR. At the case-law corner, identify at least one decision interpreting either, located through an annotated code or a citator. Then annotate each edge of the triangle: what question sends a researcher traveling from this corner to that one? *Deliverable:* a one-page triangle map, corners filled, edges annotated with the traveling questions. *Self-check:* the edges are the exercise. If your map has three full corners and empty edges, you have catalogued sources without mapping the movement between them, and movement between sources is what this chapter teaches.

**Exercise 8.2 — Go to the Source.** Pick any case from your Part I drills that quotes or paraphrases a statute (the Chapter 5 or Chapter 6 drill lines work well). First, write down the rule as the opinion presents it, using only the opinion. Then pull the statute itself in an annotated code and read it the Chapter 6 way: operative provision, definitions section, exceptions, cross-references, and a currency check on the text. *Deliverable:* a list, three items minimum, of what the code delivered that the opinion’s excerpt did not, a controlling definition, an exception, a cross-referenced requirement, an amendment postdating the opinion, plus one sentence on whether any item changes how you would state the rule. *Self-check:* if your list is empty, reread the definitions section; it is almost never empty. And if nothing on the list changes your statement of the rule, say why. That conclusion is a finding too, and you could only reach it honestly by doing the reading this exercise drills.

**Exercise 8.3 — Unsettled or Unfound?** From any research question you have worked in this course (any of the Part I drills works, or your instructor may assign one), write two findings: one that honestly reads “the law is genuinely unsettled on this point,” and one that honestly reads “I have not yet found the answer.” Defend each in two sentences, naming what you searched and why that search entitles you, or does not, to the stronger claim. “The law is unsettled” is a professional conclusion; “I could not find any law” is a confession. Knowing which one you are holding is the skill. *Deliverable:* the two findings with defenses. *Self-check:* the unsettled finding must rest on a search thorough enough that a supervisor would accept it, and its defense should say so specifically. If both of your findings could trade labels without the defenses changing, neither defense is doing its job yet.

**Exercise 8.4 — The Return Pass.** Return to the secondary source that opened any completed drill (the Chapter 4 drill is a natural choice) and reread the sections relevant to that drill’s question, now, with the whole chain behind you. *Deliverable:* either one unpulled thread, written as a properly drafted next research question with a note on which source type would answer it, or a logged finding that the return pass surfaced nothing new, naming the specific sections reread and the legal points you checked them against. *Self-check:* “nothing new” is a legitimate answer, and a vague one is not. If you cannot name the sections and the points you tested them against, you have not run the pass; you have just reread the introduction.

**Exercise 8.5 — The Drone Question.** Our client runs a small real-estate marketing business and wants to start flying a camera drone to shoot aerial photos and video of listed properties, for paying clients. The client asks: what do I have to do to operate the drone legally for this commercial work, and what legal exposure should I worry about? You do not yet know the area, and no one has narrowed it for you. Map and answer the question across whatever sources govern, using the Question Method to move among them.

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test precision search construction and the discipline of documented closure. Each one writes to the Search Log.

**Exercise 9.1 — Precision Translation.** Take three research questions from a current project, or from your instructor’s set, or from your own completed drills, and translate each into a precision search: synonyms gathered with OR, concepts bound with proximity connectors rather than bare ANDs, jurisdiction and source type filtered before the search runs. Record every search in your Search Log as you go: the query as entered, the filters, the result count, and one line on result quality. *Deliverable:* three Search Log sequences, one per question. *Self-check:* every search string should use at least one proximity connector doing real work. If AND is the only connective tissue in all three strings, you translated the Chapter 2 way, and this chapter existed to upgrade you.

**Exercise 9.2 — The Bad Translation.** Take this question: is a landlord liable when a tenant’s guest is injured on a broken staircase? Translate it twice. First, deliberately badly: all ANDs, single words, no synonyms, no proximity, no filters. Then properly, with the full Chapter 9 toolkit. Run both versions back to back and write two sentences on what the two result sets tell you. Contrast is the fastest way to sharpen the discrimination this chapter teaches, and building the bad version yourself teaches more than being shown one. *Deliverable:* both strings, both result counts, and your two sentences. *Self-check:* your sentences should name a specific difference, in volume, in relevance of the first page, in what floated to the top, not a generic “the good one was better.” If you cannot name the specific difference, look at result ten in each list and start there.

**Exercise 9.3 — The Closing-Out Run.** Run this chapter’s closing-out protocol, every step, on any research chain you have completed in this course (a Part I drill works well), and document the closure decision in your Research Log: the final sweep with alternative terms, the citator updates, the check against the original question, and your explicit finding of analytical closure or a named remaining gap. *Deliverable:* the documented closure decision. *Self-check:* one test, and it is the chapter’s test. If you cannot document why you stopped, you have not closed. You have just stopped.

The following exercises test the discipline of legislative history research: when the record may be opened, how a law’s documents are identified, and how they are weighed.

The following exercises, together called Map the Conversation, test topic generation, preemption checking, and question-form outlining. They begin an actual paper rather than simulate one.

**Exercise 17.1 — Seed the Inventory.** Go back through the sources you read for any three drills in this book, the cases, the secondary sources, the guidance documents (any three substantial legal sources you have read this term work as well), and harvest the questions they left open: the issue an opinion expressly reserved, the point flagged as beyond an article’s scope, the term two authorities read differently without ever engaging each other. *Deliverable:* a question inventory of at least five entries, each logged with where you found it. *Self-check:* every entry should be a question someone else’s text left open, not a topic you find interesting. “AI and copyright” is a shelf. “The court expressly declined to decide whether X, so what is the answer?” is an inventory entry.

**Exercise 17.2 — The One-Hour Check.** Take a candidate paper question, the strongest entry in your question inventory or any thesis you are considering, and spend exactly one hour on the cursory preemption check: search the claim’s distinctive terms in HeinOnline, Westlaw or Lexis, SSRN, and Google Scholar, and read the abstracts of the closest matches. *Deliverable:* a Research Log record of the searches you ran, the nearest neighbors you found, and the verdict: survives, needs differentiating, or preempted. *Self-check:* the hour is a real constraint; honor it, because the check’s whole value is being cheap. And if the verdict is preempted, celebrate briefly. The check just saved you a semester, and your inventory holds four more candidates.

**Exercise 17.3 — The Question-Form Outline.** For a candidate claim that has survived a preemption check, or any working thesis, draft a four-line mini-outline in question form, framework, problem, critique, proposal, each heading written as a question a section could answer. Add one line naming the non-law literature the paper would need and the database where that literature lives. *Deliverable:* the five lines. *Self-check:* each heading must be answerable, because answerable means a section can be finished, and finished sections are how forty pages get written by someone who can only hold one question at a time, which is everyone. A subject area is a shelf to browse. A question is a paper you can start, and after this exercise you have started one.
