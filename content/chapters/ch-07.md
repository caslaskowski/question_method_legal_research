Statutes create broad rules, but much of the law that affects people day to day comes from administrative agencies. Agencies like the EPA, the SEC, and the Department of Labor write the detailed rules, or regulations, that put statutes into practice. Once properly adopted, regulations carry the force of law. You need to be able to locate, read, and update them, because for many regulated activities the regulation, not the statute, is the rule your client actually has to follow.

## Regulations, Administrative Materials, and the Research Cycle

Regulations are where focusing and definitional questions about implemented statutory mandates get answered. A focusing question that begins with a statute (“the statute requires X, so what does compliance actually look like?”) is answered by following the annotated code’s “Implementing Regulations” note straight to the relevant CFR part. A definitional question about a term the statute delegated to the agency (“what does the agency mean by this term?”) is answered by the regulation’s definitions section.

And, like statutes, regulations generate new questions at every turn. An authority note raises the question of whether the regulation stays within its statutory grant. A cross-reference raises the question of what the referenced section requires. An incorporated external standard raises the question of what that standard says. Each becomes a Research Log entry and a next cycle.

## Questions This Source Answers

Regulations are your primary tool for **Focusing** questions about how a broad statutory mandate operates in practice (“What specific requirements must a food manufacturer meet to label a product organic?”) and **Definitional** questions about terms statutes delegate to agencies (“What is the federal definition of *waters of the United States* under the Clean Water Act?”). You will also use regulations for **Application** questions when a client’s conduct is governed by specific regulatory requirements. As you research any regulatory issue, the Federal Register preamble often supplies the interpretive context the regulation’s text alone cannot.

## How Regulations Are Made and What That Means for Research

Before a regulation becomes law, it typically goes through a notice-and-comment process governed by the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. Understanding the process tells you where to look for interpretive context.

- **Statutory Authorization.** Congress passes a statute delegating authority to an agency. The regulation is valid only if it falls within the scope of the authorizing statute, a point that is frequently litigated.
- **Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM).** The agency publishes a proposed rule in the Federal Register, explaining what it intends to do and why. The NPRM includes the proposed text and a preamble laying out the agency’s reasoning.
- **Public Comment Period.** The agency takes written comments from the public, including individuals, companies, and advocacy groups. Comments come in through regulations.gov and become part of the rulemaking record.
- **Final Rule.** After considering comments, the agency publishes the final rule in the Federal Register with binding text, a detailed preamble responding to significant comments, and an effective date.
- **Codification.** The final rule is codified in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), organized by subject.

Every stage produces documents you can use. The NPRM shows original intent. The comments show what stakeholders objected to. The final rule’s preamble explains why the agency made specific choices. That preamble is often your single best interpretive resource, the regulatory equivalent of legislative history, and overlooking it is the most common way researchers misread what a regulation requires. A shorthand worth keeping: NPRM = intent; comments = objections; final-rule preamble = the agency’s answers.

### Organization of Federal Regulatory Publications

Federal regulations move through a fixed set of official publications, and each answers a different question about the same rule: what has been proposed, what is codified, and what is current today. Knowing which publication holds which answer is most of regulatory search strategy.

- **Federal Register (FR).** The daily publication of proposed rules, final rules, notices, and executive orders. Best for tracing recent or proposed regulatory action.
- **Code of Federal Regulations (CFR).** The annual codification of all effective regulations, organized into 50 titles by subject. The official source for existing federal regulations. Structure: Title, then Chapter (agency), then Part, then Section. For example, 40 C.F.R. § 50.8 is Title 40 (Protection of Environment / EPA), Part 50 (National Ambient Air Quality Standards), Section 8 (carbon monoxide standards).
- **List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA).** A monthly record of which CFR sections have changed since the last annual update. Use it to verify currency when you are working from a print or annual edition.

Two small features of every CFR part deserve attention now, because later chapters lean on them. The **authority note** cites the statute that empowers the agency to issue the part. The **source note** cites the Federal Register document that created or last amended it, which is the thread you pull to reach the preamble. Appendix D’s How to Read a Regulation shows both in context.

## Searching for Regulations

If you already hold a CFR citation, retrieve it directly in the eCFR and skip the search. Otherwise, start from the statute or topic: use the annotated code’s “Implementing Regulations” notes to locate the relevant CFR part. If you know which agency regulates your issue, browse that agency’s chapters in the CFR or check the agency’s website, which often links directly to its regulations. Keyword searching, in the eCFR (free, updated daily), Westlaw, Lexis, or GovInfo.gov, comes last here too, and only with the precise terms the regulation uses, identified through secondary sources or the statute itself; regulatory drafting is at least as technical as statutory drafting, and the CFR’s structure is the better guide.

## Answering Questions with Regulations and Updating for Currency

Regulations can be amended, suspended, or invalidated at any time. Currency checking matters even more for regulatory research than for statutory research, because agencies revise regulations far more often than legislatures amend statutes.

- **The eCFR (ecfr.gov).** Updated daily; the most current freely available source. Each title carries a note indicating the date through which it has been updated.
- **List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA).** Use it when you are working from the annual CFR edition to catch any intervening amendments.
- **Federal Register.** Check for recent proposed rules, interim final rules, and final rules that may affect your regulation. The Unified Regulatory Agenda, published twice a year, lists the rules agencies plan to propose or finalize.
- **Citators.** Run KeyCite (Westlaw) or Shepard’s (Lexis) on your CFR section. Regulatory citators identify decisions that have interpreted, applied, challenged, or invalidated the regulation. A regulation struck down by a court is no longer enforceable, even if it still appears in the CFR text.
- **Agency websites.** Some agencies post compliance bulletins, guidance updates, or enforcement-policy changes that effectively change how a regulation operates in practice before the CFR text is formally amended.

*[\[Figure 7.1\]]{.flag} Regulation Currency Checklist: (1) check eCFR; (2) check the LSA; (3) check the Federal Register; (4) run KeyCite or Shepard’s; (5) check the agency website.*

## State Administrative Materials

Each state has its own administrative code and rulemaking process. State registers publish proposed and final rules; state codes compile them by subject. The research process mirrors the federal model: locate the authorizing statute, find the implementing regulation, and check for updates. Westlaw and Lexis often provide better search functionality than state agency websites do.

## Common Pitfalls

Stopping at the CFR text without checking the Federal Register for the preamble and context is the most common regulatory research error, because the agency’s explanation of what its rule means lives in the preamble, not the codified text. The next is ignoring amendments, since agencies revise regulations frequently and an older copy can be silently out of date. Watch too for overlooking related parts that hold the definitions and exceptions controlling the section you are reading. And keep guidance documents in their place; treating a non-binding guidance document as if it were a binding regulation can lead you to tell a client something is legally required when it is only the agency’s preference.

## Beyond the Basics

### Guidance Documents: Influential but Not Binding

Policy statements and guidance documents do not go through notice-and-comment and lack the force of law, but they are highly influential as statements of how agencies interpret and enforce their regulations*. Chapter 13 covers these materials, the weight courts give them, and the case law that governs that weight, in depth.*

One more development belongs on your radar now: in 2024 the Supreme Court ended the longstanding practice of courts deferring to an agency’s own reading of an ambiguous statute. The doctrinal ins and outs belong to your admin law course. What the shift means for how you weigh administrative authority is addressed in Chapter 13.

## Chapter Recap

Regulations implement statutory mandates through the APA notice-and-comment process and carry the force of law once properly promulgated. The Federal Register records rulemaking; the CFR organizes final rules. The preamble is often as important as the regulation text itself. Always trace a regulation’s statutory authority, rulemaking history, and currency, and let your unanswered questions, not the bare CFR text, tell you when you are done.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Regulations implement statutory mandates through the APA notice-and-comment process.
- The CFR codifies current federal regulations; the eCFR provides the most current free version.
- The Federal Register preamble is the most important interpretive resource for understanding regulatory intent.
- Authority notes link regulations to enabling statutes; source notes link them to Federal Register history.
- Always verify currency using the eCFR, LSA, Federal Register, and citators.
- Post-*Loper Bright*, courts independently interpret ambiguous statutes rather than deferring to agency interpretations.
:::

### Exercise 7.1 — Locate and Verify

Chapter 3's Research Log example asked what the federal definition of "organic" is. Finish the job properly. Locate the current federal regulation governing organic labeling in the eCFR, and then run the full five-step currency checklist from Figure 7.1 on the section you found: the eCFR check with its updated-through date, the LSA if you touched an annual edition, the Federal Register sweep for pending or recent rules, a citator run, and a pass through the agency's website for guidance that changes how the rule operates in practice.

**Deliverable:** the regulation's citation, and a currency note recording what each of the five steps found, including the exact date the eCFR title had been updated through on the day you checked.

**Self-check:** a currency note with five entries reading "nothing found" is possible but should make you suspicious. ### Exercise 7.2 — Trace the Delegation

Every regulation exists because a statute told an agency to write it. Find the authority note for the federal organic-labeling regulation (Appendix D shows you where the authority citation lives in a regulatory section), name the delegating statute, and write two sentences: one on what the statute itself decided, and one on what it left for the agency to fill in. **Deliverable:** the authority citation and your two sentences.

**Self-check:** the two sentences should describe genuinely different things. If they blur together, you have not yet located the line between the legislature's judgment and the agency's detail, and that line is the whole reason this source type exists.
