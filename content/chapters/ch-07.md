Statutes create broad rules, but much of the law that affects people day to day comes from administrative agencies. Agencies like the EPA, the SEC, and the Department of Labor write the detailed rules, or regulations, that put statutes into practice. Once properly adopted, regulations carry the force of law. You need to be able to locate, read, and update them, because for many regulated activities the regulation, not the statute, is the rule your client actually has to follow.

[Learning Objectives]{.box-title}

- Update regulations to confirm currency

## Regulations, Administrative Materials, and the Research Cycle

Regulations are where focusing and definitional questions about implemented statutory mandates get answered. A focusing question that begins with a statute (“the statute requires X, so what does compliance actually look like?”) is answered by following the annotated code’s “Implementing Regulations” note straight to the relevant CFR part. A definitional question about a term the statute delegated to the agency (“what does the agency mean by this term?”) is answered by the regulation’s definitions section.

And, like statutes, regulations generate new questions at every turn. An authority note raises the question of whether the regulation stays within its statutory grant. A cross-reference raises the question of what the referenced section requires. An incorporated external standard raises the question of what that standard says. Each becomes a Research Log entry and a next cycle.

## Questions This Source Answers

Regulations are your primary tool for **Focusing** questions about how a broad statutory mandate operates in practice (“What specific requirements must a food manufacturer meet to label a product organic?”) and **Definitional** questions about terms statutes delegate to agencies (“What is the federal definition of *waters of the United States* under the Clean Water Act?”). **Application** questions about how an agency has applied its rule to specific conduct are answered by agency adjudications and guidance, which Chapter 11 covers. As you research any regulatory issue, the Federal Register preamble often supplies the interpretive context the regulation’s text alone cannot.

## How Regulations Are Made and What That Means for Research

Before a regulation becomes law, it typically goes through a notice-and-comment process governed by the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. Understanding the process tells you where to look for interpretive context.

- **Statutory Authorization.** Congress passes a statute delegating authority to an agency. The regulation is valid only if it falls within the scope of the authorizing statute, a point that is frequently litigated.
- **Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM).** The agency publishes a proposed rule in the Federal Register, explaining what it intends to do and why. The NPRM includes the proposed text and a preamble laying out the agency’s reasoning.
- **Public Comment Period.** The agency takes written comments from the public, including individuals, companies, and advocacy groups. Comments come in through regulations.gov and become part of the rulemaking record.
- **Final Rule.** After considering comments, the agency publishes the final rule in the Federal Register with binding text, a detailed preamble responding to significant comments, and an effective date.
- **Codification.** The final rule is codified in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), organized by subject.

Every stage produces documents you can use. The NPRM shows original intent. The comments show what stakeholders objected to. The final rule’s preamble explains why the agency made specific choices. That preamble is often your single best interpretive resource, the regulatory equivalent of legislative history, and overlooking it is the most common way researchers misread what a regulation requires. A shorthand worth keeping: NPRM = intent; comments = objections; final-rule preamble = the agency’s answers.

### Organization of Federal Regulatory Publications

Federal regulations move through a fixed set of official publications, and each answers a different question about the same rule: what has been proposed, what is codified, and what is current today. Knowing which publication holds which answer is most of regulatory search strategy.

- **Federal Register (FR).** The daily publication of proposed rules, final rules, notices, and executive orders. Best for tracing recent or proposed regulatory action. Free at federalregister.gov (searchable, with links to the docket on regulations.gov) and govinfo.gov (the official PDF).
- **Code of Federal Regulations (CFR).** The annual codification of all effective regulations, organized into 50 titles by subject. The official source for existing federal regulations. Structure: Title, then Chapter (agency), then Part, then Section. For example, 40 C.F.R. § 50.8 is Title 40 (Protection of Environment / EPA), Part 50 (National Ambient Air Quality Standards), Section 8 (carbon monoxide standards).
- **List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA).** A monthly record of which CFR sections have changed since the last annual update. Use it to verify currency when you are working from a print or annual edition.

Two small features of every CFR part deserve attention now, because later chapters lean on them. The **authority note** cites the statute that empowers the agency to issue the part. The **source note** cites the Federal Register document that created or last amended it, which is the thread you pull to reach the preamble. Appendix D’s How to Read a Regulation shows both in context.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Which publication answers it?" bins="CFR | Federal Register | LSA"}
Match each question to the publication that answers it.

- What does the rule require today? **CFR**
    - The CFR holds the codified, operative text. The eCFR is the current version.
- Why did the agency reject a commenter's proposed exemption? **Federal Register**
    - The final rule's preamble answers the comments. The CFR carries none of that reasoning.
- Has this section changed since the annual volume on the shelf was printed? **LSA**
    - The List of CFR Sections Affected exists for exactly this question.
- Is an amendment to this rule currently proposed? **Federal Register**
    - Proposed rules appear here and nowhere in the CFR.
- Where are all of the agency's rules on this subject collected, with their definitions? **CFR**
    - Codification arranges rules by subject. The Federal Register arranges them by date.
:::

## Searching for Regulations

If you already hold a CFR citation, retrieve it directly in the eCFR and skip the search. Otherwise, start from the statute or topic: use the annotated code’s “Implementing Regulations” notes to locate the relevant CFR part. If you know which agency regulates your issue, browse that agency’s chapters in the CFR or check the agency’s website, which often links directly to its regulations. Keyword searching, in the eCFR (free, updated daily), Westlaw, Lexis, or GovInfo.gov, comes last here too, and only with the precise terms the regulation uses, identified through secondary sources or the statute itself; regulatory drafting is at least as technical as statutory drafting, and the CFR’s structure is the better guide.

::: {.try kind="choice" title="From the statute to its regulation"}
You have read 7 U.S.C. § 6502 and learned that the statute hands the definition of “organic” to the USDA. Your next question is what the USDA's regulation requires. How do you get there?

- [ ] Keyword search the eCFR for “organic.”
    - It will return hits across several titles, and the word appears in rules about chemistry as well as food. Keyword searching is usually the last resort in the CFR, and works best with the regulation's own terms.
- [x] Follow the annotated code's Implementing Regulations note to the CFR part, then retrieve it in the eCFR.
    - The annotation already connects the statute to its regulation, and you retrieve the citation it gives you instead of searching for it. You arrive at 7 C.F.R. Part 205 with the definitions section a click away.
- [ ] Browse the USDA's website for its organic rules.
    - A reasonable route. Agencies link to their regulations, and the page will show you the guidance around them too. Read the codified text in the eCFR before you rely on the agency's framing of it.
- [ ] Search case law for decisions quoting the regulation.
    - An opinion's quotation is an excerpt chosen for someone else's dispute. The definitions, exceptions, and neighboring sections do not come with it. Read the regulation itself first.
:::

## Answering Questions with Regulations and Updating for Currency

Regulations can be amended, suspended, or invalidated at any time. Currency checking matters even more for regulatory research than for statutory research, because agencies revise regulations far more often than legislatures amend statutes.

::: {.try kind="checklist" title="Is this regulation current? Five checks"}
- [ ] **The eCFR (ecfr.gov).** Updated daily; the most current freely available source. Each title carries a note indicating the date through which it has been updated.
- [ ] **List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA).** Use it when you are working from the annual CFR edition to catch any intervening amendments.
- [ ] **Federal Register.** Check for recent proposed rules, interim final rules, and final rules that may affect your regulation. The Unified Regulatory Agenda, published twice a year, lists the rules agencies plan to propose or finalize.
- [ ] **Citators.** Run KeyCite (Westlaw) or Shepard’s (Lexis) on your CFR section. Regulatory citators identify decisions that have interpreted, applied, challenged, or invalidated the regulation. A regulation struck down by a court is no longer enforceable, even if it still appears in the CFR text.
- [ ] **Agency websites.** Some agencies post compliance bulletins, guidance updates, or enforcement-policy changes that alter how the agency applies a regulation in practice, without changing the CFR text. Guidance does not amend the regulation, but it tells you how the agency reads it.
:::

## State Administrative Materials

Each state has its own administrative code and rulemaking process. State registers publish proposed and final rules; state codes compile them by subject. The research process mirrors the federal model: locate the authorizing statute, find the implementing regulation, and check for updates. Westlaw and Lexis often provide better search functionality than state agency websites do.

## Common Pitfalls

Stopping at the CFR text without checking the Federal Register for the preamble and context is the most common regulatory research error, because the agency’s explanation of what its rule means lives in the preamble, not the codified text. The next is ignoring amendments, since agencies revise regulations frequently and an older copy can be silently out of date. Watch too for overlooking related parts that hold the definitions and exceptions controlling the section you are reading. And keep guidance documents in their place; treating a non-binding guidance document as if it were a binding regulation can lead you to tell a client something is legally required when it is only the agency’s preference.

## Beyond the Basics

### Guidance Documents: Influential but Not Binding

Policy statements and guidance documents do not go through notice-and-comment and lack the force of law, but they are highly influential as statements of how agencies interpret and enforce their regulations. Chapter 11 covers these materials, the weight courts give them, and the case law that governs that weight, in depth.

One more development belongs on your radar now: in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) the Supreme Court ended the longstanding practice of courts deferring to an agency’s own reading of an ambiguous statute. The doctrinal ins and outs belong to your admin law course. What the shift means for how you weigh administrative authority is addressed in Chapter 11.

### Challenging an Agency Action in Court

Sooner or later a client will not want to comply with a regulation. They will want it set aside, or at least not applied to them. The doctrine of judicial review belongs to your administrative law course. The research belongs here, because a challenge to agency action is a chain of questions, and nearly every link in it lives in a source this chapter has already taught you to read. Take the links in order. Any one of them can end the matter before a court ever reaches the merits, and a researcher who starts with the merits has usually started in the wrong place.

The chain runs from the enabling statute (does it provide for review, and where?) through the client (can this person sue?) and the agency’s own procedures (must the client go back there first?) to the courthouse (which court, by what filing, and by what date?), and only then to the two questions that decide most challenges: what standard the court will apply, and what is in the record it will apply that standard to. Select each step to see the question it asks and the source that answers it.

::: {.try kind="explore" layout="stack" title="Challenging a rule: six questions, in order"}
Each step is a Research Log entry before it is an argument. The source named in each is where the answer usually lives.

- **1. Is there something to review? | the enabling statute and the APA.** Courts review *final* agency action, and a proposed rule, a draft guidance, or an inspector’s warning letter is usually not final (5 U.S.C. § 704). Start with the statute that delegated the rule, because many statutes carry their own review provision naming the court, the filing, and the deadline, and that provision displaces the Administrative Procedure Act’s default route. Your first entry is definitional: what does this statute say about review?
- **2. Can this client bring it? | case law in the circuit where you will file.** Standing. The challenger needs a concrete injury, caused by the action, that a court can remedy (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 1992), and the injury must fall within the interests the statute protects. The organic-labeling thread shows the shape of it: a competitor harmed by a looser definition of “organic” has a different standing argument from a shopper who merely dislikes the label.
- **3. Must the client go back to the agency first? | the statute and the agency’s procedural rules.** Exhaustion of administrative remedies, with its cousins finality and ripeness. Whether the client must take the agency’s internal appeal before suing is answered in two places: the enabling statute, and the agency’s own procedural regulations, usually a Part of the CFR near the substantive one. Read them the way Chapter 6’s deposit section taught you to. The exception two subsections down can switch the right off.
- **4. Where, how, and by when? | the review provision, or the APA.** Many statutes send a challenge to a rule straight to a court of appeals by petition for review, often within sixty days of publication in the Federal Register. Without such a provision the challenge is a complaint in district court under the APA, subject to the general six-year limit on suits against the United States, and in *Corner Post, Inc. v. Board of Governors* (2024) the Supreme Court held that for an APA claim that clock starts when the plaintiff is injured, not when the rule was published. The deadline is a focusing question with an exact answer. A day late is fatal, so log the section you relied on and the date you computed.
- **5. What will the court ask? | 5 U.S.C. § 706 and the cases applying it.** The standard of review. For most rules and informal decisions the court asks whether the action was *arbitrary and capricious*: did the agency consider the relevant factors and explain itself (*Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Ass’n v. State Farm*, 1983)? For decisions made on a formal record after a hearing, the court asks whether *substantial evidence* supports them. Whether the agency had statutory authority to act at all is a question of law that, after *Loper Bright*, the court answers for itself. Each standard is a different research question, and the preamble you learned to read in this chapter is the agency’s own defense: a reasoned response to comments is what “not arbitrary” looks like on paper.
- **6. What is in the record? | the rulemaking docket.** Review happens on the administrative record the agency compiled, not on evidence first offered in court. For a rule, that record is the notice of proposed rulemaking, the comments at regulations.gov, and the final rule with its preamble. A point nobody raised in a comment may be treated as waived. So the research for a challenge often reaches back into the rulemaking itself, and a comment letter filed years earlier can decide the case.
:::

Step 5 is where students most often reach for the wrong question. The standard of review is not a label you choose; it follows from what kind of action the agency took and how it took it, and the statute or the APA assigns it. Sort each challenge by the question the court will ask.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Which question will the court ask?" bins="Arbitrary and capricious | Substantial evidence | Question of law, decided afresh"}
- The EPA set an emissions limit through notice-and-comment, and the challenger says the final rule never addressed a cost study submitted in comments. **Arbitrary and capricious**
    - A rule made without a formal hearing is reviewed for reasoned decision-making. Ignoring a significant comment is the classic failure, and the preamble is where you look for the agency’s answer.
    - Substantial evidence is the standard for findings made on a formal record after a hearing. Notice-and-comment rulemaking produces a docket, not a hearing record.
- After an administrative hearing, the agency’s judge found that the employer’s safety violation was willful, and the employer disputes the finding. **Substantial evidence**
    - A finding of fact made on a hearing record is reviewed for whether the record reasonably supports it. The court does not reweigh the evidence.
    - The court is reviewing a factual finding made after a hearing, not the reasoning of a rule. That calls for the substantial-evidence standard.
- Whether the statute lets the agency regulate this product at all. **Question of law, decided afresh**
    - Statutory authority is a question of law, and after *Loper Bright* the court reads the statute for itself rather than deferring to the agency’s reading. The research is statutory interpretation, Chapter 6’s skill.
    - Neither deferential standard applies to the meaning of the statute. The court decides that question itself.
- The agency reversed a twenty-year policy in a final rule with a one-paragraph explanation. **Arbitrary and capricious**
    - An agency may change course, but it must acknowledge that it is changing and give a reasoned explanation. One paragraph is the research lead: what did the comments say, and did the preamble answer them?
    - The challenge is to the rule’s reasoning, which is the arbitrary-and-capricious inquiry. The length of the explanation is evidence, not the standard.
- The Board decided, on a hearing record, that a worker was an employee rather than an independent contractor. **Substantial evidence**
    - A mixed finding on a formal record. The court asks whether the record supports the Board’s conclusion, and cases applying the standard in that circuit are your source.
    - This decision came out of a hearing with a record, so the question is whether the record supports it. That is substantial evidence, not arbitrary and capricious.
:::

Two habits carry over from the rest of the chapter. First, every step is a Research Log entry with its own source, and the sources are the ones you already know: the enabling statute, the CFR, the Federal Register, the case law in your circuit. Second, the steps do not substitute for the merits. A client who clears all six still has to show the rule is wrong, and that research is the statute-regulation-case law work of Chapter 8. What the chain does is tell you, early and cheaply, whether the client can get to the merits at all.

## Chapter Recap

Regulations implement statutory mandates through the APA notice-and-comment process and carry the force of law once properly promulgated. The Federal Register records rulemaking; the CFR organizes final rules. The preamble is often as important as the regulation text itself. Always trace a regulation’s statutory authority, rulemaking history, and currency, and let your unanswered questions, not the bare CFR text, tell you when you are done.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Regulations implement statutory mandates through the APA notice-and-comment process.
- The CFR codifies current federal regulations; the eCFR provides the most current free version.
- The Federal Register preamble is the most important interpretive resource for understanding regulatory intent.
- Authority notes link regulations to enabling statutes; source notes link them to Federal Register history.
- Always verify currency using the eCFR, LSA, Federal Register, and citators.
- Post-*Loper Bright*, courts independently interpret ambiguous statutes rather than deferring to agency interpretations.
- A challenge to agency action is a chain of research questions, reviewability, standing, exhaustion, forum and deadline, standard of review, and the record, each answered in a source you already know; any link can end the case before the merits.
:::

### Exercise 7.1 — Locate and Verify

Chapter 3's Research Log example asked what the federal definition of "organic" is. Finish the job properly. Locate the current federal regulation governing organic labeling in the eCFR, and then run the full five-check currency checklist from this chapter on the section you found: the eCFR check with its updated-through date, the LSA if you touched an annual edition, the Federal Register sweep for pending or recent rules, a citator run, and a pass through the agency's website for guidance that changes how the rule operates in practice. Then find where your state publishes its regulations and its notices of proposed and final rules, the state's counterparts to the CFR and the Federal Register, and note where each lives.

**Deliverable:** the regulation's citation, and a currency note recording what each of the five steps found, including the exact date the eCFR title had been updated through on the day you checked; then one line naming where your state's regulations and rulemaking notices live.

**Self-check:** a currency note with five entries reading "nothing found" is possible but should make you suspicious. At minimum, the eCFR step always yields a date, and the Federal Register step nearly always yields activity somewhere in the part. If your note is empty, your searches were too narrow.
:::

### Exercise 7.2 — Trace the Delegation

Every regulation exists because a statute told an agency to write it. Find the authority note for the federal organic-labeling regulation (Appendix D shows you where the authority citation lives in a regulatory section), name the delegating statute, and write two sentences: one on what the statute itself decided, and one on what it left for the agency to fill in. This is the statute-to-regulation joint that Chapter 8 will build on, met here for the first time in the wild.

**Deliverable:** the authority citation and your two sentences.

**Self-check:** the two sentences should describe genuinely different things. If they blur together, you have not yet located the line between the legislature's judgment and the agency's detail, and that line is the whole reason this source type exists.
