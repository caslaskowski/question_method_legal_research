The NextGen Bar Exam tests legal research skills as part of Foundational Skills Group C. The purpose of that group is to assess how well an examinee can identify and implement research strategies, including preliminary issue-spotting, working with provided resources, developing and refining a theory of the case, and reaching closure on research questions. The Question Method prepares you for exactly this, not as a separate exam-prep exercise but as the natural byproduct of practicing the method throughout law school.

::: {.objectives}
[Learning Objectives]{.box-title}

- Synthesize a collection of provided sources to resolve a legal issue under examination conditions

## Five Core Research Skills

The NCBE competencies consolidate into five skills, which this textbook names as follows:

- **Skill 1: Ambiguity spotting.** Reading a legal text and identifying terms, phrases, or provisions whose meaning requires interpretive research. Look for undefined terms, vague standards, terms of art, structural ambiguities, and scope questions. Developed in Chapter 6 (Statutes) and Appendix C; applied in Chapters 5, 7, and 8.
- **Skill 2: Intra-jurisdiction source ranking.** Given a collection of authorities from a single jurisdiction, ranking them correctly by binding effect. A state supreme court decision ranks above a court of appeals decision. A statute controls over common law on the same subject. Developed in Chapter 1; applied throughout.
- **Skill 3: Dispositive fact identification.** Reading a judicial opinion to identify the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome, then comparing those facts to a client’s facts. Locate the court’s analytical reasoning and ask whether different facts would change the result. Developed in Chapter 5 (Case Law) and Appendix B.
- **Skill 4: Research gap identification.** Recognizing when a source set does not fully answer the question and articulating what would fill the gap. Distinguish “the law is genuinely unclear” from “I have not yet found the answer.” (This is the line Chapters 8 and 9 drilled.) Developed in Chapter 8 (Researching Across Sources).
- **Skill 5: Research sufficiency and closure.** Determining when research is complete, which is a professional judgment. Research is sufficient when all questions are answered or identified as open, all authorities are validated, the framework is consistent, and counterarguments have been addressed. Developed in Chapters 3 and 9.

## Practice Strategies for Bar Research Questions

**Approaching provided-materials questions.** Read all the provided materials before you attempt an answer, and call this move excerpt triage. Build a mental map of what each source provides. Identify binding versus persuasive authorities. Identify ambiguities and note whether the provided cases resolve them. Look for analogous and distinguishable facts. Note gaps. The exam, in effect, hands you a closed universe of sources and asks you to run a compressed Question Method cycle over them.

::: {.callout}
[Example]{.box-title}

**Excerpt triage, run once.** The item (the Chapter 5 drill, now as an exam item): a customer sues a convenience store after a parking-lot assault, and the provided materials are (1) the state supreme court’s decision adopting a special-relationship exception to the general no-duty rule, (2) an intermediate appellate decision applying that exception to a gas station with two prior robberies, (3) a neighboring state’s supreme court decision using a different foreseeability test, and (4) the state’s premises-liability statute, which addresses physical conditions of the property and says nothing about third-party crime. The triage pass, before writing a word: the supreme court decision controls and supplies the rule; the appellate decision binds and supplies the closest application; the neighboring state’s decision persuades only; and the statute, read for scope, does not reach the issue, which is a finding, not a discard. The ambiguity the item was built around: what makes third-party crime foreseeable enough to ground a duty, the point on which the two in-state decisions leave room. The compressed cycle then runs on the mapped materials: macro question (did the store owe this customer a duty?); its research questions (what is the rule; what makes crime foreseeable under it; how did the applied case treat facts like these), each answered from the source triage identified for it; then the writing step, comparing the client’s facts to those decisions; and the sufficiency call, committed: the materials answer the duty question, and any element they do not address is named as the gap rather than papered over. Forty-five minutes of exam time is this whole cycle at compression, and every move in it is one you have already run at full size.
:::

**Using the Question Method under time constraints.** The framework applies in compressed form: What is the macro question, and what research questions does it break into? What authority controls? What does it say? How have the provided decisions applied it to facts like these? Are the provided materials sufficient? Only then the writing step: how does the rule apply to this client, drafted from what the sources answered.

**Identifying the strongest authority.** Rank the provided authorities by the hierarchy: mandatory authority from the highest court controls; persuasive authority supports. A clear statutory provision controls over case law interpreting a different statute.

### Three Research Performance Tasks, in Miniature

The NextGen legal research performance task has a fixed shape, and knowing the shape saves minutes you will want. You get a memo from a supervising attorney, a transcript of a client interview or a document from the file, and a small library: usually a statute excerpt or two and two or three decisions, all from a fictional state so that nothing you memorized can help you. Then come four multiple-choice questions, one short-answer question, and a writing assignment of a page or so, in about sixty minutes. The National Conference of Bar Examiners publishes full samples on its website. The three sketches below are not full tasks. They show the kind of research questions the method writes once the file and the library are in front of you, which is the part the exam is really testing.

::: {.callout}
[Task 1: the borrowed car]{.box-title}

**The file.** A memo asks you to evaluate a negligent entrustment claim against a client who lent her car to her nineteen-year-old brother, who holds a probationary license. The interview transcript shows she knew of one speeding ticket and did not know he had been drinking. **The library.** An excerpt from the state's vehicle code on probationary licenses and accompanying drivers; a supreme court decision stating the elements of negligent entrustment; a court of appeals decision dismissing an entrustment claim where the owner knew of no unfitness.

**The task asks.** Which provision of the code governs the brother's license (multiple choice). Which fact in the transcript is legally significant to the entrustment claim (multiple choice). What one additional source would you want and why (short answer). A short memo on whether the claim survives a motion to dismiss (writing).

**The questions the method writes.** Q1 (Focusing): what are the elements of negligent entrustment in this state, and which decision states them? Q2 (Focusing): does the vehicle code's accompanying-driver rule apply to a probationary licensee at all, and does its violation bear on any element? Q3 (Application): how has the court of appeals applied the knowledge element where the owner knew of some but not all of the driver's record? Q4 (Sufficiency): does any source in the library address knowledge of drinking as opposed to driving history, or is that the gap to name? The writing step comes after Q4, not before.
:::

::: {.callout}
[Task 2: the gym membership]{.box-title}

**The file.** A pro bono client signed a two-year gym contract at a sales table in a shopping mall, changed his mind the next morning, and was told cancellations are not accepted. **The library.** Excerpts from the state's health club services act (a three-business-day right to cancel, a required notice of that right in the contract, and a remedies section); a supreme court decision holding that a contract omitting the required notice is voidable at the consumer's election; a court of appeals decision finding substantial compliance where the notice was present but in the wrong type size.

**The task asks.** Which source controls whether the contract is enforceable (multiple choice). Whether the client's oral cancellation satisfied the act (multiple choice). What fact you would want to confirm from the contract itself (short answer). A letter to the gym, or a memo to the supervisor, on the client's options (writing).

**The questions the method writes.** Q1 (Focusing): what does the act require a cancellation to look like, and by when? Q2 (Focusing): what does the act require the contract to say about that right? Q3 (Application): how have the two decisions treated contracts that fell short of the notice requirement, and how far does substantial compliance reach? Q4 (Sufficiency): the act's remedies section is excerpted; does it answer whether the client can recover a deposit, or only whether the contract binds him? Notice that the client's facts never generate a research question. They tell you which questions to ask of the library.
:::

::: {.callout}
[Task 3: the handwritten will]{.box-title}

**The file.** A memo describes a decedent who filled in a printed will form by hand, signed it, and had no witnesses. The family wants to know whether it can be admitted to probate. **The library.** An excerpt from the state's probate code on holographic wills (valid without witnesses if the signature and the material portions are in the testator's handwriting); a supreme court decision explaining what counts as a material portion; a court of appeals decision admitting a preprinted form where the handwritten words alone expressed a complete testamentary intent.

**The task asks.** Which provision governs validity (multiple choice). Which handwritten words on the form are material (multiple choice). What the proponent would need to prove and how (short answer). A memo on the likelihood of admission (writing).

**The questions the method writes.** Q1 (Focusing): what does the probate code require of a holographic will, element by element? Q2 (Definitional, answered by a court because the code does not define it): what has the supreme court said a “material portion” is? Q3 (Application): how did the court of appeals apply that definition to a preprinted form with handwritten gaps? Q4 (Sufficiency): nothing in the library addresses a signature placed somewhere other than the end; if the file raises that, name it as the gap. Four questions, in that order, is the whole research half of the task. The method is not slower than guessing. It is what keeps the writing half from guessing.
:::

### Practice in a Closed Universe

The exam hands you a small set of sources and a client problem, and everything you may rely on is on the page. Here is a universe of that kind. Every source in it is fictional. A client's apartment has had no heat for three weeks in January.

::: {.try kind="annotate" title="Skill 1: find the terms that will need research" hint="Read the statute, then select each marked phrase. Which ones does the text settle, and which ones hand you a question?"}
**Residential Rental Standards Act § 12.** (a) A landlord shall maintain every residential rental unit in [habitable condition]{.m label="Vague standard" note="The statute does not say what makes a unit habitable. A term like this is filled in by case law, and the universe supplies it: *Marsh* holds the duty includes adequate heat."}, including [operable heating facilities]{.m label="Settled by the text" note="This one is not ambiguous for this client. The statute names heating in so many words. Spotting ambiguity includes recognizing where there is none."} capable of maintaining [safe indoor temperatures]{.m label="Vague standard" note="Safe for whom, measured how? Nothing in the universe defines it. If the landlord claims the unit stayed at 58 degrees, this phrase becomes the fight."} during the [heating season]{.m label="Undefined term" note="No dates are given. For a January outage it will not matter. For one in late April it would be the first question."}. (b) Upon [written notice]{.m label="Condition" note="The meaning is clear enough, but the effect is easy to miss: the repair duty in (b) is triggered by notice in writing. Whether the client gave it is a fact to find, not a term to research."} of a defect under subsection (a), the landlord shall repair within a [reasonable time]{.m label="Vague standard" note="This is the classic open-ended phrase. How long is reasonable in January is a question for cases, and this universe contains none on the point. Name the gap."}. (c) Remedies for violation of this section are [as provided by law]{.m label="Points outside the text" note="The statute does not list remedies. It sends you elsewhere. In this universe, elsewhere is *Okafor* (withholding and repair-and-deduct) and *Delgado* (damages)."}.
:::

The universe also holds three decisions. *Marsh v. Golden Rentals* (state supreme court): a residential landlord's duty to keep a rental fit to live in includes providing adequate heat during the cold months. *Okafor v. Canyon Ridge Apartments* (state supreme court): a tenant facing an unrepaired habitability defect may repair and deduct, or may withhold rent, but only after written notice and a reasonable opportunity to cure; an earlier decision permitting withholding without notice is disapproved. *Delgado v. Mesa Verde Rentals* (court of appeals): damages for a habitability breach are generally limited to the loss in rental value, and emotional-distress damages require egregious conduct.

::: {.try kind="choice" title="Skill 2: which authority controls?"}
The client stopped paying rent yesterday without telling the landlord anything. Within this universe, which authority controls the conditions for withholding rent?

- [x] *Okafor*, the supreme court's rule, which fills in what § 12(c) leaves to “law.”
    - The statute sets the frame, and the highest court fills it in. *Okafor* conditions withholding on written notice and a chance to cure, and the client skipped the condition.
- [ ] Section 12(b), because a statute outranks a case.
    - A statute does outrank a case when they address the same thing. Section 12(b) governs when the landlord must repair. It says nothing about withholding, and § 12(c) points outward.
- [ ] *Delgado*, as the most specific decision on remedies.
    - *Delgado* addresses damages, not withholding, and as a court of appeals decision it ranks below *Okafor* in any event.
:::

::: {.try kind="choice" title="Skill 4: is the answer in the universe at all?"}
The client asks whether she can recover her moving costs if she has to relocate. Answer from the universe.

- [ ] Yes. Section 12(c) makes remedies available “as provided by law.”
    - Section 12(c) points to law the universe does not contain on this point. A pointer to nothing is a gap.
- [ ] No. *Delgado* limits damages to lost rental value.
    - This is tempting. *Delgado* says “generally,” it was deciding an emotional-distress claim, and it never mentions relocation. Stretching a holding past its facts is the classic trap.
- [x] The universe does not answer it. Say so, and say what kind of source would.
    - This is research gap identification. The competent answer names the gap and says what would fill it, such as a statute or decision on consequential damages. It does not dress a guess up as analysis.
:::

::: {.try kind="reveal" title="Skill 5: make the sufficiency call" placeholder="Do § 12 and the three decisions answer the client’s withholding question? Commit: yes, or name the gap and what would fill it."}
The client asks whether she may withhold rent now. Using only § 12 and the three decisions, write the sufficiency call you would commit to on the exam.

---

A committed call: **the universe answers it.** Section 12(c) sends remedies to “law,” *Okafor* supplies that law, and it conditions withholding on written notice and a reasonable opportunity to cure. She gave no notice, so she may not withhold yet. The one gap worth naming is how long a “reasonable opportunity to cure” runs during a January outage, which no source here addresses. “Probably enough” is not a call. Name what is answered and what is not.
:::

## Common Pitfalls

Under time pressure, the tempting mistake is to start writing before reading every provided source, which is how examinees miss the one authority that controls the answer. Close behind is misranking what they have read, treating a persuasive decision or an off-point statute as though it controlled; the hierarchy of authority is the whole game on “which source governs” questions. Another is failing to spot the ambiguity the question was built around, since the exam usually hides an undefined term or a scope question that the provided cases are there to resolve. And examinees often forget the closure step, declaring an answer without asking whether the provided materials are actually sufficient or whether a gap remains to be named.

## Chapter Recap

The NextGen Bar Exam’s Foundational Skills Group C assesses the same research competencies the Question Method develops: issue-spotting, source evaluation, analogical reasoning, and research closure. Consistent practice of the method’s five steps throughout law school is also bar exam preparation.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- The NextGen Bar Exam tests legal research as a discrete skill, balanced between litigation and transactional practice.
- Five named skills are tested: ambiguity spotting, source ranking, dispositive fact identification, research gap identification, and research sufficiency.
- The Question Method provides a compressed but applicable framework for bar exam research questions.
- Ranking authorities by the hierarchy of legal authority is the core skill for “which source controls” questions.
- Research closure, knowing when the materials are sufficient, is both a Question Method skill and a bar exam competency.
:::

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test compressed research under time pressure and your command of the five named skills.

::: {.exercise #ex-16-1}
### Exercise 16.1 — The Clocked Cycle and the Five-Skill Audit

Step one, the clock. Take a closed universe of sources: a source set your instructor provides, or the Research Log from a Part I drill you completed, with the logged sources as the provided materials and nothing else allowed in. Put forty-five minutes on a clock and run the compressed cycle from this chapter: state the macro question, break it into its research questions, rank the provided authorities by binding effect, name the ambiguity the problem turns on, apply the controlling authority to the facts, and finish with the sufficiency call, either "these materials answer the question" or a named gap and what would fill it. Step two, when time expires, the audit: mark where each of the five named skills appears in your answer, ambiguity spotting, source ranking, dispositive fact identification, gap identification, and closure.

**Deliverable:** the written answer produced inside the clock, with the five marks placed, or an honest note on any mark you could not place.

**Self-check:** the sufficiency call must be present and committed; "probably enough" is not a call. And a skill you cannot point to in your own answer is a skill you skipped; an unplaceable mark is next week's assignment, not a failure. Run the exercise again on different material next week, and once more the week after, because compression is a skill you build on material you already own.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-16-2}
### Exercise 16.2 — Break It Down, No Searching

A bar-style item, run entirely from this page. A client was fired for refusing Sunday shifts she says her religion forbids. The provided materials: the state's employment-discrimination statute; two decisions of the state's intermediate appellate court applying it; one decision of a neighboring state's supreme court; and one law review article. In ten minutes and without searching: state the macro question; break it into the research questions the materials would need to answer; say which provided material would answer each; and rank the materials by binding effect for the state's trial court.

**Deliverable:** the breakdown and the ranking.

**Self-check:** the neighboring state's decision and the article belong at the bottom of your ranking, persuasive and secondary respectively; if either appeared as the answer to a controlling-rule question, revisit the hierarchy of authority, because ranking the provided materials is the core skill behind every "which source controls" question the exam asks.
:::
