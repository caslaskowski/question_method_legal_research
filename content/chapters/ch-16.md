The NextGen Bar Exam tests legal research skills as part of Foundational Skills Group C. The purpose of that group is to assess how well an examinee can identify and implement research strategies, including preliminary issue-spotting, working with provided resources, developing and refining a theory of the case, and reaching closure on research questions. The Question Method prepares you for exactly this, not as a separate exam-prep exercise but as the natural byproduct of practicing the method throughout law school.

## Five Core Research Skills

The NCBE competencies consolidate into five skills, which this textbook names as follows:

- **Skill 1: Ambiguity spotting.** Reading a legal text and identifying terms, phrases, or provisions whose meaning requires interpretive research. Look for undefined terms, vague standards, terms of art, structural ambiguities, and scope questions. Developed in Chapter 6 (Statutes) and Appendix C; applied in Chapters 5, 7, and 8.
- **Skill 2: Intra-jurisdiction source ranking.** Given a collection of authorities from a single jurisdiction, ranking them correctly by binding effect. A state supreme court decision ranks above a court of appeals decision. A statute controls over common law on the same subject. Developed in Chapter 1; applied throughout.
- **Skill 3: Dispositive fact identification.** Reading a judicial opinion to identify the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome, then comparing those facts to a client’s facts. Locate the court’s analytical reasoning and ask whether different facts would change the result. Developed in Chapter 5 (Case Law) and Appendix B.
- **Skill 4: Research gap identification.** Recognizing when a source set does not fully answer the question and articulating what would fill the gap. Distinguish “the law is genuinely unclear” from “I have not yet found the answer.” (This is the line Chapters 8 and 9 drilled.) Developed in Chapter 8 (Researching Across Sources).
- **Skill 5: Research sufficiency and closure.** Determining when research is complete, which is a professional judgment. Research is sufficient when all questions are answered or identified as open, all authorities are validated, the framework is consistent, and counterarguments have been addressed. Developed in Chapters 3 and 9.

## Practice Strategies for Bar Research Questions

**Approaching provided-materials questions.** Read all the provided materials before you attempt an answer, and call this move excerpt triage. Build a mental map of what each source provides. Identify binding versus persuasive authorities. Identify ambiguities and note whether the provided cases resolve them. Look for analogous and distinguishable facts. Note gaps. The exam, in effect, hands you a closed universe of sources and asks you to run a compressed Question Method cycle over them.

::: {.callout}
[Example]{.box-title}

**Excerpt triage, run once.** The item: a customer sues a convenience store after a parking-lot assault, and the provided materials are (1) the state supreme court’s decision adopting a special-relationship exception to the general no-duty rule, (2) an intermediate appellate decision applying that exception to a gas station with two prior robberies, (3) a neighboring state’s supreme court decision using a different foreseeability test, and (4) the state’s premises-liability statute, which addresses physical conditions of the property and says nothing about third-party crime. The triage pass, before writing a word: the supreme court decision controls and supplies the rule; the appellate decision binds and supplies the closest application; the neighboring state’s decision persuades only; and the statute, read for scope, does not reach the issue, which is a finding, not a discard. The ambiguity the item was built around: what makes third-party crime foreseeable enough to ground a duty, the point on which the two in-state decisions leave room. The compressed cycle then runs on the mapped materials: macro question (did the store owe this customer a duty?); its research questions (what is the rule; what makes crime foreseeable under it; how do these facts compare to the applied case); each answered from the source triage identified for it; and the sufficiency call, committed—the materials answer the duty question, and any element they do not address is named as the gap rather than papered over. Forty-five minutes of exam time is this whole cycle at compression, and every move in it is one you have already run at full size.
:::

**Using the Question Method under time constraints.** The framework applies in compressed form: What is the macro question, and what research questions does it break into? What authority controls? What does it say? How does it apply to these facts? Are the provided materials sufficient?

**Identifying the strongest authority.** Rank the provided authorities by the hierarchy: mandatory authority from the highest court controls; persuasive authority supports. A clear statutory provision controls over case law interpreting a different statute.

### Practice in a Closed Universe

The exam hands you a small set of sources and a client problem, and everything you may rely on is on the page. Here is a universe of that kind. Every source in it is fictional. A client's apartment has had no heat for three weeks in January.

::: {.try kind="annotate" title="Skill 1: find the terms that will need research" hint="Read the statute, then select each marked phrase. Which ones does the text settle, and which ones hand you a question?"}
**Residential Rental Standards Act § 12.** (a) A landlord shall maintain every residential rental unit in [habitable condition]{.m label="Vague standard" note="The statute does not say what makes a unit habitable. A term like this is filled in by case law, and the universe supplies it: *Marsh* holds the duty includes adequate heat."}, including [operable heating facilities]{.m label="Settled by the text" note="Not ambiguous for this client. The statute names heating by its own terms. Spotting ambiguity includes recognizing where there is none."} capable of maintaining [safe indoor temperatures]{.m label="Vague standard" note="Safe for whom, measured how? Nothing in the universe defines it. If the landlord claims the unit stayed at 58 degrees, this phrase becomes the fight."} during the [heating season]{.m label="Undefined term" note="No dates are given. For a January outage it will not matter. For one in late April it would be the first question."}. (b) Upon [written notice]{.m label="Condition" note="Clear enough in meaning, and easy to miss in effect: the repair duty in (b) is triggered by notice in writing. Whether the client gave it is a fact to find, not a term to research."} of a defect under subsection (a), the landlord shall repair within a [reasonable time]{.m label="Vague standard" note="The classic open-textured phrase. How long is reasonable in January is a question for cases, and this universe contains none on the point. Name the gap."}. (c) Remedies for violation of this section are [as provided by law]{.m label="Points outside the text" note="The statute does not list remedies. It sends you elsewhere. In this universe, elsewhere is *Okafor* (withholding and repair-and-deduct) and *Delgado* (damages)."}.
:::

The universe also holds three decisions. *Marsh v. Golden Rentals* (state supreme court): a residential landlord's duty to keep a rental fit to live in includes providing adequate heat during the cold months. *Okafor v. Canyon Ridge Apartments* (state supreme court): a tenant facing an unrepaired habitability defect may repair and deduct, or may withhold rent, but only after written notice and a reasonable opportunity to cure; an earlier decision permitting withholding without notice is disapproved. *Delgado v. Mesa Verde Rentals* (court of appeals): damages for a habitability breach are generally limited to the loss in rental value, and emotional-distress damages require egregious conduct.

::: {.try kind="choice" title="Skill 2: which authority controls?"}
The client stopped paying rent yesterday without telling the landlord anything. Within this universe, which authority controls the conditions for withholding rent?

- [x] *Okafor*, the supreme court's rule, which fills in what § 12(c) leaves to “law.”
    - The statute frames and the highest court fills. *Okafor* conditions withholding on written notice and a chance to cure, and the client skipped the condition.
- [ ] Section 12(b), because a statute outranks a case.
    - A statute does outrank a case when they address the same thing. Section 12(b) governs when the landlord must repair. It says nothing about withholding, and § 12(c) points outward.
- [ ] *Delgado*, as the most specific decision on remedies.
    - *Delgado* addresses damages, not withholding, and as a court of appeals decision it ranks below *Okafor* in any event.
:::

::: {.try kind="choice" title="Skill 4: is the answer in the universe at all?"}
The client asks whether she can recover her moving costs if she has to relocate. Answer from the universe.

- [ ] Yes. Section 12(c) makes remedies available “as provided by law.”
    - Section 12(c) points to law the universe does not contain on this point. A pointer to nothing is a gap.
- [ ] No. *Delgado* limits damages to lost rental value.
    - Tempting. *Delgado* says “generally,” it was deciding an emotional-distress claim, and it never mentions relocation. Stretching a holding past its facts is the classic trap.
- [x] The universe does not answer it. Say so, and say what kind of source would.
    - Research gap identification. The competent answer names the gap and what would fill it, such as a statute or decision on consequential damages, and does not dress a guess as analysis.
:::

## Common Pitfalls

Under time pressure, the tempting mistake is to start writing before reading every provided source, which is how examinees miss the one authority that controls the answer. Close behind is misranking what they have read, treating a persuasive decision or an off-point statute as though it controlled; the hierarchy of authority is the whole game on “which source governs” questions. Another is failing to spot the ambiguity the question was built around, since the exam usually hides an undefined term or a scope question that the provided cases are there to resolve. And examinees often forget the closure step, declaring an answer without asking whether the provided materials are actually sufficient or whether a gap remains to be named.

## Chapter Recap

The NextGen Bar Exam’s Foundational Skills Group C assesses the same research competencies the Question Method develops: issue-spotting, source evaluation, analogical reasoning, and research closure. Consistent practice of the method’s five steps throughout law school is also bar exam preparation.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- The NextGen Bar Exam tests legal research as a discrete skill, balanced between litigation and transactional practice.
- Five named skills are tested: ambiguity spotting, source ranking, dispositive fact identification, research gap identification, and research sufficiency.
- The Question Method provides a compressed but applicable framework for bar exam research questions.
- Ranking authorities by the hierarchy of legal authority is the core skill for “which source controls” questions.
- Research closure, knowing when the materials are sufficient, is both a Question Method skill and a bar exam competency.
:::

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test compressed research under time pressure and your command of the five named skills.

::: {.exercise #ex-16-1}
### Exercise 16.1 — The Clocked Cycle and the Five-Skill Audit

Step one, the clock. Take a closed universe of sources: a source set your instructor provides, or the Research Log from a Part I drill you completed, with the logged sources as the provided materials and nothing else allowed in. Put forty-five minutes on a clock and run the compressed cycle from this chapter: state the macro question, break it into its research questions, rank the provided authorities by binding effect, name the ambiguity the problem turns on, apply the controlling authority to the facts, and finish with the sufficiency call, either "these materials answer the question" or a named gap and what would fill it. Step two, when time expires, the audit: mark where each of the five named skills appears in your answer, ambiguity spotting, source ranking, dispositive fact identification, gap identification, and closure.

**Deliverable:** the written answer produced inside the clock, with the five marks placed, or an honest note on any mark you could not place.

**Self-check:** the sufficiency call must be present and committed; "probably enough" is not a call. And a skill you cannot point to in your own answer is a skill you skipped; an unplaceable mark is next week's assignment, not a failure. Run the exercise again on different material next week, and once more the week after, because compression is a skill you build on material you already own.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-16-2}
### Exercise 16.2 — Break It Down, No Searching

A bar-style item, run entirely from this page. A client was fired for refusing Sunday shifts she says her religion forbids. The provided materials: the state's employment-discrimination statute; two decisions of the state's intermediate appellate court applying it; one decision of a neighboring state's supreme court; and one law review article. In ten minutes and without searching: state the macro question; break it into the research questions the materials would need to answer; say which provided material would answer each; and rank the materials by binding effect for the state's trial court.

**Deliverable:** the breakdown and the ranking.

**Self-check:** the neighboring state's decision and the article belong at the bottom of your ranking, persuasive and secondary respectively; if either appeared as the answer to a controlling-rule question, revisit the hierarchy of authority, because ranking the provided materials is the core skill behind every "which source controls" question the exam asks.
:::
