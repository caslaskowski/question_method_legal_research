Chapter 7 introduced the basics of regulatory research: locating regulations in the CFR and tracing their rulemaking history. This chapter extends that foundation to the full range of materials agencies produce, including guidance documents, policy statements, opinion letters, and adjudicatory decisions. You need these materials, because agencies often clarify, and sometimes change, their positions through guidance rather than rulemaking, and the practical answer to a client’s question frequently lives in these materials rather than in the CFR itself.

## Advanced Administrative Materials and the Research Cycle

Advanced administrative research is what the Question Method points you toward when the formal regulation answers your question only partway. Chapter 7’s regulatory cycle ends with a recurring kind of next question: the CFR text states a standard but does not tell you what it means in practice, or how the agency actually enforces it. That residual question is the entry point to this chapter’s materials.

The method shapes how you find these materials, because the type of unanswered question tells you which agency product to seek. A question about what a vague regulatory term means in operation (“what does *adequate ventilation* actually require?”) points to interpretive guidance, compliance manuals, and opinion letters. A question about how the agency has applied the regulation to specific facts (“has the agency found conduct like my client’s to be a violation?”) points to administrative adjudication decisions, the agency’s equivalent of case law. A question about enforcement priorities points to enforcement guidelines and recent actions. Each question type maps to a different agency output, and naming the question keeps you from treating all “agency stuff” as interchangeable.

The method also shapes how you weigh what you find, which matters more here than almost anywhere else in research. Because guidance documents are influential but not binding, every finding from this chapter’s materials has to be logged with an explicit note about its authority level: binding regulation, persuasive guidance, or merely advisory. In practice, that adds one required clause to the Chapter 3 Research Log entry. Open the Findings field with the authority level, before you say what the document says, so that no finding sits in your log without its weight attached. If an administrative project needs more, add an Authority Level field of your own; either way, the tool Chapter 3 handed you and the tool this chapter assumes should stay the same tool. The method’s habit of recording whether a source fully answers your question, partially answers it, or only redirects you becomes, in administrative research, a habit of recording “and how much legal weight this answer actually carries.” Skipping that step is how researchers end up advising clients that something is legally required when it is only the agency’s non-binding preference.

## Questions This Source Answers

Advanced administrative research typically arises from Focusing questions that formal regulations do not fully answer (“The regulation requires *adequate ventilation*, so how does OSHA define adequate in practice?”) and Application questions about agency enforcement (“Has the FTC taken enforcement action against this type of marketing claim?”). These questions push past the CFR text to the full range of materials agencies produce.

## How Advanced Administrative Materials Are Made and What That Means for Research

### Guidance Documents: Influential but Not Binding

Guidance documents, including agency manuals, policy statements, interpretive rules, compliance manuals, FAQ documents, staff memoranda, and enforcement guidelines, do not go through notice-and-comment and do not have the force of law. They are enormously influential anyway. Regulated entities follow them, enforcement decisions reflect them, and courts give them some deference. *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944), established that courts may give guidance documents persuasive weight proportional to the agency’s expertise and the thoroughness of its reasoning. Always distinguish a binding regulation from a guidance document when you advise a client, because that distinction can decide whether the client is legally required to do something or merely advised to.

### Administrative Adjudication

Many federal agencies resolve disputes through their own administrative processes. An administrative law judge (ALJ) hears cases; parties may appeal to the agency head and then to federal court. Administrative decisions are published and may function as precedent within the agency. They are often the most detailed interpretations of a regulation available, because they apply the regulation to specific factual patterns—exactly the application analysis a bare regulation cannot give you.

### Post-Loper Bright: No More Chevron Deference

Under the former *Chevron* framework, courts deferred to an agency’s reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous statute. In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), the Supreme Court overruled *Chevron*. Courts now independently determine the best interpretation of ambiguous statutes rather than simply accepting the agency’s view. This does not eliminate *Skidmore* deference for informal guidance, but it fundamentally changes how courts evaluate regulations that reach beyond clear statutory text. For researchers, agency guidance still tells you how the agency will enforce the law, but it carries less guaranteed judicial weight than it once did. Always check how courts in your jurisdiction have treated the specific guidance document you are relying on.

Two refinements matter in practice. First, the deference question changes when the ambiguity sits in an agency’s own *regulation* rather than in a statute. Courts apply a separate and narrower doctrine there, associated with *Auer v. Robbins*, 519 U.S. 452 (1997), as limited by *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019), which asks whether the regulation is genuinely ambiguous and whether the agency’s reading is authoritative, reasonable, and grounded in its expertise. That is the doctrine you will meet when you search for how an agency defines a vague regulatory term. Second, *Loper Bright* did not reopen every earlier decision that had applied *Chevron*; those holdings keep their force as precedent. Chapter 10 raised a parallel question about legislative history: how much weight should a court give an account of what a statute means that the court did not write and that the enacted text does not itself contain? After *Loper Bright*, an agency’s reading arrives in a similar posture. It is something a court may find persuasive, not something it must accept. Check which way the court in front of you leans before you decide how hard to lean on the agency.

## Searching for Advanced Administrative Materials

- **Agency websites and electronic reading rooms.** Most agencies publish guidance documents in dedicated Guidance or Policy sections. The quality and organization of these repositories varies dramatically by agency.
- **Regulations.gov.** The federal rulemaking portal includes guidance documents in the docket alongside formal rulemaking materials and provides access to public comments on proposed rules.
- **Federal Register.** Some guidance documents are published as “notices” even though they are not regulations.
- **Commercial databases.** Westlaw, Lexis, and Bloomberg Law include selected agency guidance documents and adjudicatory decisions, though coverage is not comprehensive. Agency-specific sources (the SEC’s website for no-action letters, the NLRB website for board opinions, the EEOC for enforcement guidance) are often more comprehensive for their respective agencies.
- **FOIA requests.** The Freedom of Information Act (5 U.S.C. § 552) can be used to request unpublished agency documents, internal guidance, and enforcement records. Responses can take months or years, so factor that into your timeline.

## Answering Questions with Administrative Materials

Administrative materials answer the questions that formal regulations leave open. If your question asks what an employer must do to accommodate an employee’s religious practice, the binding regulation (29 C.F.R. Part 1605) provides the framework, but the EEOC’s Compliance Manual and enforcement guidance explain how the agency applies that framework in practice. Administrative adjudication decisions show how the agency has ruled on specific fact patterns. Each answers a different level of question about the same issue, and your annotated outline should keep them visibly distinct so the binding framework is never confused with the persuasive gloss.

### Evaluating Administrative Authority

Binding authority includes regulations properly promulgated through notice-and-comment and administrative adjudication decisions (binding within the agency, subject to judicial review). Non-binding but influential authority includes guidance documents, policy statements, and interpretive rules (*Skidmore* deference), along with no-action letters and informal opinion letters (advisory only). When you cite administrative materials, always characterize their authority level accurately. Representing a guidance document as binding law is a professional error, and after *Loper Bright*, an increasingly consequential one.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Must the client comply, or is the agency advising?" bins="Force of law | Persuasive only"}
- A rule adopted through notice-and-comment and codified in the CFR **Force of law**
    - Properly promulgated regulations bind regulated parties.
- An FAQ page on the agency's website **Persuasive only**
    - Guidance. It tells you how the agency reads its rule and will probably enforce it. It is weighed under *Skidmore*, not obeyed as law.
- The agency's enforcement manual for its own inspectors **Persuasive only**
    - Guidance again, and often the best window into enforcement priorities.
- An interpretive notice the agency published in the Federal Register **Persuasive only**
    - Publication in the Federal Register does not turn guidance into a rule. Ask how it was made, not where it was printed.
- A no-action letter issued to a different company **Persuasive only**
    - Advisory, and addressed to someone else's facts.
- A published decision of the agency's appeals board, cited in a later proceeding before the same agency **Force of law**
    - Adjudication decisions bind within the agency, subject to judicial review. Outside the agency, a court reads them as the agency's view.
:::

## Common Pitfalls

The error that matters most in administrative research is treating a guidance document as binding law, because the line between “legally required” and “the agency’s preference” is exactly the line a client is paying you to draw, and *Loper Bright* has made that line sharper. A related slip is failing to record an authority level for each finding, which is how a persuasive gloss quietly migrates into your outline as if it were the binding rule. Watch, too, for stopping at the CFR when the real answer lives in the agency’s adjudication decisions or compliance manual, the materials that show the regulation applied to facts like your client’s. And do not lean on FOIA when the clock is running, since responses can take months or years and are no help on an urgent matter.

## Beyond the Basics

### FOIA as a Research Tool

The Freedom of Information Act requires federal agencies to disclose most records on request. FOIA requests can surface internal agency documents, unpublished guidance, enforcement records, and decision-making processes not otherwise available. Responses can take months or years, which makes FOIA impractical for urgent matters but valuable for thorough compliance or scholarly research.

### State Administrative Adjudication

States have their own administrative court systems, varying widely in organization and availability. Check your state’s administrative procedures act and the relevant agency’s website. Some states publish ALJ decisions in comprehensive searchable databases; others provide only limited access.

## Chapter Recap

Administrative agencies produce a range of materials beyond formal regulations. Guidance documents are influential but not binding; administrative adjudication decisions interpret regulations in specific factual contexts. Post-*Loper Bright*, courts independently evaluate agency statutory interpretations. Effective advanced administrative research means identifying not just what an agency has enacted but what it has said through guidance and decided through adjudication, and recording, for each, exactly how much weight it carries.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Guidance documents are influential but not legally binding; only formally promulgated regulations carry the force of law.
- Administrative adjudication produces decisions that function as precedent within an agency’s domain.
- Post-*Loper Bright*, courts determine the best interpretation independently, without automatic deference to agency statutory interpretations.
- FOIA requests can uncover otherwise unavailable agency documents.
- Always distinguish the type of administrative material and represent its authority accurately.
:::

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test your ability to distinguish binding regulations from agency guidance and to record an authority level for everything you find. All three work the same area this chapter opened: religious accommodation in employment.

::: {.exercise #ex-11-1}
### Exercise 11.1 — Binding or Guidance?

Locate the binding framework at 29 C.F.R. Part 1605, one EEOC guidance document interpreting it, and one adjudication decision applying it to facts, or, where the agency's decisions prove hard to access, an adjudication from another agency your instructor designates, logged the same way. Log all three, each with an explicit authority level: binding regulation, persuasive guidance, or advisory.

**Deliverable:** three Research Log entries, each with the authority level stated at the start of the Findings field.

**Self-check:** the log entry is the exercise. If any entry's authority level is missing from the start of Findings, or hedged, redo it, because skipping that clause is precisely how a persuasive gloss migrates into an outline dressed as the rule.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-11-2}
### Exercise 11.2 — The Vague-Term Drill

Pick the vaguest operative term you met in the regulation and ask how the agency defines it in practice. Find the answer, and note which agency product answered you: a compliance manual, an opinion letter, an adjudication. The question type told you where to look; this pass tests whether you heard it.

**Deliverable:** the term, the practical definition with its source, and one sentence naming which of this chapter's question-to-product mappings your search followed.

**Self-check:** if your answer came from the CFR text itself, the term you picked was not vague enough to need this chapter. Pick again. The drill only works when the regulation genuinely runs out.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-11-3}
### Exercise 11.3 — The Post-Loper Bright Check

Take any agency guidance document in this area and search a case-law database for opinions that cite it by name or title, and check what your citator does and does not cover for agency guidance, to find how courts have actually treated it.

**Deliverable:** a short note: what weight, if any, courts have given the document, with citations, or a documented finding that no court has addressed it.

**Self-check:** "legally required" versus "the agency's preference" is the line a client pays you to draw. Your note should let a reader place your guidance document on the correct side of that line without reading anything else. Draw the line three times here, cheaply, before you ever have to draw it for real.
:::
