Chapter 2 introduced the opening phase of search: natural language searching, synonym mapping, and the core Boolean connectors. This chapter picks up where that process leads, at the moment your questions become specific and your goal shifts from discovery to precision.

At this stage you know the legal terminology and you know which sources govern. Your remaining questions are targeted: Does the rule apply in this specific factual context? Is there a circuit split on this point? Has this particular statutory phrase been interpreted by courts in my jurisdiction? Those questions call for search tools built for precision, not exploration. This chapter also takes on the question every new researcher struggles with: when am I done? The narrowing tools and the closing-out protocol are two halves of the same final phase. One finds the right authority, the other confirms you have all of it.

::: {.objectives}
[Learning Objectives]{.box-title}

- Construct precise Boolean searches using proximity, exclusion, and frequency connectors
- Transition from broad orientation searching to precise, targeted retrieval as your questions narrow
- Apply a systematic closing-out protocol to confirm research is complete, current, and gap-free
- Diagnose and respond to common research problems: no results, too many results, conflicting authority, circular trails
- Document the research closure decision in a defensible, professional manner
:::

## The Full Connector Set: Proximity, Frequency, and Exclusion

Building on the core connectors from Chapter 2, this chapter adds the precision tools that make Boolean searching a real narrowing instrument.

**Proximity connectors** specify how close together terms must appear, which dramatically cuts false positives. /n requires terms within n words of each other in either order (landlord /5 heat, within five words). /s requires terms within the same sentence. /p requires terms within the same paragraph. Be aware that /s and /p do not look for terms within sentences or paragraphs as we think of them structurally. They are database shorthand for a default number. For example, /s might be shorthand for /14. What the actual number is a database-specific artifact they don’t share.

Databases also allow you to determine distance between terms as well as the order. pre/n requires the first term to appear before the second within n words (reasonable pre/3 accommodation). Platforms also allow you to use connectors to exclude results where terms are within a distance from each other (e.g., cases where medical is within three words of liability), but the syntax is not uniform. At the time of publication, Lexis uses *term NOT /n term* while Westlaw uses BUT *NOT (or %) term /n term*. Check the system’s documentation before attempting to use proximity exclusion.

**Frequency connectors.** ATLEAST n(term) requires the term to appear at least n times in the document. ATLEAST3(preemption) retrieves only documents where preemption is discussed substantively rather than mentioned in passing. This helps you find documents where a concept is a major focus rather than a stray reference.

## Translating Your Research Question into a Precision Search

The discipline of the Question Method pays off here, because a precisely stated question already contains the terms and relationships you need for a Boolean search. This is the most direct link between the method and the mechanics: a well-drafted question is, in effect, a Boolean search waiting to be formatted.

**Example.** Your question is “Have Ninth Circuit courts held that remote work is a required reasonable accommodation under the ADA for employees with anxiety disorders?” Extract the components: core concepts (ADA, reasonable accommodation, remote work / telecommute / work from home, anxiety); the relationship (accommodation and remote work should be close together); the jurisdiction (Ninth Circuit filter). The resulting search: “reasonable accommodation” /10 (remote! OR telecommut! OR “work from home”) AND (anxiety OR “mental health”), with the Ninth Circuit filter applied.

Now look at the same question translated badly: ADA AND accommodation AND remote AND anxiety. Every concept is welded to a single word, so the telecommuting cases vanish, and nothing requires the concepts to appear anywhere near one another, so a passing mention of anxiety in an unrelated ADA opinion still qualifies. Set the two side by side and you can see what the good version is actually doing: synonyms gathered with OR, concepts bound with proximity, jurisdiction filtered. A bad example next to a good one is the fastest way to learn the difference.

The general principle: find all synonyms for a concept using OR and the expansion operators; require concepts to co-occur using AND; specify how close together they must appear using proximity connectors; find specific legal tests using quotation marks; exclude irrelevant context using NOT; require substantive treatment using ATLEAST; and require term order using pre/n.

### Database-Specific Connector Syntax

Westlaw, Lexis+, and Bloomberg Law use similar but not identical syntax. Westlaw uses /n for proximity; Bloomberg uses W/n; Lexis uses /n but with slightly different behavior for /s and /p. Always check the syntax documentation for the specific database before you run precision searches. A misread connector can produce a radically different result set, and worse, it can silently exclude the very authority you are looking for. You can find each database’s terms and connectors in their help file.

## Building a Search Log

For any substantial project, record each search string, the database used, the number of results, and whether the search was productive. A search log keeps you from repeating yourself, provides evidence of thoroughness, and supports your research closure documentation. It works hand in hand with the Research Log: the Research Log tracks the questions and answers, the search log tracks the exact queries that produced them. As your research log evolves with your the growth of your research skills, so too will your search log.

## The Closing-Out Protocol

Precision searching narrows your results; closing out confirms that what you found is complete and current. Notice that this is the Question Method’s Checkpoint again, run at full scale: where the checkpoint asks whether one answer was sufficient, the protocol asks whether the whole project is.

- **Run a final sweep.** Before you declare research complete, run at least one more search using different terms than the ones that produced your best results, and at least one search aimed deliberately at authority that cuts against your position, the adverse-authority question Rule 3.3 will hold you to (Chapter 10). These additional searches, even when they turn up nothing new, create a documented record that you searched comprehensively.
- **Update every case and statute.** Run KeyCite or Shepard’s on every primary authority you plan to cite, and read each one for the specific proposition you cite it for, because you never cite what you have not read. For cases, check for negative treatment in your jurisdiction, and note which points of law it reaches. For statutes, check for pending amendments and recent session laws. For regulations, check the eCFR effective date and the LSA.
- **Evaluate the outline for gaps.** Every legal proposition should be supported by primary authority, and every ambiguous term should have a source addressing it. A gap is either a remaining research question or a genuine area of legal uncertainty your analysis must acknowledge.
- **Document the closure decision.** Record that you completed the protocol. Note the databases searched, the final search strings, the dates you verified authorities, and any open questions. This protects you professionally; if your research is ever questioned, you can show exactly what you did and why you concluded it was enough.

## The Difference Between No Results and No Law

A search that returns nothing means one of two things: there is no authority on the point, or your search was too narrow. Before you conclude the law is silent, try alternative terms, remove proximity constraints, expand to persuasive authority from other jurisdictions, and check secondary sources for discussion of the issue. Only after you have exhausted the alternatives is a “no results” conclusion defensible, and the difference matters enormously, because “there is no law” is a powerful argument while “I could not find any law” is a confession. (This is the same line Chapter 8 drew for research gaps; here it becomes a closing-out discipline.)

## When Research Goes Wrong: Diagnosing Common Problems

Every researcher hits moments when the research is not working. What sets competent researchers apart is that they diagnose the problem accurately and apply a specific correction instead of just searching harder.

- **No results.** Check whether your terms are too specific, and broaden with OR and synonyms, remove proximity constraints, or reduce the number of terms. Check whether the issue is genuinely novel and may call for analogous authority. Check whether you are in the wrong source type, the single-source trap from Chapter 8, and should return to a secondary source.
- **Too many results.** The search is probably too broad. Add a proximity connector, add a third term, apply jurisdiction and date filters, or use ATLEAST for substantive treatment. Remember that you are looking for the answer to a specific question, not every possibly relevant document.
- **Conflicting authority.** Diagnose whether the conflict is jurisdictional, factual, temporal, or doctrinal. Apply the hierarchy of authority. Address the conflict honestly in your analysis; a genuine split is itself a research finding, not something to hide.
- **The law is genuinely uncertain.** Before you conclude the law is unsettled, verify with secondary sources, search for analogous authority, and check persuasive authority from other jurisdictions. Law review articles, Restatement provisions, and legislative history give you the analytical framework for genuinely open questions.

## Common Pitfalls

The temptation at the finish line is to declare victory the moment you find your best authority, but skipping the final sweep with alternative terms is how a controlling case slips past you. A quieter danger lives in the syntax itself: a mistyped connector does not error out, it simply returns the wrong set, sometimes excluding the very authority you needed, so verify the database’s connector rules before you trust a precision search. Be careful, too, not to read an empty result list as proof the law is silent; “I could not find it” and “it does not exist” are very different claims, and only the second is an argument. Finally, do not leave closure undocumented. The note explaining what you searched and why you stopped is what protects you if the work is ever questioned.

## Chapter Recap

Narrowing and closing out are the final phases of every research cycle. Precision Boolean searching converts broad orientation results into a targeted, comprehensive authority set. The closing-out protocol confirms that every authority is current, every gap is identified, and the research decision is documented. Together they convert “I think I’m done” into “I can demonstrate that I’m done.”

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Proximity connectors (/n, /s, /p), platform-specific exclusion operators, ATLEAST, and the exact-phrase quotation are the primary precision instruments.
- Translate your research questions directly into Boolean searches: key terms become search terms, and the relationship between them determines the connectors.
- Closing out requires a final sweep with alternative terms, citator updates on all primary authority, gap analysis against the annotated outline, and documentation of the closure decision.
- Document the closure decision in your Research Log.
- No results is not the same as no law; exhaust alternative search strategies before concluding the law is silent.
:::

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test precision search construction and the discipline of documented closure. Each one writes to the Search Log.

::: {.exercise #ex-9-1}
### Exercise 9.1 — Precision Translation

Take three research questions from a current project, or from your instructor's set, or from your own completed drills, and translate each into a precision search: synonyms gathered with OR, concepts bound with proximity connectors rather than bare ANDs, jurisdiction and source type filtered before the search runs. Record every search in your Search Log as you go: the query as entered, the filters, the result count, and one line on result quality.

**Deliverable:** three Search Log sequences, one per question.

**Self-check:** every search string should use at least one proximity connector doing real work. If AND is the only connective tissue in all three strings, you translated the Chapter 2 way, and this chapter existed to upgrade you.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-9-2}
### Exercise 9.2 — The Bad Translation

Take this question: is a landlord liable when a tenant's guest is injured on a broken staircase? Translate it twice. First, deliberately badly: all ANDs, single words, no synonyms, no proximity, no filters. Then properly, with the full Chapter 9 toolkit. Run both versions back to back and write two sentences on what the two result sets tell you. Contrast is the fastest way to sharpen the discrimination this chapter teaches, and building the bad version yourself teaches more than being shown one.

**Deliverable:** both strings, both result counts, and your two sentences.

**Self-check:** your sentences should name a specific difference, in volume, in relevance of the first page, in what floated to the top, not a generic "the good one was better." If you cannot name the specific difference, look at result ten in each list and start there.
:::

### Exercise 9.3 — The Closing-Out Run

Run this chapter's closing-out protocol, every step, on any research chain you have completed in this course (a Part I drill works well), and document the closure decision in your Research Log: the final sweep with alternative terms, the citator updates, the check against the original question, and your explicit finding of analytical closure or a named remaining gap.

**Deliverable:** the documented closure decision.

**Self-check:** one test, and it is the chapter's test. If you cannot document why you stopped, you have not closed. You have just stopped.
