Everything you have learned so far applies across all areas of practice. But how you apply it, the sources you prioritize, the questions you ask, the closure criteria you use, varies depending on whether you are litigating a case, closing a transaction, advising on regulatory compliance, or doing something else. The NextGen Bar Exam recognizes this; it is built to balance the skills and knowledge needed in litigation and transactional practice. This chapter prepares you for that balance by showing how the Question Method adapts to different contexts.

::: {.objectives}
[Learning Objectives]{.box-title}

- Identify how research strategies differ across litigation, transactional, and regulatory practice contexts
- Adapt the Question Method to the distinct research demands of each practice area
- Recognize the source types and databases most relevant to each practice context
- Apply different research closure criteria depending on the professional context
:::

## Practice-Specific Research and the Research Cycle

The Question Method is deliberately context-neutral: question, research, synthesize, next question, repeat. That neutrality is its strength, because the same disciplined cycle survives the move from a litigation file to a merger to a compliance audit. What changes from one context to the next is not the cycle but the parameters the cycle runs with.

Three parameters shift. First, the default question type shifts: litigation runs heavily on application questions (how do these facts compare to the controlling cases?); transactional work runs on focusing questions about requirements and risks (what does this deal type require?); regulatory work spans all four types. Second, the first source shifts: litigation starts in case law, transactional work starts in statutes and standardized forms, regulatory work starts in the CFR and agency guidance. Third, and most important, the closure criterion shifts: what counts as “research sufficient” in litigation (controlling authority for each issue, adverse authority identified) differs from what counts as sufficient in a transaction (all applicable requirements and risks identified) or in compliance work (all obligations mapped).

This is why the method is worth learning as a method rather than as a set of source-specific recipes. A lawyer who has internalized the cycle can walk into an unfamiliar practice area and immediately ask the right meta-question, “in this context, what is my default question type, where do I start, and what will tell me I’m done?”, and then run the same five steps they have always run. The Research Log even changes function accordingly: in litigation it documents the authority behind each argument; in transactional practice it tracks requirements and risk factors; in regulatory practice it maps compliance obligations.

## Litigation Research

A court will sometimes chastise counsel whose motion ignored a settled body of case law:

*In this case, for example, plaintiff ignores basic principles and long standing consistent case law covering motions for reconsideration and fails to make the showing required under that consistent body of case law.*

*Kiss Pharm LLC v. Becker Prof’l Dev. Corp.*, No. 18 C 7848, 2021 WL 3207822, at \*[\[pin\]]{.flag} (N.D. Ill. July 29, 2021).

**The research context.** Litigation research supports the prosecution or defense of claims in court. The goal is to find controlling authority governing the disputed issues, identify and address adverse authority, and build a framework for persuasive argument. Case law is the dominant source, because the arguments you make to a court have to be supported by judicial authority. Statutes and regulations provide governing rules, and the case law interpreting them is equally important. Court rules and local rules govern procedural requirements, and failing to follow them can be dispositive.

**Characteristic questions.** Application questions dominate: “How do the facts of my case compare to the leading cases?” “Does the statute of limitations bar my client’s claim given these specific dates?” “What standard of review applies to this issue on appeal?”

**Research closure in litigation.** Controlling authority identified for each issue; adverse authority identified and addressed; procedural requirements confirmed; all cited authorities updated and validated.

**Specialized databases.** Docket research: PACER (federal), state court electronic filing systems, Bloomberg Law. Judicial analytics: tools tracking individual judges’ rulings and citation preferences. Brief banks: Westlaw, Lexis, Bloomberg Law.

## Transactional Research

**The research context.** Transactional research supports the negotiation, drafting, and closing of deals: mergers, acquisitions, financings, real estate transactions, corporate formations, and commercial contracts. The goal is to identify the legal requirements, risks, and market practices that should shape the structure and documentation. Statutes and regulations are typically dominant, because transactions have to comply with governing law.

**Characteristic questions.** Focusing questions about requirements and risks dominate: “What approvals are required for this type of corporate acquisition in Delaware?” “Are there regulatory restrictions on this type of financing arrangement?” “What representations and warranties are market-standard for this deal type?”

**Research closure in transactional practice.** All applicable regulatory requirements identified; tax implications analyzed; comparable transactions reviewed for market terms; risk factors identified and addressed in the deal documentation; due diligence complete.

**Specialized databases.** SEC EDGAR (essential and free for federal securities research; public company filings, prospectuses, precedent transaction documents). Practical Law (Westlaw) and Lexis Practice Advisor (market-standard forms, checklists, and practice notes organized by transaction type). Bloomberg Law (transactional intelligence tools, deal analytics).

## Regulatory and Compliance Research

**The research context.** Regulatory research supports clients navigating the requirements of administrative agencies. The goal is to identify all applicable requirements, understand how the agency interprets and enforces them, and assess the client’s compliance status. Regulations (the CFR) are the primary source, and agency guidance documents are essential for understanding enforcement in practice.

**Characteristic questions.** Definitional, focusing, and application questions all arise: “What does the EPA consider a *point source* under the Clean Water Act?” “What reporting obligations apply to this facility?” “Does my client’s waste management process comply with RCRA Subtitle C?”

**Research closure in regulatory practice.** All applicable regulations identified (federal, state, local); agency guidance consulted for interpretive questions; recent enforcement actions reviewed for current priorities; compliance status assessed against each applicable requirement; pending rulemaking checked for upcoming changes.

## Adapting the Question Method Across Practice Contexts

The five steps and intermittent checkpoints stay the same regardless of context; only the parameters move. You have now seen that shift played out three times: a different default question type, a different first source, a different closure criterion, and a Research Log doing a different job in each.

## Common Pitfalls

The most common practice-specific mistake is importing one context’s habits into another, like running a litigation-style case-law-first search on a transactional problem whose answer lives in statutes, regulations, and standardized forms. Closing out by the wrong criterion is a close relative: “I found controlling authority for each issue” is the right finish line in litigation but not in a transaction, where the question is whether you have identified every applicable requirement and risk. New lawyers also tend to reach reflexively for the source type they trained on rather than asking the meta-question first: “in this context, what is my default question type, where do I start, and what will tell me I’m done?” In an unfamiliar practice context, that reflex ripens quickly into Chapter 8’s single-source trap—a governing authority type never reached, or reached only through another source’s description of it—because the authority types that govern the new context are exactly the ones your old habits never taught you to open. And in any context, do not forget the procedural layer; in litigation especially, a substantively perfect argument can still be undone by a missed court rule.

## Chapter Recap

Research strategies differ across practice contexts, but the Question Method provides a consistent framework that adapts to each. Litigation research prioritizes case law and adversarial analysis. Transactional research prioritizes statutory requirements, regulatory compliance, and market practices. Regulatory research spans the full range of agency-produced materials. Understanding these differences prepares you for the NextGen Bar Exam’s balanced testing approach and for the varied demands of professional practice.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Litigation research prioritizes case law and adversarial analysis; closure requires controlling authority for each issue and identification of adverse authority.
- Transactional research prioritizes statutes, regulations, forms, and market practices; closure requires identification of all applicable requirements and risks.
- Regulatory research spans formal regulations, agency guidance, and enforcement actions; closure requires mapping all compliance obligations.
- The Question Method adapts to each context through different question types, source priorities, and closure criteria.
:::

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises test your ability to adapt the method's parameters, question type, first source, and closure criterion, to different practice contexts.

::: {.exercise #ex-14-1}
### Exercise 14.1 — Three Postures, One File

Reread the drone scenario printed in Chapter 8, same client, same facts, and research it three times without researching it once. Everything you need is the scenario itself and this chapter's parameters. From the litigation posture, a neighbor has sued over the flights; from the transactional posture, the client is selling the business, drone operation included; and from the compliance posture, the client wants an audit of everything the operation must do to stay legal. For each posture, write only the first three research questions you would draft. Then label each question's type, name the source you would open first, and state the closure criterion that would tell you the research was done. Nine questions, three postures, one page.

**Deliverable:** that one page.

**Self-check:** now read the page. The facts never changed, but the question types shifted, the first sources shifted, and the finish lines shifted. If any two postures produced the same three questions, you researched the file instead of the posture, and this chapter's entire argument is that the posture sets the parameters.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-14-2}
### Exercise 14.2 — The Meta-Question, Cold

Pick a practice context this chapter never mentions, immigration, tax, family law, anything, and answer the meta-question cold: in this context, what is my default question type, where do I start, and what will tell me I am done? Write your three answers, then spend fifteen minutes in a secondary source checking them, and revise.

**Deliverable:** both versions, cold and checked, with a line on what the check changed.

**Self-check:** you will not know the sources before the check, and that is fine. The lawyer who can ask the meta-question walks into any unfamiliar area already oriented, and if the check changed nothing at all, you either guessed remarkably well or checked remarkably little. Be honest about which.
:::
