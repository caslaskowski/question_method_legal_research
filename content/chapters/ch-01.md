Before you can research well, you have to know where answers are likely to live. That means understanding how the U.S. legal system is structured and what primary sources of law govern. Think of this chapter as the map you study before the trip. Every research question you ask in this course eventually lands on one or more of the primary sources described here, and knowing how those sources relate to one another is what lets you make smart decisions about where to look. A researcher who does not understand the hierarchy of authority cannot tell a source that settles a question from one that merely informs it, and will burn time reading material that could never control the outcome. This chapter is also the ground the Question Method stands on. When Chapter 3 asks you to write a research question and choose the source most likely to answer it, the choice will come from the US legal structure you are about to study.

## Sources of U.S. Law

The United States has a federal system, which means governing power is shared between the federal government and the states. Each level has its own constitution, its own legislature, its own agencies, and its own courts. The law itself comes from a handful of primary sources: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties. Knowing what each one is, and what each one can and cannot do, is the first step toward knowing where to look for an answer.

[**Constitutions**]{.gloss} set up the structure of government, define the limits of government power, and guarantee individual rights. A constitution is the supreme law of its jurisdiction, so any other law that conflicts with it is invalid. The U.S. Constitution structures the federal government and, through the Fourteenth Amendment and the doctrine of incorporation, also limits much of what state governments may do. Each state also has its own constitution, supreme within that state so long as it does not conflict with federal law.

[**Statutes**]{.gloss} are laws passed by a legislature, such as the U.S. Congress or a state legislature. Once enacted, they are first published as a standalone document called a **[slip law]{.gloss}**. They are later gathered chronologically and published in bound volumes called **[session laws]{.gloss}**. Then they are organized by subject into collections called codes, like the United States Code for federal law. A statute is the considered, generally applicable judgment of the elected legislature, and it can create rights, impose duties, prohibit conduct, and set up entire regulatory schemes.

**[Regulations]{.gloss}** are rules created by government agencies, like the Environmental Protection Agency or the Department of Labor, under authority a statute delegated to them. Regulations fill in the technical detail that statutes leave to the agencies. A statute might direct an agency to set “safe” exposure limits for a workplace chemical; the regulation supplies the actual number. Properly adopted regulations carry the force of law.

**[Case law]{.gloss}** is the body of published judicial opinions.When an appellate-level judge writes and publishes an opinion, that opinion generally becomes part of the law, binding future lower courts in the same jurisdiction under the doctrine of **[stare decisis]{.gloss}**. (There is some nuance here that you’ll find covered in Chapter 5.) Some case law is **[common law]{.gloss}**, rules courts created where no statute governs. For example, much of tort law comes from judicial decisions alone. The rest of case law interprets and applies constitutions, statutes, or regulations.

[**Treaties**]{.gloss} are formal agreements between the United States and foreign or tribal nations. Once ratified, a treaty has the force of federal law and can preempt conflicting state law or older federal law.

## The Interplay of U.S. Legal Authorities

The U.S. legal system grew out of the English common law tradition, where law developed case by case through judicial decision. Over time, legislatures began codifying areas of law, which produced the modern dual structure of judge-made and legislatively enacted law.

A few domains, like negligence, defamation, contract formation, and much of property law, remain governed mostly by common law because their principles are flexible and have been refined over centuries of judicial decision-making. Courts have proven better suited than legislatures to the incremental, fact-sensitive elaboration these doctrines need. Other areas, like environmental protection, securities regulation, tax, or immigration, demand comprehensive statutory frameworks and agency oversight, because they require uniformity, technical precision, and the ability to update rules as circumstances change. In practice, most modern legal issues pull in all three at once: statutes set the framework, regulations supply the detail, and case law interprets and applies both.

Because power is split between federal and state governments, a single issue might be governed by federal law, state law, or both, depending on the subject. It might also involve statutes, case law, and regulations at one or both levels at the same time. An employment dispute, for example, might implicate a federal statute (Title VII), federal regulations (EEOC rules), federal case law interpreting both, a parallel state anti-discrimination statute, and a state common law claim like wrongful discharge, all together.

So, as you research, you have to keep all of the authorities that might apply in view. This is one reason secondary sources (Chapter 4) are so useful early on. They can describe the whole landscape of an area and point you to the relevant authorities, which saves you from tracking them all down yourself and protects you from the dangerous assumption that the first source you find is the only one that matters.

These sources also act on each other. Statutes create agencies, which then write regulations. Courts interpret both statutes and regulations, and they fill gaps with common law where no statute applies. Courts can strike down regulations they find invalid, and legislatures can pass new statutes that override a court’s reading of a statute. Courts can also strike down statutes that conflict with the Constitution. The result is a moving system in which authority is constantly being created, interpreted, revised, and occasionally invalidated.

**Examples of complex areas of U.S. law:**

- **Environmental regulation.** Congress passes the Clean Air Act (a statute). The EPA writes emissions standards under that Act (regulations). Courts interpret the statute and the regulations when disputes arise (case law). No single source tells the whole story; the rule that governs a particular smokestack emerges only from reading all three together.
- **Tax law.** In *Rowan Companies, Inc. v. United States*, 452 U.S. 247 (1981), the Supreme Court struck down Treasury regulations that interpreted “wages” inconsistently for different tax purposes. Congress responded by amending the Internal Revenue Code through the Social Security Amendments of 1983 to allow exactly the differentiation the Court had rejected, effectively reversing the decision through new legislation. It is a textbook illustration of the legislature having the last word over a court’s reading of a statute.
- **Constitutional law.** In *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397 (1989), the Supreme Court struck down a law criminalizing flag burning, holding that it was symbolic speech protected by the First Amendment. Here the court had the last word, because the conflict was between a statute and the Constitution rather than between a court and a legislature over a statute’s meaning.

This constant interplay is why you always have to check that your authority is still valid. A statute you plan to cite might have been struck down by a court. A case you want to rely on might have been superseded by a new statute or overruled by a higher court. A regulation might have been invalidated or amended. Verifying all of this is part of competent research, and later chapters treat it as a required step rather than a courtesy.

## The Hierarchy of Legal Authority

Not all primary authority carries the same weight, and two distinctions decide most of it. The interactive below lets you work them out with a map, then test yourself; the short version follows.

**Primary versus secondary.** *Primary authority* is the law itself: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties. Secondary sources explain, interpret, or analyze the law, but they hold no authority to bind government or citizen action. For research, secondary sources are usually where you start and primary authorities are usually where you end.

**Binding versus persuasive.** *Binding* (also called mandatory or controlling) authority is authority a court must follow; *persuasive* authority is authority a court may be influenced by but need not follow. An authority binds your court only when it comes from home: your jurisdiction's own constitution, statutes, and regulations, the decisions of courts above yours in the same system, and the federal law that applies there. Everything else, including other jurisdictions' primary authority, decisions of courts at the same level or below, and all secondary sources, is persuasive.

**Preemption.** Under the Supremacy Clause (Article VI), federal law overrides conflicting state law in areas where Congress has constitutional power to act. Preemption can be express or implied; where federal law sets only a floor, states may be stricter. Whenever you research a state-law question in a federally regulated area, ask whether federal law preempts the state rule first.

### When Persuasive Becomes Binding

Persuasion can also travel further than a single case. The shift from contributory to comparative negligence in tort law shows how persuasive authority can gradually reshape binding law. The change started slowly, was advocated in secondary sources like the Restatement of Torts, was adopted by a few courts, and eventually became the majority rule across the country. What began as merely persuasive became, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, binding.

**In practice: Watching Persuasive Become Binding.** California’s switch is a clean specimen. For decades the state followed contributory negligence, under which a plaintiff even slightly at fault recovered nothing. Nothing binding required a change. What existed was persuasive material: scholarly criticism, other states’ decisions, and comparative rules already in force elsewhere. In Li v. Yellow Cab Co., 13 Cal. 3d 804 (1975), the California Supreme Court drew on exactly that body of non-binding authority and replaced the rule with comparative fault. The moment the opinion issued, the persuasive material had done its work and the new rule was binding on every court in the state. If you had researched this question in 1974, the best answer would have been “contributory negligence controls, but the persuasive weight is running the other way,” and that second clause is the one a client needed.

## Research Implications

A solid grasp of this structure lets you do three things:

- **Decide where to begin.** Which level of law governs this issue? If the area is comprehensively codified, start with the statute. If it is common-law territory, start with cases. If you are not sure, start with a secondary source that will tell you.
- **Sort binding from persuasive sources.** Which authorities must you follow, and which can you cite for support? That judgment sets how much weight to give each source and how hard to keep searching for something better.
- **Track how sources interact.** How do statutes, regulations, and cases shape one another? A complete answer often means assembling several sources rather than finding a single one.

Working this way keeps your arguments grounded in the right authorities, which is most of what people mean when they call a memo well researched. It also feeds directly into the Question Method introduced in Chapter 3: every cycle there begins by asking which authority can answer the question at hand, and this structure is where that answer comes from.

## Common Pitfalls

The mistake that does the most damage early on is treating the first source you find as the whole answer. A single statute or case rarely tells the full story when regulations and judicial interpretation are also in play, and stopping too soon leaves a gap exactly where the governing rule lives. A related error is confusing persuasive authority for binding authority, then building an argument on a case your court has no obligation to follow. Watch the jurisdiction line carefully, because a perfectly good California decision does nothing for you in an Arizona court. And do not skip the validation step. Authority that was good law last year may have been amended, overruled, or struck down since, and citing it as though it still controls is the kind of error that follows you into practice.

## Chapter Recap

U.S. law comes from five primary sources (constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties), layered across one federal system and fifty state systems. Within any jurisdiction, the hierarchy runs constitution over statute, statute over regulation, with case law interpreting all three or, where no statute governs, supplying the rule itself as common law. And for any given court, an authority is either binding, meaning the court must follow it, or persuasive, meaning the court can listen and still say no. This structure is what will inform where you look to answer your research questions.

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- U.S. law comes from five interconnected primary sources: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties.
- Most modern issues involve multiple types of authority at once.
- The hierarchy of authority determines which source controls: constitutions over statutes, statutes over regulations, and so on.
- Mandatory authority is law a court must follow; persuasive authority is law a court may consider but is not required to follow.
- Read the law as interlocking parts of a system that is always evolving, which is why validating every authority is a required step, not an optional one.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-1-1}
### Exercise 1.1 — The Layered-Authority Fact Pattern

A client comes to you with this: she was fired from her job at a private company in your state after a routine drug test detected marijuana. She holds a medical authorization to use it under your state's law, though marijuana remains prohibited by federal statute regardless of state authorization. She used it only at home, never worked impaired, and had no written employment contract. She wants to know whether the firing was legal. Do not answer her question; you cannot yet, and that is the point. Instead, using the chapter's map of the sources of law and its employment-dispute example as your template, identify every layer of authority that might bear on her situation, and for each layer write one sentence predicting what it might contribute to the eventual answer.

**Deliverable:** a table with one row per authority, three columns: the level of government, the type of source (constitution, statute, regulation, case law), and your one-sentence prediction.

**Self-check:** a complete map reaches both levels of government and at least three source types, and every entry should be traceable to something the chapter or the scenario told you, a prediction, not a guess about doctrines you have not met. If every row landed at one level or in one type, reread The Interplay of U.S. Legal Authorities; this fact pattern was built to resist a one-box answer.
:::

### Exercise 1.2 — Binding or Persuasive?

A trial court in Arizona is hearing a negligence claim, an area the chapter told you is governed mostly by common law. The parties have put seven authorities in front of the court. Classify each as binding or persuasive for that court, with one sentence of justification tied to the chapter's rules: (a) a decision of the Arizona Supreme Court on the duty at issue; (b) a decision of the Arizona Court of Appeals on the same duty; (c) a decision of the California Supreme Court on nearly identical facts; (d) a decision of a different Arizona trial court; (e) a decision of a federal district court sitting in Arizona discussing Arizona negligence law; (f) a section of a well-regarded treatise on negligence; (g) a decision of the U.S. Supreme Court interpreting the U.S. Constitution on a point raised in the case. Then one final sentence: for the authorities you classified as persuasive, when does the chapter say a court is most likely to follow them anyway?

**Deliverable:** the seven classifications with justifications, plus the final sentence.

**Self-check:** every justification should invoke a rule the chapter stated, higher court in the same system, home jurisdiction, primary versus secondary, and nothing else. If you found yourself wanting to research Arizona law to answer, stop; everything you need is on the page, and needing more means a classification is leaning on something other than the chapter's rules.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-1-3}
### Exercise 1.3 — Who Gets the Last Word?

The chapter told two stories about authorities acting on each other: *Rowan Companies*, where Congress overrode the Supreme Court's reading of a statute by amending the statute, and *Texas v. Johnson*, where the Court struck a statute against the Constitution and the legislature had no reply. In two or three sentences each, explain who held the last word in each story and why. Then answer the question the two stories together set up, in two sentences, using only the interplay rules the chapter stated: what determines which actor gets the last word, the identity of the actors involved, or the kind of conflict between the authorities?

**Deliverable:** two short explanations and the two-sentence answer.

**Self-check:** your final answer should turn on the kind of conflict, a statute's meaning versus a constitutional limit, not on which institutions happened to be involved. If it turned on the actors, reread the two stories side by side.
