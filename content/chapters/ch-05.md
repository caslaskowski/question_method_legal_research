[Case law]{.gloss} is what courts make when judges write and publish opinions explaining how a legal rule applies to a particular dispute. Those opinions interpret constitutions, statutes, and regulations, and where no statute exists, they create [common law]{.gloss} from the ground up. Engaging with them is not optional; failing to do so can lead to sanctions.

*\[H\]e did not conduct legal research beyond \[an older decision on the issue\], nor did he reread any of the cases cited to or relied on in . . . the most recent First Circuit decision addressing the contours of \[the issue\]. Thus, I conclude that \[his\] erroneous interpretation of the controlling law at the time constituted a breach of the professional duty.*

*ST Engineering Marine, Ltd. v. Thompson, MacColl & Bass, LLC, 633 F. Supp. 3d 354, 361 (D. Me. 2022), aff’d, [\[\_\_ F.4th \_\_\]]{.flag} (1st Cir. Dec. 6, 2023) (No. 22-1844) [\[pull the F.4th cite at the check\]]{.flag}.*

Case law only exists for disputes that have actually reached a court, which has a consequence worth sitting with. If you cannot find a case on point, you do not necessarily lack an argument. You may just need to get more creative: reach for persuasive authority from another jurisdiction, reason by analogy from related cases, or lean on the policy reasoning in secondary sources. The absence of a case directly on point is a research finding in its own right. It is not a dead end.

## Case Law and the Research Cycle

Case law is where your focusing and application questions get answered. Start with finding. Your question decides your entry point. If you are asking a focusing question (“What is the standard for X?”), the fastest route is usually to let a secondary source or an annotated statute hand you the leading case. The editors have already done the work of identifying the case that states the rule, so there is no reason to run a cold keyword search and sift the results yourself. An application question (“Have courts applied this rule to facts like mine?”) works differently. You take that question to a case that already addresses the issue, then follow only the headnotes, key numbers, and citing references that bear on it, until you reach the factually similar decisions you are after. If there are none, you can try performing narrowing searches covered in [Chapter 9](../ch-9/index.html).

The method also changes how you read. You read with your current question as a lens instead of giving the whole opinion equal attention. If your question is about the elements of a claim, you read for the court’s statement of those elements and skim the rest. If your question is about how a standard plays out on particular facts, you read for the **[dispositive facts]{.gloss}** (the facts the decision actually turned on; Appendix B’s How to Read a Case teaches you to spot them) and the court’s application, and you treat the general statement of law as background. Reading a case to answer a specific question is faster and more accurate than reading a case “to understand it,” because the question tells you what to pull out and what to set aside for later.

And the method shapes what a case gives you. One well-chosen opinion usually answers your current question and raises several more at the same time: a sub-rule you had not seen coming, a cited case that looks even more on point, a distinction that matters to your facts. You synthesize the answer to your current question into your annotated outline and log the rest as next questions, with the citations the case handed you noted right beside them. When the current question is answered and that answer documented, determine what your next question is in the current line of legal reasoning. Refer to your notes to either find sources you have already mapped as addressing this question, or to find your next question if you have reached the end of a line of legal reasoning.

## Questions This Source Answers

Case law is your primary tool for **Focusing** questions about how courts have interpreted a specific rule (“What is the current standard for reasonable accommodation under the ADA in the Ninth Circuit?”) and for **Application** questions about how a legal standard plays out on particular facts (“Have courts applied this standard to facts like my client’s?”). You will also reach for case law on **Definitional** questions when a statute’s term has been construed by courts or when the definition is the subject of common law. Case law is usually not your starting point for **Exploratory** questions. Go to secondary sources first to orient yourself before you dive into opinions, or you will drown.

## How Case Law Is Created and What That Means for Research

Most published opinions come from appellate courts reviewing the decisions of lower courts. Federal district courts publish opinions, but those carry only persuasive weight as they are trial-level courts. Understanding the structure of the court that produced an opinion tells you immediately how much weight to give it, which is why the caption is the first piece of research intelligence in any case.

### The Hierarchy of Case Law Authority

Federal courts run in a hierarchy: U.S. Supreme Court, then the Courts of Appeals, then District Courts (the trial level). State systems usually track the same shape: a Supreme Court, one or more intermediate appellate courts, and trial courts.

A case is binding authority only when it comes from a higher court in the same jurisdiction. A case from another jurisdiction is persuasive: it may influence a court, but it need not be followed. An Arizona trial court must follow Arizona Supreme Court decisions. A federal district court in Arizona must follow Ninth Circuit precedent. Both must comply with U.S. Supreme Court constitutional interpretations. An Arizona court may look to California courts for guidance on a question of first impression, but it is not bound by what they decided.

### Published vs. Unpublished Opinions

Not every opinion is published in an official reporter. Courts designate some opinions as unpublished (or “non-precedential”), which usually means they are not binding. Rules about citing unpublished opinions vary a lot by jurisdiction. Some forbid it outright, others allow it with specific formatting or caveats. Before you cite any opinion, check three things: whether it is published or unpublished; what your court’s rules say about citing unpublished opinions; and whether the opinion has any subsequent negative treatment. The fact that a database *contains* an opinion does not make it *citable* in your jurisdiction.

## Searching for Case Law

Secondary sources and annotated statutes are often your best starting points, because they cite leading cases directly and spare you a cold search. Once you have a citation, it becomes the entry point for the question-driven cycle below. Without one, browse by subject using key numbers (Westlaw) or topics (Lexis), or keyword search in Westlaw, Lexis, Bloomberg Law, or Google Scholar; keyword searching earns a larger role for cases than for statutes, because opinions are written in ordinary prose, but a curated finding aid still beats a guessed term. Whichever route you take, the question you are trying to answer dictates the tool, not the other way around. You reach for the retrieval method that best answers the specific question in front of you.

## Answering Questions with Case Law

### Running the Cycle Through a Case: Question, Read, Synthesize, Repeat

Case law research is the same cycle you run everywhere else in this book, performed one source at a time. Here is the middle of the cycle as a five-move protocol, formatted like Chapter 3’s Five Steps:

**Move 1: Bring the question to the case.** Locate a case that clearly addresses your question, through a secondary source citation, an annotated code annotation, a colleague’s reference, or a keyword search, and keep the question, not the case, in the foreground.

**Move 2: Read for the answer.** Read the case critically, with your current question as the lens.

**Move 3: Synthesize.** Record the answer the case gives to your question in your annotated outline, in your own words.

**Move 4: Log the collateral questions.** As you read, note the other questions the case answers; you do not have to synthesize them now. Next to each, record the case’s citation and the cases it cites for that point. Those become your research pathways for later questions.

**Move 5: Choose the next question.** Is it one this case addresses, or one from your secondary source research? The headnotes, key numbers, and citator tools below will connect you to related cases on the same specific point. Research it, and keep the cycle going until every question is answered.

The hardest application questions are the ones no decision squarely answers, and they come in two forms worth telling apart. Sometimes the rule is articulated and what is missing is a decision applying it to a relationship or situation like yours; the work is then analogical, synthesizing the decided applications and arguing which side of the line your facts belong on. And where the rule states its own exception, the argument runs through the rule’s rationale: what is this line for, and does your relationship sit inside it? The condensed worked example later in this chapter, the foster mother as bystander, models that move, and its full chain appears in Appendix G. Other times no articulated rule governs your question at all, and the rule itself has to be built from other sources; that is constructive application.

::: {.pullquote}
**The foster mother and the “closely related” element.** Whether a long-term foster mother can sue as a bystander for negligent infliction of emotional distress comes down to one contested question: is a long-term foster parent “closely related” to her foster child under California law? Thing v. La Chusa (1989) 48 Cal.3d 644 supplies the rule: absent “exceptional circumstances,” the qualifying relationships are those of blood or marriage. No California court has addressed foster parenthood at all, so the argument must be made that the foster relationship warrants the exception. In researching, it is discovered that courts have rejected the in-law relationship, *M**oon v. Guardian Postacute Services* (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 1005. Is a foster parent more like a parent, or more like a mother-in-law? The answer is genuinely open, and saying so, with the strongest argument for each side, is the research finding. (The full chain, question by question, appears in Appendix G.)
:::

### Editorial Enhancements in Case Law Databases

The commercial databases wrap every opinion in an editorial apparatus, and each layer answers a different research question. Learn what each layer is for, and, just as important, what authority it lacks.

#### Headnotes

**[Headnotes]{.gloss}** are brief, editor-written summaries of specific legal points in a case. Each one corresponds to a portion of the opinion. Use them to find the relevant sections quickly and to find other cases on the same point, but never cite a headnote. Only the underlying case text carries precedential authority. A headnote is an editor’s summary; it may not perfectly capture the court’s actual holding, and editors occasionally get it wrong. These can help you navigate to a relevant part of the case and also to navigate to other cases that address the legal point.

#### Key Number System (Westlaw) and Topic Headings (Lexis)

Westlaw’s Key Number System organizes American law into more than 400 major topics and thousands of subtopics, each with its own key number. Once you find a relevant key number, say “Torts, Negligence, Duty, Foreseeability,” you can pull every case classified under that issue, searching by legal concept rather than by keyword. Lexis offers LexisNexis Topics, which work much the same way. These tools are invaluable for pursuing your next question, because they connect the case you have to the whole universe of cases on the same specific point.

#### Citators: KeyCite and Shepard’s

**[Citators]{.gloss}** track a case’s history and allow you to explore cases connected to the one you are currently reading, both the ones it cites and the ones that cite it. You can use these systems to search through this web for other cases that address the current question in your research cycle by searching within results on the database’s filter sidebar.

These tools show whether the points of law within the case remain valid (colloquially, whether it is “still good law”), how each point has been handled (followed, distinguished, overruled, criticized), and a list of citing references. Learn each system’s color-coded signals: generally red for overruled or serious negative treatment, yellow for caution. A signal is a reminder to read the citing opinions, not a verdict. A case with a yellow signal may have been distinguished on facts nothing like yours, in which case it remains perfectly good authority for your situation. You will only know by reading the citing opinion.

Notice what the citator is actually tracking: points of law, not the case as a whole. Subsequent treatment attaches to specific propositions, which is why both systems tie their analysis to particular headnotes. A case overruled on one point can remain solid authority on another. *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), is the canonical example. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), repudiated its separate-but-equal doctrine, yet courts went on citing *Plessy* for unrelated procedural points for decades afterward. So a red flag starts an inquiry rather than ending one: which point of law drew the treatment, and is it the point you rely on? The citing decisions hold the answer.

::: {.try kind="choice" title="Read the flag, then read the case"}
A case you want carries a red flag. The citator shows it was overruled in part: a later decision rejected its holding on punitive damages. You want to cite it for its separate holding on when the limitations period begins to run. What now?

- [ ] Drop the case. A red flag means it is no longer good law.
    - The flag attaches to the case, but treatment attaches to points of law. You may be discarding the best authority you have.
- [ ] Cite it. The flag concerns a different issue.
    - Probably true, and not yet verified. You are relying on a one-line citator summary of what the later court did.
- [x] Read the overruling decision, confirm it left the limitations holding untouched, then cite the case and note the partial overruling.
    - The citator told you where to look. Reading the later opinion tells you what happened. The citation form itself should then disclose the negative history.
:::

## Identifying Relevant Materials and Synthesizing Into Your Writing

Use headnotes to navigate to the relevant part of a long opinion. Pull the rule out precisely, and quote the court’s articulation of the standard where the exact wording matters. Then synthesize across cases: identify the consistent rule, note the exceptions, and flag any conflicting authority. Record your case law findings in your annotated outline under the relevant question heading, with citation, key holding, dispositive facts, and procedural posture noted. Always note the jurisdiction. A Ninth Circuit case carries different weight in a Ninth Circuit matter than the same case would in a Second Circuit matter.

## Common Pitfalls

Case law research has a few traps that catch nearly everyone at some point. The first is treating a headnote as if it were the law. A headnote is an editor’s summary written to help you navigate, and it carries no authority of its own; the opinion text is what you read and what you cite. Just as common is relying on a case without running it through a citator and then learning too late that it was overruled or heavily criticized. A subtler trap is the case that sits squarely in your subject area but actually answers a different question than the one you are holding, which is easy to seize on in relief and hard to dislodge once you have built around it. Quieter still is citing dicta as if it were holding: a court’s aside on a question it was not deciding can read exactly like a rule, but it decided nothing, and opposing counsel will know the difference. Beyond the Basics, below, walks the holding-dictum line. And it is worth remembering that finding an unpublished opinion in your database does not make it citable. Whether you can rely on it depends on your jurisdiction’s rules, so check them before you build on it.

## Beyond the Basics

### Cross-Jurisdictional Persuasive Authority

When your jurisdiction has little or no case law on an issue, cases from elsewhere can provide persuasive support, especially cases from the same regional circuit or from states with similar statutory frameworks. Look for the jurisdictions whose law most closely resembles yours. A case from a jurisdiction that has adopted the same statutory scheme or the same Restatement section carries more persuasive weight than a case from a jurisdiction built on different foundations.

::: {.pullquote}
**Borrowed statutes and the neighbor’s answer.** A Nevada client wants anti-SLAPP protection for posts made in a small, closed professional listserv, and neither Nevada’s statute (NRS 41.635–41.670) nor any Nevada appellate decision says whether that setting is a protected public forum. The chain that resolves it is constructive application in its clean form. With the home jurisdiction silent, the research surfaces that Nevada modeled its statute on California’s, pegged part of it to California law by amendment, and that the Nevada Supreme Court reads the parallel provisions the way California does, while California has a developed body of forum decisions reaching closed and limited-audience settings. The predicted rule is then built from those sources, the borrowing relationship, the prior-construction canon, and California’s construction, checked against the points where the statutes diverge, and stated as a prediction with its confidence qualified, because a first-impression answer cannot honestly be stated any other way. (The full chain, question by question, appears in Appendix G.)
:::

### Opinions, Concurrences, and Dissents, Oh My!

A single decision can contain more than one opinion, and knowing which is which tells you how much weight each one carries. The **[majority opinion]{.gloss}** is the one a majority of the participating judges joined, and it is the law of the case. Sometimes no single opinion wins a majority and you get a **[plurality opinion]{.gloss}**, the one with the most votes short of a majority. Pluralities bind only as to their narrowest common ground, so read them carefully before you rely on one.

A **[concurrence]{.gloss}** is written by a judge who agrees with the result but gets there by different reasoning, or who wants to add something. Concurrences are not binding, but they can help identify persuasive arguments for future cases.

A **[dissent]{.gloss}** is written by a judge who disagrees with the result. Dissents are not binding either, but they can be powerful. When a dissent’s reasoning later becomes the majority view, as happened with comparative negligence (Chapter 1 covers the same shift from the persuasive-authority side) and with the evolution of Fourth Amendment digital privacy doctrine, spotting that trajectory early lets you argue for where the law is heading rather than only where it has been.

::: {.pullquote}
**A dissent that became the law.** In Olmstead v. United States, 277 U.S. 438 (1928), the Supreme Court held that wiretapping a phone line was not a Fourth Amendment search, because federal agents had trespassed on nothing and seized no tangible thing. Justice Brandeis dissented, arguing that the Amendment reaches invasions of privacy, not merely intrusions onto property, and that a rule tied to physical trespass would not survive changes in technology. For nearly forty years that reasoning was persuasive authority and nothing more. Then, in Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), the Court overruled Olmstead on this point, holding that the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places, and adopting the dissent’s premise. A researcher tracking the trajectory in 1965 would have found the argument that eventually won sitting in a dissent, marked as losing.
:::

### Holding vs. Dictum

This distinction does more work in legal argument than almost any other, so it is worth getting right. The **holding** is the court’s resolution of the specific legal question raised by the specific facts in front of it. **Dictum** (plural **dicta**) is everything else the court says along the way: observations, hypotheticals, asides about issues that were not actually presented. Dictum can be persuasive, and dictum from an important court can be very persuasive, but it does not bind anyone.

The test is whether the statement was necessary to resolve the dispute before the court. If the court had to decide it to reach its result, it is holding. If the court could have left it out and reached the same result, it is dictum. Mistaking one for the other is a serious error, because you can end up building an argument on language that no court is actually obligated to follow. When you read a case through the lens of your research question, ask of each useful-sounding sentence: did the court need this to decide the case? Your answer tells you how much you can lean on it.

::: {.try kind="sort" title="Holding or dictum?" bins="Holding | Dictum"}
A tenant's guest fell when a stair rail gave way in a common hallway. The landlord had been told about the rail a month earlier. The appellate court affirmed a judgment for the guest. Sort these statements from the opinion.

- “A landlord with notice of a dangerous condition in a common area owes a duty of reasonable care to a tenant's guests.” **Holding**
    - The court had to decide this to resolve the appeal.
- “Had the plaintiff been a trespasser, a different analysis might apply.” **Dictum**
    - No trespasser was before the court. A statement about facts the case did not present decides nothing.
- “Because the landlord had actual notice of the broken rail and a month to repair it, the judgment is affirmed.” **Holding**
    - The rule applied to the dispositive facts: this is the decision itself.
- “We observe that the legislature has not revisited the landlord-tenant code in forty years.” **Dictum**
    - An aside. The result would be identical without it.
- “The same duty would presumably extend to commercial leases.” **Dictum**
    - This was a residential lease. The word “presumably” is the court telling you it is not deciding the point.
:::

## Chapter Recap

Case law is the backbone of U.S. common law and the place where statutes and regulations get their judicial interpretation. Researching it well means reading cases critically with your research question as the lens, using editorial enhancements to guide you to the answers to your next questions, following citation trails backward and forward, and confirming that the points of law you take from each case remain valid. Dispositive fact identification, knowing which facts actually drove the court’s decision, is the key skill for predicting how a case will apply to new facts. The how-to lives in [Appendix B’s How to Read a Case](../appendices/b.html).

::: {.takeaways}
[Key Takeaways]{.box-title}

- Case law interprets constitutions, statutes, and regulations, and creates common law where no legislation exists.
- Only cases from higher courts within the same jurisdiction are binding; other cases are persuasive.
- Run the cycle one source at a time: bring a specific question to a case, read it critically to answer that question, synthesize the answer, log the next questions the case reveals, and use editorial tools to pursue whichever question comes next. The question, not the case, drives the path.
- Headnotes are research tools, not authority. Only the opinion text carries precedential weight.
- Dispositive fact identification is the key skill for predicting how a case applies to new facts.
- Always update cases using KeyCite or Shepard’s before you rely on them, and read far enough to learn which points of law any negative treatment reaches.
:::
