Legal scholarship asks something different of your research. In practice, you find the controlling law and apply it to a client’s facts. In scholarship, you are trying to add an original argument to an ongoing conversation. That requires thorough doctrinal research and a survey of the scholarly landscape: who has written on this topic, what arguments have been made, and where genuine controversy or underdevelopment exists. A note on this chapter’s shape: scholarship is a context, not a source, so this chapter deliberately drops the source-chapter template you have followed since Chapter 4. No Questions This Source Answers section, no source anatomy. That is on purpose. You will most likely return to this chapter when you write a seminar paper, a journal note or comment, or a moot court brief. Those are the occasions it is for.

::: {.objectives}
[Learning Objectives]{.box-title}

- Distinguish legal scholarship research from practice-oriented research in goals, sources, and methodology
- Recognize the many kinds of questions a scholarly paper can answer, and turn any of them into a research question
- Generate and narrow a viable paper topic
- Map the scholarly landscape on a legal topic: positions staked out, points of agreement and disagreement, and genuine gaps
- Run preemption checks at the right moments, from the first cursory search to the final pre-submission sweep
- Locate relevant interdisciplinary scholarship that bears on a legal question
- Use the Question Method to research and draft the paper section by section, one question at a time

## Finding a Topic

Where does a paper come from? Students tend to imagine there is one kind of scholarly paper, usually some version of “find a question nobody has answered.” The truth is roomier. A paper can explore an unsettled question, where the law genuinely has not decided and your paper works out what the answer is or should be. Does training an AI model on licensed stock photography exhaust the license? Nobody knows yet. A paper can wade into a live controversy, where courts or scholars are actively fighting, and pick a side, broker a peace, or show that everyone has been asking the wrong question. A paper can argue that a working system needs fixing. How might we fix the DMCA’s notice-and-takedown process to protect creator rights without stifling creation in the new social-media landscape? Notice what that question is doing. Nothing about the DMCA is unsettled; takedown notices go out every day. The paper’s claim is that the machine runs exactly as designed and the design is hurting people it was never built to see. And these are only the common shapes. A paper can synthesize a scattered body of law that nobody has pulled together, respond to a single important new opinion, predict where a doctrine is heading, or carry another field’s insight into legal territory. What every version shares is the ending: the topic resolves into a question, and your thesis is the answer you will defend.

If you have been running the method, you already hold a supply of candidates. Every project leaves questions behind: the issue an opinion expressly reserved (“we need not decide today whether…”), the open question an article names in its final pages, the point two authors dispute without ever quite engaging, the framework a practitioner grumbles about because it makes her clients’ lives harder. In practice research you set those aside, because the client needs an answer, not an agenda. In scholarship they are the agenda. Get in the habit of writing them down as you read. Call this your question inventory, and a well-fed inventory means you may never face the blank page asking “what should I write about?” at all.

If the inventory is thin, you can hunt. Circuit splits are the classic starting point, because a split is a controversy with the proof already assembled: the Congressional Research Service publishes a regular roundup of new splits, several blogs track them weekly, and a terms-and-connectors search like circuit /s split, filtered to the last two years, surfaces them on demand. New technology straining an old statute is another dependable well, and so is its mirror image, a new statute colliding with settled practice. Fifty-state surveys and ALR annotations show you where the law is in disarray. For the fix-the-system paper, look where the complaints pile up: chronic litigation over the same provision, agencies issuing guidance to patch a statute that no longer fits, compliance regimes everyone works around instead of through. And talk to people. Professors, former supervisors, and practitioners all carry questions they wish someone would answer. Come with the areas you are considering; leave with their sense of which problems actually hurt.

Before you commit to a candidate, ask four things of it. Is it supportable? A literature has to exist for you to stand on and push against. Is it open or fixable? You need a question that has not been answered, or a system whose flaws have not been honestly reckoned with. Is it worth answering? Someone (a court, a legislature, a practice community, the next scholar) should be better off once your question is resolved. And can you live with it? You will spend a semester or more in this question’s company, so pick one you actually want to think about. Be realistic about scope while you test. The articles you admire in the journals are often the product of years, and your seminar paper cannot cover the same ground. It does not need to. A narrow question answered thoroughly is a contribution; a broad one gestured at is a literature summary with ambitions.

In method terms, choosing a topic is choosing your project’s first research question, and the narrowing you have practiced all book long (area to question to claim) is the same move at a larger scale. You are not alone at this stage, either. Most law libraries maintain research guides built for exactly this moment, collecting topic-finding strategies, preemption checklists, and advice on the shape of a scholarly article. Start there, and ask a law librarian what else the library holds for note writers; that conversation is usually the cheapest hour of the whole project.

## Mapping the Scholarly Landscape

The Question Method changes shape slightly in scholarship, and the change is worth naming before you start searching. In practice, your questions come from the client’s problem, and you work one at a time until each closes. In scholarship you run two tracks at once. The first is the question you are working right now, and early in a project it is usually some version of “has anyone already made my argument?” The second is the collecting habit from the last section: as you read, you keep harvesting the questions the field is asking into your inventory. The two tracks feed each other. Today’s working question sends you into the literature, the literature restocks the inventory, and the inventory supplies next semester’s paper.

Run a cursory preemption check before anything else. The temptation at the start of a project is the grand tour: every database, every article, a mountain of PDFs. Resist it, because there is a cheaper question to answer first. Does your argument already exist? Spend an hour, not a week: search the distinctive terms of your claim in HeinOnline, Westlaw or Lexis, SSRN, and Google Scholar, and read the abstracts of the closest matches. If someone has written your paper, you want to know before the outline exists, not after. If the claim survives the hour, proceed to the full sweep; the formal preemption protocol later in this section digs deeper, and it will run again.

Searching for articles and working papers. HeinOnline is the most comprehensive full-text database, with strong historical coverage. Westlaw and Lexis offer sophisticated search tools. SSRN is free and carries cutting-edge working papers, often months or years ahead of publication. Google Scholar is free and useful for citation counts and finding key articles. And do not overlook bepress’s Digital Commons network and institutional repositories, where new scholarship often lands first, free to anyone. One more habit, and it matters: do not stop at the law reviews. Depending on your issue, the conversation you are joining may be running partly in another field’s journals (economics, political science, psychology, public health, history), and a landscape map that omits them is incomplete. The interdisciplinary section below tells you where those literatures live.

**Organizing the literature.** Organize by doctrinal position (the dominant view, the competing minority, emerging positions); by theoretical approach (law and economics, critical theory, originalism, purposivism, empirical legal studies); and by chronology (foundational articles, significant responses, the most recent contributions).

**Conducting preemption research.** Preemption in scholarship means checking whether your argument has already been made. The cursory check was your first pass. This one is deeper, and it recurs, because a preempting article can land on SSRN while you are writing. A contribution that has been fully preempted is not a contribution, and discovering that early, rather than after a semester of writing, is one of the highest-value research tasks in scholarship.

The protocol, formatted like the book’s others:

**Step 1: Search the claims.** Look for articles asserting your thesis or something near it: keyword searches on the claim’s distinctive terms, symposium issues on your topic, and student notes, which cluster around exactly the questions a note-writer would pick.

**Step 2: Read the closest matches for actual overlap.** An article in the same area is not preemption; an article making your argument is. Read for the thesis, not the topic.

**Step 3: Differentiate or pivot.** If the argument exists, name precisely what you add (new doctrine, new data, new mechanism, new implication) or choose a different focus. State, in one sentence, what your piece says that the existing piece does not. If you cannot write that sentence, pivot.

## Writing the Paper One Question at a Time

A scholarly paper looks like one long argument, and that is what makes it intimidating: forty pages, hundreds of footnotes, a thesis that has to hold from the first line to the last. Here is the reassuring secret. Underneath, most papers answer a short, recognizable sequence of questions. What is the current legal framework? What problem has emerged that the framework must face? Why does the framework fail to handle it? What should be done instead? And, running beneath all of it, what will the strongest objection be? Open the table of contents of almost any article you admire and you will find some version of that sequence: background, problem, critique, proposal. Each section exists to answer one big question, and each big question breaks down into the same kind of research questions you have been drafting since Chapter 3.

The question taxonomy travels with you, and scholarship stacks one more level on top of it. A scholarly project usually opens with a doctrinal question, the familiar kind: What does the law currently require on X? It then moves to a scholarly question: Is this rule defensible, and who has argued for or against it? And it matures into a normative question: What should the law be, and why? The destination differs from practice, because the goal is an original, well-supported argument rather than the controlling rule for a client. The engine, though, never changes. Each answer becomes a section of your annotated outline, and each gap you find in the existing literature becomes a potential contribution. In the Introduction’s vocabulary, the scholarly and normative questions are the macro questions of scholarship, and your thesis is the answer the paper exists to defend. The research questions are still the steps.

That means you research a paper the way you research everything else: one question at a time. An exploratory survey of the whole issue is right at the start; that is how you found the topic and mapped the landscape. But once the outline exists and drafting begins, resist the urge to hold the entire argument in your head at once. Nobody can digest every part of a forty-page argument simultaneously, and the method does not ask you to. When you sit down to work on the framework section, run cycles on the framework: what does the statute actually require, how have courts read it, where are its edges? When you reach the problem section, the framework questions are closed and the problem gets your full attention. Each section is a small research project with its own chain of questions, its own Research Log entries, and its own closure.

Your annotated outline is where this becomes concrete. Draft the outline as questions rather than topics: not “III. The DMCA framework” but “III. What does the DMCA’s takedown process actually require?” The difference matters, because a question can be answered, and answered means done. As each section closes, the outline fills with answers in your own words, anchored to authority in your Research Log, and the paper begins assembling itself from parts you have already verified. Drafting becomes what it should be: writing up answers you already hold, and noticing the gaps that send you back for one more cycle.

## Using Interdisciplinary Sources

Legal questions often benefit from insights in other disciplines, and knowing where to look for them is its own research skill. Some examples are included below, but this is not an exhaustive list.

- Contract interpretation: cognitive linguistics and behavioral economics (JSTOR, SSRN, NBER working papers).
- Constitutional design: comparative political science (JSTOR political science journals, comparative politics databases).
- Criminal sentencing: criminology, psychology, sociology (SSRN, Sociological Abstracts, PsycINFO).
- Administrative regulation: public administration and organizational theory (JSTOR, Public Administration Review).
- First Amendment: philosophy of speech and sociology of media (PhilPapers, communication journals).

Useful search tools include JSTOR (humanities and social sciences), PhilPapers (philosophy), PsycINFO (psychology), SSRN (economics, finance, social science), NBER (economics working papers), and Google Scholar (broad interdisciplinary coverage). Consider making an appointment with a law librarian to learn what relevant databases you might have access to.

## Common Pitfalls

The pitfall that wastes the most time in scholarship is skipping preemption research and discovering, a semester in, that your “original” argument was published three years ago; running that first cursory check before anything else is what protects the whole project. A second is choosing a subject area instead of a question and calling it a topic: a subject area is a shelf to browse, not a paper to write. A third is mistaking a literature summary for a contribution, gathering what everyone has said without staking out what you will add. A fourth is reaching for interdisciplinary sources as decoration rather than support, citing an economics paper you have not actually integrated into the argument. If your paper argues that a system needs fixing, remember that a complaint is not a proposal: the paper needs the repair, not just the diagnosis. And be careful with working papers: SSRN puts you at the front of the conversation, but a paper that has not been peer-reviewed may change before publication, so rely on it with that caveat in mind.

## Beyond the Basics

### Citing Secondary Sources in Scholarly Writing

In law review articles, citing treatises, other articles, and Restatements is standard and often essential for establishing scholarly context. The goal is to join and advance the conversation. Unlike court filings, where secondary source citations are rare, scholarship depends on engaging the full literature.

### SSRN and Working Papers

SSRN working papers represent the cutting edge, often months or years ahead of publication. Engaging with them ensures your scholarship responds to the most current conversation. When you rely on a working paper, note that it has not been peer-reviewed and its arguments may change before final publication.

## Chapter Recap

Scholarship gives the method a wider field and a longer clock. A paper can explore an unsettled question, join a controversy, argue that a system needs fixing, or take another shape entirely; whatever the shape, the topic resolves into a question your thesis will answer. Check cheaply and early whether the argument already exists, then map the landscape, reaching past the law reviews whenever the issue does, and run the full preemption protocol when the thesis firms up and again before you submit. When the writing starts, the paper itself becomes a chain of questions (what is the framework, what is the problem, why does the framework fail, what is the fix), and you research each section’s question the way you have researched everything in this book: one at a time, to closure. And keep the question inventory restocked as you read. The working question finishes this paper. The inventory starts the next.

## The Scholarship Research Checklist

This chapter has no source-by-source drills, because scholarship is a context, not a source. What it has instead is a working method, and the checklist below puts that method in the order you will need it. Run it when the seminar paper, note, or comment actually arrives. (Adapted from the interactive companion's Chapter 17 page.)

1. **Run a cursory preemption check before anything else.** An hour, not a week. Search your claim's distinctive terms in HeinOnline, Westlaw or Lexis, SSRN, and Google Scholar, and read the closest abstracts. If someone has written your paper, know it before the outline exists.
2. **Choose a question, not a subject area.** A subject area is a shelf to browse, not a paper to write. The topic resolves into the question your thesis will answer.
3. **Map the scholarly landscape.** Note the positions staked out, the points of agreement and disagreement, and the genuine gaps. Read the closest matches for actual overlap: an article in the same area is not preemption; an article making your argument is.
4. **Run the full preemption protocol when the thesis firms up.** If the argument exists, differentiate your contribution or refocus. A fully preempted contribution is not a contribution.
5. **Write the contribution sentence.** Say what you add beyond a summary of what everyone else has said. A literature summary is not a contribution.
6. **Research and draft one question at a time.** Framework, problem, why the framework fails, the fix: take each section's question to closure.
7. **Reach past the law reviews when the issue does,** but integrate interdisciplinary sources as support, not decoration. If your paper argues the system needs fixing, the paper needs the repair, not just the diagnosis.
8. **Mind working papers.** SSRN puts you at the front of the conversation, but an un-peer-reviewed paper can change before publication. Rely on it with that caveat.
9. **Run the final preemption sweep before submission.** A preempting article can land while you are writing.
10. **Restock the question inventory as you read.** The working question finishes this paper. The inventory starts the next.

## Reviewing Through Practice

The following exercises, together called **Map the Conversation**, test topic generation, preemption checking, and question-form outlining. They begin an actual paper rather than simulate one.

::: {.exercise #ex-17-1}
### Exercise 17.1 — Seed the Inventory

Go back through the sources you read for any three drills in this book, the cases, the secondary sources, the guidance documents (any three substantial legal sources you have read this term work as well), and harvest the questions they left open: the issue an opinion expressly reserved, the point flagged as beyond an article's scope, the term two authorities read differently without ever engaging each other.

**Deliverable:** a question inventory of at least five entries, each logged with where you found it.

**Self-check:** every entry should be a question someone else's text left open, not a topic you find interesting. "AI and copyright" is a shelf. "The court expressly declined to decide whether X, so what is the answer?" is an inventory entry.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-17-2}
### Exercise 17.2 — The One-Hour Check

Take a candidate paper question, the strongest entry in your question inventory or any thesis you are considering, and spend exactly one hour on the cursory preemption check: search the claim's distinctive terms in HeinOnline, Westlaw or Lexis, SSRN, and Google Scholar, and read the abstracts of the closest matches.

**Deliverable:** a Research Log record of the searches you ran, the nearest neighbors you found, and the verdict: survives, needs differentiating, or preempted.

**Self-check:** the hour is a real constraint; honor it, because the check's whole value is being cheap. And if the verdict is preempted, celebrate briefly. The check just saved you a semester, and your inventory holds four more candidates.
:::

::: {.exercise #ex-17-3}
### Exercise 17.3 — The Question-Form Outline

For a candidate claim that has survived a preemption check, or any working thesis, draft a four-line mini-outline in question form, framework, problem, critique, proposal, each heading written as a question a section could answer. Add one line naming the non-law literature the paper would need and the database where that literature lives.

**Deliverable:** the five lines.

**Self-check:** each heading must be answerable, because answerable means a section can be finished, and finished sections are how forty pages get written by someone who can only hold one question at a time, which is everyone. A subject area is a shelf to browse. A question is a paper you can start, and after this exercise you have started one.
:::
