Part I: Foundational Legal Research Topics

Chapter 6 Statutes Research

1 interactive Statutes Practice

Case law often explains how judges interpret the law, but statutes are where many legal rules begin. Most areas of modern law, including criminal law, taxation, environmental regulation, employment, and civil rights, are governed by statutes a legislature enacted. Locating, interpreting, and using statutes is its own skill, different from reading cases, because a statute states a rule without explaining itself. One habit will carry you through everything that follows: when you hit an ambiguity, do not groan. Write it down as your next question.

Key Takeaways

  • Describe how statutes are enacted and organized at the federal and state levels
  • Locate statutes using annotated codes in print and electronic databases
  • Read and interpret statutory text, including definitions, cross-references, and exceptions
  • Apply basic canons of statutory construction to resolve ambiguous language
  • Use annotated code features (case notes, cross-references, historical notes) to research a statutory question
  • Update statutes to confirm they remain current and valid law
  • Integrate statutory research into the Question Method framework

Statutes and the Research Cycle #

Statutes are where focusing questions about codified law get answered, and the Question Method shapes both how you reach the statute and how you read it once you arrive.

It shapes how you reach the statute because the first fork comes before any searching at all: if you already have a citation, from a secondary source, a case, or the assignment itself, you retrieve rather than search, exactly as Chapter 2’s known-source rule says. When you do not have one, the question type tells you the fastest route to a citation. If your question is “what law governs this regulated activity?”, the quickest path is rarely a keyword search of the code; it is a secondary source or the annotated code’s index naming the controlling section and handing you the citation. Either way, naming the question keeps you out of the frustrating experience of keyword-searching a code and drowning in scattered, decontextualized sections.

It shapes how you read the statute because a statute almost never answers your question in one self-contained sentence. Reading a statute to answer a focusing question means reading the operative provision, the definitions section that controls its terms, the exceptions that might remove your client’s conduct from its reach, and the cross-referenced sections it incorporates. The method’s habit of asking “did this fully answer my question?” is what stops you from relying on a subsection you read in isolation, which is the single most common statutory research error.

The occasions when a statute does answer your application question are those where legislatures draw bright lines, and a bright line performs the application step on its own. Three forms show up constantly. Numeric thresholds: was the complaint filed within the limitations period? Enumerated lists and schedules: is this substance on the controlled-substances schedule? Deeming provisions and safe harbors: a statute voiding any covenant longer than a stated term has already applied itself to every longer covenant. Each of these closes a genuine application question on the statutory text, without a case. Call these self-applying rules, and read the form of a provision early, because its form tells you where the answer lives.

A bright line closes your question only when every operative term is defined, and your facts map onto it without inference, and that condition fails more often than you would expect. A limitations period is a precise number, but whether the clock started is usually a case-law question. A coverage threshold of fifteen employees is precise, but whether a particular worker counts as an “employee” is not. So a self-applying rule does one of two things: it closes the question, or it hands you a candidate answer plus a fresh definitional question, which is the same two-level move that runs from statute to regulation in the organic thread below. Chapter 3’s tariff case shows the failure mode. The Act named its categories and defined neither, so the statute could not close anything, and the answer had to come from a court. Had the schedule listed tomatoes by name, the research would have ended at the statute. Same facts, same question type, a different source, because the drafting changed.

Most of all, statutes produce new questions. Every undefined term, every vague standard, every exception, every cross-reference is a potential next cycle. A statute that uses the word “employer” generates the question “how does this statute define employer?” A statute that says “reasonable accommodation” generates “what have courts held constitutes reasonable accommodation?” The method captures these as discrete entries in your Research Log, so the ambiguities you spot become an organized research agenda rather than a vague sense that the statute is complicated.

Questions This Source Answers #

Statutes are the right starting point when your question asks what the law requires, prohibits, or permits in a legislatively regulated area. If you need the elements of a federal employment discrimination claim or the procedures for filing a patent application, you begin with the statute. Focusing questions (“What does Title VII require of an employer who receives a complaint of religious discrimination?”) are the most common type you will answer with statutes. Definitional questions come up constantly too (“The statute uses the term employer, so how does it define that term?”). Each annotated-code feature generates potential sub-questions that become entries in your Research Log.

How Statutes Are Made and What That Means for Research #

A statute begins as a bill introduced in a legislative chamber. It is assigned to a committee, marked up, reported for floor consideration, passed by both chambers in identical form (sometimes requiring a conference committee to reconcile differing versions), and signed by the President or Governor. Every stage produces documents that may help you understand what the statute means; these legislative history materials are covered in Chapter 10. The two relevant artifacts that are created in the lawmaking process are session laws and codes.

Session Laws vs. Codes #

Session laws are statutes published chronologically as enacted, like the United States Statutes at Large. Codes reorganize session laws by subject into a unified collection, like the United States Code. Annotated codes, such as the United States Code Annotated (U.S.C.A.) published by Westlaw and the United States Code Services (U.S.C.S. ) published by Lexis, are the most useful research tools, because they pair the statute text with editorial enhancements such as case annotations, cross-references, and research aids, turning bare text into a hub that connects to the rest of the legal landscape.

Searching for Statutes #

Start from a citation, drawn from a secondary source or a case, and look it up directly in an annotated code. Without a citation, work through the code’s own finding aids before you reach for a search box. The Popular Name Table locates statutes by their common names (“Clean Air Act,” “Americans with Disabilities Act”) and is often the fastest route when you know what a law is called but not where it is codified. The subject index and table of contents, in print or online codes, sort the code by topic and reward the vocabulary your secondary sources handed you. Keyword searching within statutory databases comes last, not second: statutory drafting is technical and rarely matches the words a searcher would guess, so the search box misses provisions the finding aids surface easily. Save it for when the finding aids fail or when you are hunting a phrase you know the statute uses.

Answering Questions with Statutes: Using Annotated Codes #

An annotated code is the statute plus a research apparatus. Beneath the official text, editors stack everything the provision has touched: the cases construing it, the statutes and rules it connects to, its amendment history, the secondary sources discussing it, and citator links. That is why the annotated code, not the bare statutory text, is where statutory research happens; one page holds the law and every pathway out of it. Each annotation type answers a different kind of question, so read what follows as a menu keyed to the question you are holding.

  • Case Notes / Notes of Decisions. Brief summaries of cases interpreting the statute. Read several to spot leading cases or conflicting interpretations. Each is a focusing question a court has already answered about the provision: “how did the court read this element in this context?”
  • Cross-References. Links to related statutes, regulations, or court rules. Each one is a potential research pathway and a potential next question.
  • Historical Notes and Amendments. The legislative evolution of the statute. Critical for figuring out which version was in effect when the relevant events occurred.
  • Research References. Pointers to secondary sources discussing the statute, a shortcut back to treatises and law review articles that may supply the interpretive context you need.
  • Citator Links. Subsequent amendments, cases interpreting the statute, and pending legislative changes. Always run a citator check on any statute you plan to rely on.

A brief worked thread shows the annotations in motion. Suppose your question is the definitional one this book has carried since Chapter 3: What is the federal definition of “organic”? The annotated code’s index points you to the Organic Foods Production Act. The next move is the one experts stop noticing: pull up the code’s table of contents for the Act, chapter 94 of Title 7, and scan its structure. Near the front, where definitions almost always sit, is a section titled “Definitions,” 7 U.S.C. § 6502, and it supplies the program’s defined terms. Reading the operative provisions in context, you find the statute directs the Secretary of Agriculture to establish the certification program, which hands definitional authority to the agency. That delegation is your next question: what does the USDA’s regulation require? The annotations’ implementing-regulations note points you to 7 C.F.R. Part 205, and the cycle crosses into Chapter 7. One statute, read in context, answered the question you brought and told you exactly where the next one lives.

Common Pitfalls #

The most damaging statutory error is reading a single subsection in isolation and missing the definitions, exceptions, or cross-referenced provisions that change what it actually requires. Read the section in context every time. The second pitfall is citing the wrong version, since statutes are amended and the language in force when your client’s events occurred may differ from today’s text; the historical notes are there to keep you honest. A third is treating an ambiguity as a dead end rather than as your next question, when in fact an undefined term or vague standard is exactly where the research goes next, into case law, legislative history, or agency interpretation. And do not skip the citator check, because a statute can be amended or held unconstitutional and still sit unchanged in an older database copy.

Beyond the Basics #

Relationship Between Statutes and Regulations #

Statutes often delegate implementation authority to agencies. The annotated code’s “Implementing Regulations” notes are your pathway to the Code of Federal Regulations, covered in Chapter 7. The full picture of what the law requires usually emerges only when you read the statute alongside its implementing regulations: the statute sets the framework, the regulation supplies the operative detail. Chapter 8 covers researching across sources in more depth.

Ambiguous Statutes and Legislative History #

When statutory text is genuinely ambiguous, courts may look to legislative history (Chapter 10). Identifying and articulating the ambiguity is itself an important skill, because before you can argue that legislative history resolves an ambiguity, you first have to show the text is ambiguous. A court committed to plain meaning will never reach the legislative history at all.

Federal Preemption of State Statutes #

When a federal statute preempts state law, the state statute is invalid to that extent. Always consider preemption when you research a state statutory issue in a federally regulated area. The bounds and application of preemption are the subject of a doctrinal course, as it is too complex for the scope of this textbook.

Canons of Statutory Construction #

When statutory text is ambiguous, courts do not just guess. They reach for the canons of construction, a toolbox of reading rules courts have relied on for centuries, and knowing the handful that do most of the work helps you predict which reading a court will pick. The plain meaning rule says unambiguous text gets applied as written. Expressio unius says that when a statute lists some things, it probably meant to leave out the rest. Ejusdem generis says a catch-all phrase at the end of a list only catches things like the ones listed. And the rule against surplusage says every word is presumed to be doing a job. (Your legislation or statutory interpretation course will hand you the full toolbox and the fights over when each tool applies. For research purposes, recognizing them is enough.)

Treat the canons the way this chapter has taught you to treat every interpretive tool: as generators of next questions. If two canons point in different directions, which one your jurisdiction’s courts favor is itself a researchable question. Appendix C’s How to Read a Statute carries the full working list with examples; that appendix is where the skill gets its reps. The canons also have a secondary source dedicated to them: Sutherland Statutes and Statutory Construction, the treatise courts themselves cite. Even a browse through its table of contents will give you a sense of the range of canons courts use, and it is the place to turn when a canon starts to matter in your case.

Chapter Recap #

Statutes are binding expressions of legislative will. The annotated code is the researcher’s primary statutory tool, connecting legislative text to judicial interpretation, scholarly commentary, and procedural rules. Always read statutes in context, consult the annotations for interpretive case law, and verify currency. And every ambiguity you find is simply your next question.

Key Takeaways

  • Statutes are organized chronologically (session laws) and by subject (codes); annotated codes are the best research tool.
  • Read statutory text in context: neighboring sections, definitions, and exceptions all matter.
  • Annotated code features connect the statutory text to its full legal context.
  • Ambiguous statutory language generates the next Question Method cycle: check case law, legislative history, and agency interpretation.
  • Always update statutes using citator tools.

Practice Online #

Reviewing Through Practice: Can the Podcaster Record the Call? #

The following exercises test your ability to map a statute’s anatomy and to use annotations as research pathways, followed by the statutes drill from the single-source set.

Exercise 6.1 — Anatomy of a Section #

Take a statute your instructor assigns, or choose any substantive section of your state’s code that regulates conduct. Map its anatomy: the operative provision (who must or must not do what), every defined term it uses and where the definition lives, every exception and where it lives, the penalty or consequence provision, and the currency information telling you the text is current. Some of these will live in neighboring sections rather than the one you started in; finding them is the exercise.

Deliverable: a labeled anatomy map of the section, with a citation for each component you located outside it.

Self-check: if your map shows no defined terms and no exceptions, one of two things is true: you chose an unusually self-contained section, or you did not look in the neighboring sections. It is almost always the second. Statutes are schemes, and reading one section alone is how their traps get sprung.

Exercise 6.2 — Annotations Are Pathways #

In an annotated code, take any substantive statute (your instructor may assign one, or reuse a statute you have already read this term) and use its Notes of Decisions or case annotations to identify two cases interpreting the section’s most important term. Do not read the cases yet. Log each in your Research Log as a pathway: the question it appears to answer, the citation, and where the annotation pointed you. This is the discipline of the Chapter 5 protocol’s fourth move, applied from the statute side: annotations hand you leads, and a lead recorded is not an answer synthesized.

Deliverable: two Research Log entries, each honestly marked as a lead: the Findings field opens with the words lead only and attributes the claim to the annotation.

Self-check: if either entry’s Findings field states what the case held, you exceeded the exercise. All you know is what the annotation claims, and an annotation is an editor’s summary, not the case.

Exercise 6.3 — The Chapter 6 Drill (Statutes Only): Can the Podcaster Record the Call? #

This is the statutes drill from the single-source set introduced at the end of Chapter 4. Same rules: one source type only, every question drafted, every answer logged.

The scenario. A client runs a small podcast and wants to record phone interviews with sources. Some sources are in California. The client asks: can I record a phone call with a California source without telling them I am recording? The supervising attorney tells you the governing law is California’s recording-consent statute and asks you to answer from the statute.

Run the chain yourself. When you have it, check your work against the walkthrough in Appendix E.
