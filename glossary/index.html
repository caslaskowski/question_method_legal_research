Glossary

Every term the book defines, in one place. Flip through the terms as flashcards, or filter the full list beneath them. In the chapters, a dotted underline marks the spot where each term is first defined.

Every defined term

administrative adjudication
An agency’s own dispute-resolution process: an administrative law judge hears the case, with appeal to the agency head and then to federal court; published decisions may serve as precedent within the agency and are often the most detailed interpretations of a regulation available. (Chapter 11)
ALR annotation
An article in American Law Reports collecting and analyzing the case law of many jurisdictions on a narrow legal issue; a primary tool for survey questions. (Chapter 4)
ambiguity spotting (Skill 1)
Reading a legal text and identifying the terms, phrases, or provisions whose meaning requires interpretive research — undefined terms, vague standards, terms of art, structural ambiguities, scope questions. (Chapter 16)
analytical closure
The judgment that research is complete because every question has been answered or identified as genuinely open, the rule and its application are covered, and further research would only refine wording. (Chapter 3)
annotated code
A statutory code enhanced with editorial notes: case annotations, cross-references to regulations and secondary sources, and history notes. (Chapter 5)
annotated outline
The running document that records the product of research: each question’s synthesized answer, organized under the structure the final document will take. (Chapter 3)
application question
A question asking how a rule operates on specific facts, answered by locating cases with similar facts (fact-finding) or by building the rule from multiple sources when nothing is on point (constructive application). (Chapter 3)
authority note
The note accompanying a CFR part that cites the statute empowering the agency to issue it. (Chapter 7)
binding authority
Law a court must follow: the constitution, statutes, and regulations of its own jurisdiction, the decisions of higher courts in its own system, and federal law applicable within it. (Chapter 1)
candor to the tribunal (Rule 3.3)
The duty not to knowingly misstate the law to a court, and to disclose controlling authority directly adverse to the client’s position when opposing counsel has not. (Chapter 12)
canons of construction
Established interpretive principles courts use to read ambiguous statutory text, such as the plain meaning rule, expressio unius, ejusdem generis, and the rule against surplusage. (Chapter 6)
citator
A tool (KeyCite or Shepard’s) that tracks an authority’s subsequent history and treatment, showing whether it remains good law. (Chapter 5)
closed universe
The provided-materials exam format: a fixed set of sources over which you run a compressed Question Method cycle — nothing outside the set may be relied on. (Chapter 16)
closing-out protocol
The systematic end-of-project sequence: run a final sweep, update every case and statute, check the currency of secondary sources, evaluate the outline for gaps, and document the closure decision. (Chapter 9)
code
Statutes arranged by subject as currently amended, in an official or annotated compilation. (Chapter 1)
committee report
The report accompanying a bill out of committee; generally the most authoritative legislative history document. (Chapter 10)
common law
Judge-made law developed case by case through judicial decision. (Chapter 1)
competence (Rule 1.1)
The duty to bring the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness, and preparation the matter requires — making research quality itself an ethical obligation; Comment 8 extends it to understanding the benefits and risks of technology. (Chapter 12)
constructive application
Answering an application question no decision squarely addresses by building the rule from the cases that exist and reasoning by analogy. (Chapter 3)
definitional question
A question asking what a word or phrase means, answered by a dictionary for a working definition or by the statute’s definitions section, a regulation, or case law when precision is critical. (Chapter 3)
dictum
A statement in an opinion not necessary to the decision; persuasive at most, never binding. (Chapter 5)
dispositive fact identification (Skill 3)
Reading a judicial opinion to identify the facts that, if changed, would change the outcome, then comparing those facts to a client’s facts. (Chapter 16)
dispositive facts
The facts a decision actually turned on; identifying them is the key skill for predicting how a case applies to new facts. (Chapter 5)
docket
The court’s running record of a litigation: every filing, order, and event, in order. (Chapter 13)
exploratory question
A question asking what the legal landscape looks like, answered by an encyclopedia or general search (no-clue), a treatise (foundational), or ALRs, Restatements, and 50-state surveys (survey). (Chapter 3)
Federal Register
The daily federal publication of proposed and final rules, notices, and presidential documents. (Chapter 7)
focusing question
A question asking what the specific governing rule, standard, or test is; the move from secondary to primary authority. (Chapter 3)
FOIA request
A request under the Freedom of Information Act for agency records not otherwise published — a research route to materials the agency has not made public. (Chapter 11)
frequency operator
A search operator requiring a term to appear at least n times in a document (ATLEAST3(preemption)), retrieving documents that discuss the term substantively rather than in passing. (Chapter 9)
guidance document
Agency material, such as manuals, opinion letters, and policy statements, that explains the agency’s interpretation without the force of law. (Chapter 7)
hallucination
A generative AI output that is plausible but fabricated, including invented citations, quotations, and holdings. (Chapter 15)
headnote
An editor-written summary of a single point of law in an opinion; a research tool, not authority. (Chapter 5)
holding
The court’s resolution of the issue necessary to its decision; the portion of an opinion carrying precedential weight. (Chapter 5)
hornbook
A one-volume treatise summarizing an area of law, written primarily for students. (Chapter 4)
intra-jurisdiction source ranking (Skill 2)
Given a collection of authorities from a single jurisdiction, ranking them correctly by binding effect — a supreme court over a court of appeals; a statute over common law on the same subject. (Chapter 16)
legislative history
The documents produced as a bill moves through the legislature, used to uncover legislative intent when statutory text is ambiguous. (Chapter 10)
List of CFR Sections Affected (LSA)
The monthly record of which CFR sections have changed since the last annual update. (Chapter 7)
litigation research closure
The litigation closure criterion: controlling authority identified for each issue, adverse authority identified and addressed, procedural requirements confirmed, and all cited authorities updated and validated. (Chapter 14)
Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM)
The Federal Register notice proposing a rule and opening the public comment period; the record of the agency’s intent. (Chapter 7)
opinion letter
An agency’s written answer to a specific question, often from a specific regulated party — narrow by design, and revealing of how the agency applies its own rules to concrete facts. (Chapter 11)
persuasive authority
Authority a court may consider but need not follow: primary authority from other jurisdictions, lower or coordinate courts, secondary sources, and dicta. (Chapter 1)
practical closure
Stopping research because an external limit, a deadline, a budget, or an instruction, forces the stop, with what remains documented. (Chapter 3)
preemption
Federal law displacing state law in areas where Congress has constitutional power, either expressly or impliedly. In scholarship, preemption research instead means checking whether your argument has already been made. (Chapter 1)
primary authority
The law itself: constitutions, statutes, regulations, case law, and treaties. (Chapter 1)
proximity connector
A search connector specifying how close together terms must appear — /n within n words in either order (landlord /5 heat), /s same sentence, /p same paragraph — dramatically cutting false positives as questions narrow. (Chapter 9)
Question Method
The research method this book teaches: write a single research question, research it, record the answer scoped to that question, and let the answer produce the next question, repeating until closure. (Chapter 3)
regulatory research closure
The compliance closure criterion: all applicable regulations identified at every level, agency guidance consulted for interpretive questions, recent enforcement actions reviewed, and compliance status assessed against each requirement. (Chapter 14)
research closure
The professional judgment that research is good enough for its purpose; it comes in analytical and practical forms. (Chapter 3)
research gap identification (Skill 4)
Recognizing when a source set does not fully answer the question and articulating what would fill the gap — distinguishing ‘the law is genuinely unclear’ from ‘I have not yet found the answer.’ (Chapter 16)
Research Log
The running record of the research process: each question, its type, the source consulted, the scoped answer, the citation, and the next question. (Chapter 3)
research sufficiency and closure (Skill 5)
The professional judgment that research is complete: every question answered or identified as open, all authorities validated, the framework covered. (Chapter 16)
Restatement
The American Law Institute’s synthesis of common-law rules in an area; among the most persuasive secondary authorities. (Chapter 4)
root expander
The search operator (!) that retrieves every word sharing a root, such as negligen! for negligent, negligence, and negligently. (Chapter 2)
secondary source
Material about the law, such as encyclopedias, treatises, ALR annotations, and law review articles, used to find and understand primary authority but rarely cited as law. (Chapter 4)
session laws
Laws as enacted, published in chronological order, such as the United States Statutes at Large. (Chapter 1)
single-source trap
A long run of Research Log entries in one source type with the question still only partly answered; the log-visible signal to switch sources. (Chapter 8)
slip law
The first official publication of a newly enacted statute, issued as a standalone document. (Chapter 1)
source note
The note citing the Federal Register document that created or last amended a CFR part; the thread you pull to reach the preamble. (Chapter 7)
stare decisis
The doctrine that courts stand by precedent: their own prior decisions and those of the courts above them. (Chapter 1)
supervisory duties (Rules 5.1 & 5.3)
The duties requiring lawyers with supervisory authority to make reasonable efforts to ensure that subordinate lawyers’ and nonlawyers’ work — including delegated research — conforms to professional obligations. (Chapter 12)
terms and connectors
The precision search mode built from Boolean operators, proximity connectors, expanders, and phrase searching. (Chapter 17)
transactional research closure
The transactional closure criterion: all applicable regulatory requirements identified, tax implications analyzed, comparable transactions reviewed for market terms, risk factors addressed in the deal documentation, and due diligence complete. (Chapter 14)
treatise
A comprehensive scholarly treatment of a single area of law, often multivolume, organized to give the field’s structure. (Chapter 3)
